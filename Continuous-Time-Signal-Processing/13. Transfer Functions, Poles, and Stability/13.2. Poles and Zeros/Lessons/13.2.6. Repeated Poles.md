## Introduction to Repeated Poles

In transfer-function form, the denominator polynomial $D(s)$ determines pole locations, because poles are the values of $s$ that make the denominator zero in $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$. In this section, the target move is to recognize repeated roots directly from factored factors in $D(s)$.

To do this, express one denominator factor as $(s-r)^m$. The same $r$ repeated $m$ times means there is one pole at $s=r$ with multiplicity $m$. Multiplicity is the count of how many times the same root appears in the factored denominator.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)},\quad D(s)=(s-r)^m D_\text{simple}(s)
$$

Use the anchor case $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+1)^2}$. Set $D(s)=0$, so $(s+1)^2=0$. This gives $s=-1$ and because the same factor is squared, the pole at $s=-1$ has multiplicity 2. The action is to read both the pole location and its multiplicity together.

$$
r:\;\text{is a pole with multiplicity }m
$$

Contrast this with two distinct poles, such as $D(s)=(s+1)(s+3)$. That form gives one pole at $s=-1$ and one at $s=-3$, each with multiplicity 1. A repeated pole is different because the same location is counted more than once, which changes how it is represented in pole-zero interpretation.

## Identifying a Repeated Real Pole from a Factored Denominator

**Example:** Given $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+2)^2(s+5)}$, identify all poles and each pole multiplicity.

**Explanation**

Read the denominator of $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$ as a factored collection of linear factors. A repeated real pole appears when the same factor repeats: $(s-r)^m$ means there is a pole at $s=-r$ with multiplicity $m$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+2)^2(s+5)} \Rightarrow D(s)=(s+2)^2(s+5)
$$

List each denominator factor and its exponent. From the factorization above, one factor is $(s+2)^2$ and one factor is $(s+5)^1$. This gives two distinct roots: one with exponent $2$ and one with exponent $1$.

$$
(s+2)^2 \rightarrow s=-2,\; m_{-2}=2, \quad (s+5) \rightarrow s=-5,\; m_{-5}=1
$$

Convert each factor to a pole location: $(s+2)=0$ gives $s=-2$, and $(s+5)=0$ gives $s=-5$. The multiplicity is the exponent on each repeated factor.

$$
\text{Poles: } s=-2 \text{ (multiplicity 2), } s=-5 \text{ (multiplicity 1)}
$$

Result: the repeated pole is at $s=-2$ with multiplicity $2$, and $s=-5$ is a simple pole with multiplicity $1$. Keep this distinction in the representation when moving to pole-zero interpretation.

## Finding Pole Multiplicity by Factoring the Denominator

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{1}{s^3+3s^2+3s+1}$, factor the denominator and list the poles with multiplicity.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is an expanded denominator. For pole extraction we still use the same rule from repeated-pole notation: rewrite the denominator as factors and then read multiplicities from repeated linear terms.

$$
D(s)=s^3+3s^2+3s+1=(s+1)^3
$$

This gives one repeated linear factor, so the pole locations come from solving $s+1=0$ after expansion of the factored form.

$$
s+1=0 \;\Rightarrow\; s=-1
$$

Count how many times that same factor appears. The factor is $(s+1)$ to the third power, so this pole is repeated three times.

$$
\text{Multiplicity: } (s+1)^3 \Rightarrow s=-1 \text{ has } m=3
$$

Result: the denominator factors as one repeated pole at $s=-1$ with multiplicity $3$, so we can report $s=-1\; (\times 3)$ for later pole-zero representation.

## Marking Pole Multiplicity on a Pole-Zero Plot

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{3}{(s+1)^2(s+4)}$, show the pole-zero plot and annotate each pole with multiplicity.

**Explanation**

This step extends the previous algebraic counting to notation on the $s$-plane: the cue is now to display the poles from denominator factors with explicit multiplicity labels.

$$
H(s)=\frac{3}{(s+1)^2(s+4)} \Rightarrow D(s)=(s+1)^2(s+4)
$$

List poles from each linear factor: $(s+1)^2$ gives $s=-1$ with multiplicity $2$, and $(s+4)^1$ gives $s=-4$ with multiplicity $1$.

$$
s=-1\; (m=2),\quad s=-4\; (m=1)
$$

Place both poles on the real axis of the pole-zero plot: both points are left of the origin. Use the repeated-pole convention at $s=-1$ (same location marked twice or with $m=2$), and a single marker for $s=-4$.

## Representing Repeated Complex-Conjugate Poles

**Example:** Find and plot the repeated complex-conjugate poles for $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+1-2j)^2(s+1+2j)^2}$ with multiplicity annotations.

**Explanation**

The cue here is a denominator already written as conjugate linear factors with exponents. Use the same rule: a repeated linear factor of the form $(s-r)^m$ contributes one pole location with multiplicity $m$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+1-2j)^2(s+1+2j)^2} \Rightarrow D(s)=(s+1-2j)^2(s+1+2j)^2
$$

Read each conjugate linear factor separately. The first factor is centered at $s=-1+2j$ and the second at $s=-1-2j$, and each factor is squared.

$$
\text{From }(s+1-2j)^2:\; s=-1+2j,\; m=2, \quad (s+1+2j)^2:\; s=-1-2j,\; m=2
$$

So the repeated complex-conjugate poles are $s=-1\pm 2j$, each with multiplicity $2$, and the representation must keep the pair symmetric in the $s$-plane with matching multiplicity labels.
