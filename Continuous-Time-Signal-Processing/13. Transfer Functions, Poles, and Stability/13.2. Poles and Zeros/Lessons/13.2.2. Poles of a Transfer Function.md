## Introduction to Poles of a Transfer Function

A transfer function is written as $H(s)=\dfrac{N(s)}{D(s)}$, where $N(s)$ is the numerator polynomial and $D(s)$ is the denominator polynomial. Poles are defined from the denominator roots, while zeros come from numerator roots, so the two are distinguished by which polynomial is set to zero.

$$
H(s)=\dfrac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

This distinction is not cosmetic. If $D(s)=0$, the expression $H(s)$ has a denominator of zero, so the transfer-function value is not finite; that is why those roots are the poles and they mark singular points in symbolic form.

$$
D(s)=0
$$

Use the base case $H(s)=\dfrac{1}{s+4}$. The denominator is $D(s)=s+4$. Set $s+4=0$, which gives the pole at $s=-4$. That single root is the pole value.

Next sections keep the same pole rule and add one variation at a time: first another linear denominator, then factored and expanded denominators, and then how pole locations are used in the $s$-plane interpretation.

## Finding a Pole from a Linear Denominator Factor

**Example:** Find the poles of $H(s)=\dfrac{3s+1}{s+5}$.

**Explanation**

Identify the transfer function form first: in $H(s)=\dfrac{3s+1}{s+5}$, the denominator is the piece that determines poles. So we focus on the denominator polynomial only.

$$
H(s)=\frac{3s+1}{s+5}
$$

Step 1 is to read the denominator polynomial directly from the transfer function: the denominator is $D(s)=s+5$.

$$
D(s)=s+5
$$

Step 2 applies the pole rule: set $D(s)=0$ and solve.

$$
s+5=0 \Rightarrow s=-5
$$

Step 3 states the final pole result as a set: $\{ -5 \}$.

## Finding Poles from Factored Denominator Polynomials

**Example:** Find all poles of $H(s)=\\frac{4}{(s+2)(s-3)}$.

**Explanation**

The target cue is a factored denominator. For a transfer function, poles are exactly the values of $s$ that make the denominator zero.

$$
H(s)=\\frac{4}{(s+2)(s-3)}
$$

From this form, read the denominator as a product: $D(s)=(s+2)(s-3)$.

Use the pole rule for each factor: set the denominator equal to zero and use the zero-product rule to split into two equations.

$$
D(s)=0 \\Rightarrow (s+2)(s-3)=0 \\Rightarrow s+2=0 \\text{ or } s-3=0
$$

Solve each linear equation. From $s+2=0$, we get $s=-2$. From $s-3=0$, we get $s=3$.

State the poles as a complete set: $\\{-2, 3\\}$.

## Finding Poles by Factoring an Expanded Denominator

**Example:** Find the poles of $H(s)=\\frac{2}{s^2+s-6}$.

**Explanation**

This cue is an expanded denominator, so the first action is to identify the denominator polynomial: $D(s)=s^2+s-6$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{2}{s^2+s-6}
$$

For a pole computation, set the denominator to zero and factor the quadratic before solving.

$$
D(s)=s^2+s-6=0
$$

Factor the denominator as $s^2+s-6=(s+3)(s-2)$. The zero-product rule then gives two equations.

$$
(s+3)(s-2)=0 \Rightarrow s+3=0 \text{ or } s-2=0
$$

Solve each linear equation: $s=-3$ from $s+3=0$ and $s=2$ from $s-2=0$.

Report the full pole set with both solutions: $\{-3,2\}$. This gives two poles, matching the quadratic degree.

## Reading Pole Values as Locations in the $s$-Plane

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{2}{s^2+2s+5}$, find poles and state their $s$-plane coordinates.

**Explanation**

The cue is a rational transfer function, so first identify the denominator and read the pole equation directly: $D(s)=s^2+2s+5$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{2}{s^2+2s+5}
$$

Apply the pole rule and set the denominator to zero: $s^2+2s+5=0$.

$$
D(s)=s^2+2s+5=0
$$

Complete the square as $s^2+2s+5=(s+1)^2+4$, so $(s+1)^2=-4$ and $s+1=\pm 2j$.

$$
s+1=\pm 2j \Rightarrow s=-1\pm 2j
$$

Report the pole values as a pair: $\{-1+2j, -1-2j\}$.

Convert each pole with $s=\sigma+j\omega \leftrightarrow (\sigma,\omega)$: first write the real part then the imaginary part. The poles map to $-1+2j\mapsto(-1,2)$ and $-1-2j\mapsto(-1,-2)$.

$$
-1+2j \mapsto (-1,2),\quad -1-2j \mapsto (-1,-2)
$$

![](<../Source/13.2.2. Poles of a Transfer Function/Images/l002-s005-te-section-005-s-plane.png>)

Both poles share the real coordinate $-1$, with imaginary coordinates that are opposite in sign, confirming the conjugate-pair coordinates expected from this denominator.
