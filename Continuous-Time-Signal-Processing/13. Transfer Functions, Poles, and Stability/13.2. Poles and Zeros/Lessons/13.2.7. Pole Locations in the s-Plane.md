## Introduction to Pole Locations in the $s$-Plane

Start by identifying where poles sit on the transfer function $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$. A pole is a root of the denominator, so each pole value is a candidate for an exponential mode in the natural response.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

Use the base case first: for a pole at $p=-1$, the response contribution is $e^{-1\cdot t}=e^{-t}$, so the mode is a decaying nonoscillatory term.

Now label any pole as $p=\sigma+j\omega$ and read this coordinate split directly.

$$
p=\sigma+j\omega
$$

$$
e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{\sigma t}\big(\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)\big)
$$

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s001-te-section-001.png>)

Apply the rule: if $\sigma<0$, the mode decays; if $\sigma=0$, it sustains; if $\sigma>0$, it grows. This is the stability language you will reuse at every pole-interpretation step.

The imaginary coordinate $\omega$ sets oscillation frequency in radians per second, and if $\omega=0$, there is no oscillation. If $\omega\neq0$, include a sinusoidal component.

Checklist for each pole: read $\sigma$ and $\omega$, classify $\sigma$ for decay/growth, use $|\omega|$ for oscillation rate, then map the pole location to the mode form.

## Reading Pole Coordinates in the $s$-Plane

**Example:** Read the pole at $-2+j1$ from the $s$-plane and identify the corresponding term in the natural response.

**Explanation**

Cue: the plotted point is at $p=-2+j1$, so first read the coordinate form $p=\sigma+j\omega$ directly from the graph.

Extract the coordinates: $\sigma=-2$ and $\omega=1$. These are the real and imaginary parts of the pole location.

Write the associated natural-response factor using that pole.

$$
e^{(-2+j1)t}
$$

Separate real and imaginary effects with Euler form.

$$
e^{(-2+j1)t}=e^{-2t}\left(\cos t+j\sin t\right)
$$

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s002-te-section-002.png>)

Interpretation: $\sigma=-2$ gives a decaying envelope, and $\omega=1$ gives sustained oscillation at $1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so this mode is a decaying sinusoidal contribution.

## Interpreting the Real Coordinate of a Pole

**Example:** Interpret the pole at $-3$ on the $s$-plane and choose the correct response-type statement.

**Explanation**

Cue: read the pole location from the plotted point, which is on the real axis at $p=-3$.

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s003-te-section-003.png>)

The real part is $\sigma=-3$ and the imaginary part is $\omega=0$.

Use the mode rule from the prior section: a pole $p=\sigma+j\omega$ maps to $e^{pt}=e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}$.

$$
e^{(-3+0\,j)t}=e^{-3t}
$$

Since $\sigma=-3<0$, this is a decaying envelope. With $\omega=0$, there is no oscillation, so the response is a decaying real exponential term.

## Interpreting the Imaginary Coordinate of a Pole

**Example:** Read the pole at $j2$ and identify the oscillation frequency and mode form.

**Explanation**

Cue: read the plotted pole location, which is $p=j2$, meaning the pole lies on the imaginary axis.

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s004-te-section-004.png>)

Extract the coordinates from $p=\sigma+j\omega$: here $\sigma=0$ and $\omega=2$.

Use the same pole-mode rule from prior sections and write the associated factor.

$$
e^{(0+j2)t}=e^{j2t}
$$

Rewrite the complex exponential into sinusoidal components.

$$
e^{j2t}=\cos(2t)+j\sin(2t)
$$

Interpretation: $\sigma=0$ gives a constant envelope (no decay or growth), and $\omega=2$ gives oscillation at $2\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. If needed, $-j2$ would oscillate at the same rate with opposite direction of rotation.

## Interpreting Poles with Both Decay and Oscillation

**Example:** Interpret the pole $-0.5 + j3$ and summarize the natural response behavior.

**Explanation**

Cue: read the pole location from the diagram. Here $p=-0.5+j3$, so both real and imaginary parts are nonzero.

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s005-te-section-005.png>)

Write the coordinates in $p=\sigma+j\omega$ form and extract $\sigma=-0.5$ and $\omega=3$.

Use the same rule from earlier sections: a pole $p$ contributes a factor $e^{pt}$ in the natural response.

$$
e^{(-0.5+j3)t}
$$

Separate the real and imaginary parts to read envelope and oscillation.

$$
e^{-0.5t}\left(\cos 3t + j\sin 3t\right)
$$

Conclusion: $\sigma=-0.5<0$ gives a decaying envelope, and $\omega=3$ gives sustained oscillation at $3\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, so this is a damped oscillatory mode.

## Comparing Pole Locations for System Behavior

**Example:** For poles at $-4$, $-1+j4$, and $-1-j2$, identify which pole governs the long-term behavior and why.

**Explanation**

Cue: read the three poles from the diagram and list each as $p=\sigma+j\omega$. The points are $-4$, $-1+j4$, and $-1-j2$.

![](<../Source/13.2.7. Pole Locations and Response Behavior/Images/l007-s006-te-section-006.png>)

Convert each pole into coordinates: $-4\to(\sigma,\omega)=(-4,0)$, $-1+j4\to(-1,4)$, and $-1-j2\to(-1,-2)$.

Compare real parts to find the slowest-decay envelope. The real-part values are $-4$, $-1$, and $-1$, so the two poles with $\sigma=-1$ dominate long-term because they are closest to the imaginary axis.

$$
e^{-4t},\;e^{(-1+j4)t}=e^{-t}(\cos 4t+j\sin 4t),\;e^{(-1-j2)t}=e^{-t}(\cos 2t-j\sin 2t)
$$

Interpretation: $-4$ is transient and decays fastest, while $-1+j4$ and $-1-j2$ are the dominant terms with shared decay rate $e^{-t}$; among them, $\omega=4$ gives faster oscillation than $\omega=2$.

Final ranking: long-term magnitude is controlled by the $\sigma=-1$ pair, and oscillation speed ranking is $-1+j4$ (faster oscillation) over $-1-j2$ (slower oscillation).
