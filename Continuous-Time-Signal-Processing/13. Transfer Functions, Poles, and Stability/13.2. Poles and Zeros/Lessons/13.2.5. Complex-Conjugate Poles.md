## Introduction to Complex-Conjugate Poles

For transfer functions with real coefficients, non-real poles must appear in conjugate pairs. The recognition rule is: if one pole is $p=\sigma+j\omega$, the partner must be $\bar p=\sigma-j\omega$.

Read this as two mirror points in the $s$-plane with the same real part and opposite imaginary part. Use this cue before you solve: poles in conjugate pairs are always written together as $\sigma \pm j\omega$.

Write the pole pair as $s=\sigma \pm j\omega$ and map it to the response component $e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{\sigma t}(\cos \omega t + j\sin \omega t)$. Then interpret $\sigma$ as the envelope growth/decay rate and $\omega$ as oscillation frequency.

$$
s=\sigma \pm j\omega
$$

$$
e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{\sigma t}(\cos \omega t + j\sin \omega t)
$$

Use the baseline case $s=\pm j\omega$ first, where $\sigma=0$ so the mode is sustained oscillation with no envelope growth or decay. Then add $\sigma\neq0$ to create decay for $\sigma<0$ and growth for $\sigma>0$, while keeping the same $\omega$ interpretation.

Next in this topic, identify pole pairs from transfer functions, then classify each pair by the sign of $\sigma$ and the frequency from $|\omega|$, and finally practice reading the same move from pole-zero plots.

## Identifying Complex-Conjugate Pole Pairs from a Transfer Function

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{5(s+1)}{(s+3)(s^2+4s+8)}$, identify the complex-conjugate poles and express them as $-\sigma \pm j\omega$.

**Explanation**

The first cue is the denominator: all poles solve $D(s)=0$. The linear factor gives one real pole, and the irreducible quadratic gives the complex-conjugate pair.

$$
D(s)=(s+3)(s^2+4s+8),\quad H(s)=\frac{5(s+1)}{D(s)}.
$$

Solve each denominator factor as needed: from $s+3=0$, the real pole is $s=-3$. For the quadratic part, rewrite it in completed-square form.

$$
s^2+4s+8=(s+2)^2+2^2.
$$

Now set the quadratic term to zero and read off the pair: $(s+2)^2+2^2=0$ means $s+2=\pm j2$, so the non-real poles are $s=-2\pm2j$.

$$
s=-2\pm 2j.
$$

Expressed in the standard form $-\sigma \pm j\omega$, this pair is $-(2)\pm j(2)$, i.e., $\sigma=2$ and $\omega=2$. Keep this pattern: identify the quadratic, complete the square, then record the mirrored pair.

## Reading Decay Rate from a Negative Real Part

**Example:** Given poles $-3\pm j4$, report the decay rate and the qualitative response classification.

**Explanation**

Use the standard complex-conjugate form $s=-\sigma\pm j\omega$ as the cue: the real part gives a decay/growth rate axis, and a nonzero imaginary part gives oscillation.

$$
-3\pm j4=-\sigma\pm j\omega
$$

Match the given poles to $-\sigma\pm j\omega$. Read $\sigma=3$ from the real part and $\omega=4$ from the imaginary part.

$$
\sigma=3,\quad \omega=4
$$

Because the real part is negative, the pair is decaying. The envelope is $e^{-\sigma t}=e^{-3t}$, so the mode decays at rate $3$.

Because $\omega\neq 0$, this decay is modulated by oscillation, so this is a stable decaying oscillatory response. If the sign flipped to $+3\pm j4$, stability would reverse to growing oscillation at rate $3$.

## Reading Growth Rate from a Positive Real Part

**Example:** Given poles $+2\pm j5$, identify the growth rate and state whether the mode is stable, unstable, or marginal.

**Explanation**

Use the same extraction cue as before: read a complex pair as $s=\sigma\pm j\omega$ and treat the real part as the exponential-rate axis.

$$
2\pm j5=\sigma\pm j\omega
$$

Match coefficients and read $\sigma=2$ from the real part and $\omega=5$ from the imaginary part.

$$
\sigma=2,\quad \omega=5
$$

Because $\sigma>0$, this pair is exponentially growing, so the envelope is $e^{\sigma t}=e^{2t}$.

Also keep $\omega\neq 0$, so the growth is oscillatory. The contrast with the decay step is only the sign of the real part: positive means unstable growth; negative means stable decay.

## Reading Oscillation Frequency from the Imaginary Part

**Example:** Given poles $-1\pm j7$, determine the oscillation frequency and write it in rad/s.

**Explanation**

Use the same pair form as before and identify the imaginary component that controls oscillation.

$$
-1\pm j7=-\sigma\pm j\omega
$$

Match terms to get $\sigma=1$ and $\omega=7$, then apply the frequency rule using magnitude.

$$
f_{\text{osc}}=|\omega|=|7|=7\,\mathrm{rad/s}
$$

The pair has nonzero imaginary part, so this mode is oscillatory at $7\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.

A common check: do not confuse frequency with $2\omega$, $\omega^2$, or the real part magnitude; those are different quantities.

## Interpreting Complex-Conjugate Pole Pairs on a Pole-Zero Plot

**Example:** On the provided pole-zero plot, the highlighted pair is at $(-2\pm j4)$. State the corresponding response form and behavior.

**Explanation**

Use the plot-to-coordinates cue first: pick the highlighted complex pair and read each point from the axis grid before doing any time-domain interpretation.

![](<../Source/13.2.5. Complex-Conjugate Poles/Images/l005-s006-te-section-006.png>)

From symmetry in the plot, the pair is at $s=-2\pm j4$. Write that as a standard pole form so each quantity is explicit.

$$
s=-\sigma\pm j\omega\quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma=2,\;\omega=4
$$

The real part is negative, so the envelope decays at rate $2$; the response includes an exponential decay factor $e^{-2t}$. The imaginary part is nonzero, so the mode is oscillatory.

$$
h(t)\sim e^{-2t}(\cos 4t + j\sin 4t)
$$

So the correct behavior is a decaying oscillatory mode with oscillation frequency $4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.
