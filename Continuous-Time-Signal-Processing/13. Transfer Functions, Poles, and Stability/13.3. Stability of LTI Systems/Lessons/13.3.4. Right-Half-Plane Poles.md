## Introduction to Right-Half-Plane Poles

A right-half-plane pole is identified by its location in the $s$-plane. The right half-plane is the region where the real part of the pole is positive, so the cue to check is the sign of $\Re\{s\}$.

![](<../Source/13.3.4. Right-Half-Plane Poles/Images/l004-s001-te-section-001.png>)

$$
\text{RHP condition: } \Re\{s\} > 0
$$

For a denominator-root example, use $H(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}$. The denominator is zero at $s=1$, so the single pole has real part $1$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{s-1},\; \text{pole at } s=1
$$

Because $\Re\{s\}=1>0$, this pole is in the right half-plane. In a causal LTI system, that pole contributes a growing exponential mode, so the impulse response is not absolutely integrable and the system is BIBO unstable.

$$
\Re\{s\}=1>0\Rightarrow y(t)\text{ grows as }e^{t}\text{ for causal systems}
$$

The classification rule is direct: for a causal LTI system, one or more poles with $\Re\{s\}>0$ means there is at least one growing mode, and the system is BIBO unstable.

## Identifying a Positive Real Pole

**Example:** Classify stability of $H(s)=\frac{1}{s-4}$.

**Explanation**

A first-order transfer function has poles where the denominator is zero. For this extension of the previous template, set $s-4=0$ to find the pole location.

$$
s-4=0 \Rightarrow s=4
$$

The real part is $\Re\{s\}=4$, so the pole has positive real part.

$$
\Re\{s\}=4>0
$$

A positive real part means the pole is in the right-half-plane, which maps to a growing exponential mode in a causal LTI system.

Therefore, $H(s)=\frac{1}{s-4}$ is unstable in the BIBO sense for a causal LTI interpretation because at least one pole has $\Re\{s\}>0$.

## Classifying a Transfer Function with One Right-Half-Plane Pole

**Example:** Classify the stability of $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s-5)(s+2)}$.

**Explanation**

This example shows the same pole test on a two-pole transfer function. The poles come from solving the denominator equation. Start from $ (s-5)(s+2)=0 $.

$$
(s-5)(s+2)=0 \Rightarrow s=5 \text{ or } s=-2
$$

The pole set is $\{s=5,\; s=-2\}$. At least one pole has a positive real part: $\Re\{s\}=5>0$.

$$
\Re\{s\}=5>0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{RHP pole present}
$$

Because one pole is in the right half-plane, this causal LTI transfer function is BIBO unstable even though the second pole at $s=-2$ is stable.

Final classification: unstable.

## Detecting Right-Half-Plane Poles Among Multiple Poles

**Example:** Classify $H(s)=\frac{10(s+3)}{(s-1)(s+2)(s+5)}$.

**Explanation**

To scan many poles efficiently, begin by setting each denominator factor to zero so every pole is listed at once.

$$
(s-1)(s+2)(s+5)=0
$$

From these factors, the poles are $s=1$, $s=-2$, and $s=-5$.

$$
s=1,\; s=-2,\; s=-5
$$

Check real parts: $\Re\{s\}=1>0$, $\Re\{s\}=-2<0$, and $\Re\{s\}=-5<0$. Only $s=1$ is in the right half-plane.

A single right-half-plane pole is enough to classify the causal LTI transfer function as BIBO unstable; stable left-half-plane poles do not override that result.

Final result: the system is unstable.

## Recognizing Right-Half-Plane Complex Poles

**Example:** Classify $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s-(2+j))(s-(2-j))}$.

**Explanation**

Use the same pole-finding cue as before: set each denominator factor to zero, now applied to a complex-conjugate pair.

$$
s-(2+j)=0, \; s-(2-j)=0
$$

Those equations give poles at $s=2+j$ and $s=2-j$.

$$
\Re\{2+j\}=2, \; \Re\{2-j\}=2
$$

Since each pole has positive real part, the rule is to classify the causal LTI transfer function as BIBO unstable.

The unstable behavior is growing oscillation, not just oscillation alone, because the shared real part is positive.

Final result: unstable.
