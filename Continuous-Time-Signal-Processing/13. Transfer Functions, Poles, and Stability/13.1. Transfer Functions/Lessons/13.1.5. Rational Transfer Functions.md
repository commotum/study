## Introduction to Rational Transfer Functions

A transfer function is rational when it can be written as a quotient of two polynomials in $s$. In this lesson, the key form is $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$, where $N(s)$ is the numerator polynomial and $D(s)$ is the denominator polynomial.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

Polynomials in $s$ can include constants, powers of $s$, and sums of such terms. So an expression like $\frac{s+2}{s^2+3s+2}$ is already rational, because both the top and bottom are polynomials in $s$.

$$
N(s)=b_ms^m+\cdots+b_1s+b_0,\quad D(s)=a_ns^n+\cdots+a_1s+a_0
$$

If $H(s)$ does not yet look like one polynomial divided by another, rewrite it until that structure is visible. Once the rational form is clear, the numerator and denominator polynomials are ready for later zero, pole, and stability analysis.

## Identifying Polynomial Quotients in $H(s)$

**Example:** Is $H(s)=\frac{s+2}{s^2+3s+2}$ a rational transfer function?

**Explanation**

The cue is the quotient form itself: a rational transfer function is any $H(s)$ that can be written as one polynomial divided by another polynomial in $s$. Here, the right representation is $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{s+2}{s^2+3s+2}
$$

The numerator $s+2$ is a polynomial in $s$, and the denominator $s^2+3s+2$ is also a polynomial in $s$. Because both parts are polynomials, the expression already fits the rational form without any additional rewriting.

$$
N(s)=s+2,\quad D(s)=s^2+3s+2
$$

So $H(s)$ is a rational transfer function. The only decision here is recognizing the polynomial-over-polynomial structure; later steps can use $N(s)$ and $D(s)$ for further analysis.

<a id="rewriting-hs-as-a-quotient-of-polynomials"></a>
## Rewriting $H(s)$ as a Quotient of Polynomials

**Example:** Rewrite $H(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}+\frac{2}{s+3}$ as one quotient of polynomials in $s$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the transfer function is a sum of two rational terms, so the right move is to combine them into a single quotient of polynomials in $s$. The common denominator is $(s+1)(s+3)$.

$$
\frac{1}{s+1}+\frac{2}{s+3}=\frac{(s+3)+2(s+1)}{(s+1)(s+3)}
$$

After rewriting each term over the common denominator, combine the numerators: $(s+3)+2(s+1)=3s+5$. That gives a single rational expression with a polynomial numerator and a polynomial denominator.

$$
H(s)=\frac{3s+5}{(s+1)(s+3)}=\frac{3s+5}{s^2+4s+3}
$$

So the rewritten transfer function is still rational, and now the polynomial-over-polynomial form is explicit. That is the form later lessons will use for pole, zero, and stability analysis.

<a id="recognizing-rational-form-after-algebraic-simplification"></a>
## Recognizing Rational Form After Algebraic Simplification

**Example:** Show that $H(s)=\frac{s/(s+2)}{s+5}$ is rational by simplifying it into polynomial-over-polynomial form.

**Explanation**

The cue is the nested quotient: the transfer function is not yet written as one polynomial divided by another. The right representation is to flatten the outer division so the rational form becomes visible.

$$
\frac{s/(s+2)}{s+5}=\frac{s}{(s+2)(s+5)}
$$

Now the numerator is $s$, which is a polynomial in $s$, and the denominator is $(s+2)(s+5)$. Expanding that product gives another polynomial in $s$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{s}{(s+2)(s+5)}=\frac{s}{s^2+7s+10}
$$

So the simplified expression is rational, because both the numerator and denominator are polynomials in $s$. The simplification step did not change the form; it made the polynomial-over-polynomial structure explicit.

<a id="recognizing-rational-form-for-later-pole-zero-analysis"></a>
## Recognizing Rational Form for Later Pole-Zero Analysis

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{s+4}{(s+1)(s+3)}$, identify the numerator and denominator expressions used for later pole-zero analysis.

**Explanation**

The cue is the rational form itself: $H(s)$ is already written as a quotient of polynomials in $s$. For later analysis, the key move is to label the top and bottom expressions as $N(s)$ and $D(s)$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)},\quad N(s)=s+4,\quad D(s)=(s+1)(s+3)
$$

The numerator is $N(s)=s+4$, and the denominator is $D(s)=(s+1)(s+3)$. Both are polynomials in $s$, so the expression is rational whether the denominator is left factored or expanded.

$$
D(s)=(s+1)(s+3)=s^2+4s+3
$$

So the rational form is explicit, and the next step in the analysis sequence is to use $N(s)=0$ for zeros and $D(s)=0$ for poles. This example stops at naming the symbolic parts and does not solve for the roots yet.
