## Introduction to RLC Circuit Transfer Function

The target object in this section is one transfer function ratio, not a long derivation. Every later step in this topic should reuse the same goal: from a differential equation, produce $H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}$ in one algebraic pass so stability analysis can be done directly from the denominator.

Use the canonical base model from this topic and hold it fixed while students learn the method: $L C\,\frac{d^2 y(t)}{dt^2}+R C\,\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=x(t)$. The only required action is to Laplace-transform each term and then collect powers of $Y(s)$ and $X(s)$.

Under the zero-initial-condition convention used in this module, use these substitutions: $\mathcal{L}\{y'(t)\}=sY(s)$, $\mathcal{L}\{y''(t)\}=s^2Y(s)$, $\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}=Y(s)$, and $\mathcal{L}\{x(t)\}=X(s)$. No extra initial-condition terms are carried in this setup.

$$
L C\,s^2Y(s)+R C\,sY(s)+Y(s)=X(s)
$$

Now isolate the ratio by dividing by the input-side factor: $H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}$. This is the one move students need to memorize before moving on.

$$
H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}=\frac{1/(L C)}{s^2+(R/L)s+1/(L C)}
$$

The skill you should carry forward from this section is the denominator-first check: rewrite $H(s)$ in rational form, read the denominator $D(s)$, and use it for pole location work in the next steps.

## Laplace-Transforming an RLC Differential Equation

**Example:** Apply the Laplace transform to $L C\,\frac{d^2 y(t)}{dt^2}+R C\,\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=x(t)$ under zero initial conditions and express the equation in terms of $Y(s)$ and $X(s)$.

**Explanation**

The cue for this example is the differential equation itself. The action is the same conversion pattern as the previous step: map each time-domain term to its Laplace-domain form, set initial conditions to zero, and collect a single algebraic relation in $Y(s)$ and $X(s)$.

Use $\mathcal{L}\{\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2}\}=s^2Y(s)-s y(0^-)-\dot y(0^-)$ for the second-derivative term and $\mathcal{L}\{\frac{dy}{dt}\}=sY(s)-y(0^-)$ for the first-derivative term.

Use the zero-initial-condition rule from this module: set $y(0^-)=0$ and $\dot y(0^-)=0$, so the derivative transforms reduce to multiplication by $s$. Also use $\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}=Y(s)$ and $\mathcal{L}\{x(t)\}=X(s)$.

$$
L C\left(s^2Y(s)-s y(0^-)-\dot y(0^- )\right)+R C\left(sY(s)-y(0^-)\right)+Y(s)=X(s)
$$

$$
L C\,s^2Y(s)+R C\,sY(s)+Y(s)=X(s)
$$

Collect the transformed terms on one line and verify the result is purely algebraic: a single relation between $Y(s)$ and $X(s)$ with no lingering time-domain terms. The final transformed equation is the completed target for this step.

$$
L C\,s^2Y(s)+R C\,sY(s)+Y(s)=X(s)
$$

## Isolating the Output-to-Input Ratio

**Example:** From the transformed relation $L C\,s^2Y(s)+R C\,sY(s)+Y(s)=X(s)$, isolate $\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}$ in one fraction.

**Explanation**

The cue for this example is the transformed differential equation itself. The action is to isolate the system ratio form by turning one transformed equation into the explicit transfer expression $H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}$.

First, factor all left-side $Y(s)$ terms into one polynomial coefficient times $Y(s)$. This keeps the final ratio step purely mechanical.

$$
(L C\,s^2 + R C\,s + 1)Y(s)=X(s)
$$

Next, divide both sides by $X(s)$ to place $Y(s)$ over $X(s)$ and keep all input dependence in the denominator factor only.

$$
\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}
$$

That gives the core transfer ratio. Optionally normalize by dividing numerator and denominator by $L C$ to expose the standard second-order template when desired:

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{L C\left(s^2+\frac{R}{L}s+\frac{1}{L C}\right)}
$$

<a id="writing-an-rlc-transfer-function-as-a-rational-function-of-s"></a>
## Writing an RLC Transfer Function as a Rational Function of s

**Example:** Rewrite $H(s)=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}$ as $\frac{K}{s^2+(R/L)s+1/(L C)}$ for $L=1$, $R=4$, $C=\frac12$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the raw transfer form $H(s)=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}$. The rule is to divide through by the leading $s^2$ coefficient so the denominator is in standard second-order form.

Start by dividing numerator and denominator by $L C$. This is legal because $L C\neq 0$ for passive components, and it exposes the normalized template explicitly.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{L C\,s^2+R C\,s+1}=\frac{\frac{1}{L C}}{s^2+\frac{R}{L}s+\frac{1}{L C}}
$$

Now substitute $L=1$, $R=4$, $C=\frac12$. Then $L C=\frac12$, so the normalized gain becomes $\frac{1}{L C}=2$ and the denominator coefficients become $\frac{R}{L}=4$ and $\frac{1}{L C}=2$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{2}{s^2+4s+2}
$$

Keep both checkpoints. The unnormalized denominator is $\frac12 s^2+2s+1$, and the standardized denominator is $s^2+4s+2$. Both are equivalent, but the latter is the form you reuse for pole and stability work.

## Identifying the Denominator Polynomial for Pole Analysis

**Example:** From $H(s)=\frac{1}{s^2+3s+2}$, state the characteristic polynomial and list the two poles.

**Explanation**

The cue is the denominator in $H(s)$, since poles are defined by the values of $s$ where the transfer function denominator is zero. So we read $H(s)$ in denominator-first form and identify

$$
D(s)=s^2+3s+2
$$

Next, factor this quadratic to expose poles directly as roots of $D(s)=0$.

$$
s^2+3s+2=(s+1)(s+2),\quad D(s)=0\iff s=-1\text{ or }s=-2
$$

Both roots are negative real parts, so this pole candidate set signals a stable pattern in later LTI stability checkpoints.

Checkpoint statement to reuse: characteristic polynomial $D(s)=s^2+3s+2$, poles $\{-1,-2\}$, stable-sign pattern $(\Re\{s\}<0)$.
