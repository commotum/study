<a id="introduction-to-high-pass-behavior"></a>
## Introduction to High-Pass Behavior

High-pass behavior means an LTI filter attenuates low-frequency components and preserves high-frequency components. In a magnitude response, that shows up as a small value of $|H(j\omega)|$ near $\omega=0$ and a larger value at higher $\omega$.

The test is a comparison between the low-frequency end and the high-frequency end of $|H(j\omega)|$. If the curve is lower near $\omega=0$ than it is farther to the right, the response is moving in the high-pass direction.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| \ll |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})|
$$

$$
|H(j0)| \approx 0, \qquad |H(j\omega)| \text{ grows with } \omega
$$

The clean prototype is a filter whose gain is near $0$ at low frequency and near $1$ at high frequency. That is the simplest model of high-pass behavior before the curve gets more complicated.

So the decision rule is simple: if the low-frequency gain is smaller than the high-frequency gain, classify the magnitude response as high-pass. Later sections will apply the same comparison to specific curves.

## Recognizing Low-Frequency Attenuation

**Example:** A filter has $|H(j\omega)| \approx 0.15$ for small $\omega$. What does that say about low-frequency components?

**Explanation**

The cue is the small value of $|H(j\omega)|$ near $\omega=0$. A gain of about $0.15$ is well below $1$, so the low-frequency end of the response is attenuating the signal.

$$
|H(j0)| \approx 0.15 < 1
$$

That means a low-frequency component comes out with a much smaller amplitude than it went in. In other words, the system suppresses low-frequency content rather than passing it through unchanged.

This is the low-frequency side of a high-pass response: the low end is small, so the filter is already showing attenuation where $\omega$ is near zero.

## Recognizing High-Frequency Passage

**Example:** A filter has $|H(j\omega)| \approx 0.95$ for large $\omega$. What does that say about high-frequency components?

**Explanation**

The cue is the large-$\omega$ end of the magnitude response. A value of $|H(j\omega)| \approx 0.95$ is close to $1$, so the high-frequency side is not being cut down very much.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| \approx 0.95 \approx 1
$$

That means a high-frequency component passes through with almost the same amplitude it had at the input. The response is showing high-frequency passage rather than strong suppression.

Do not call this amplification just because the component is passed. Passage means the amplitude is essentially preserved; amplification would require a gain above $1$. This is the high-frequency side of a high-pass response.

<a id="comparing-low-and-high-frequency-gain"></a>
## Comparing Low- and High-Frequency Gain

**Example:** A filter has $|H(j\omega)| \approx 0.2$ near $\omega=0$ and $|H(j\omega)| \approx 1.1$ for large $\omega$. Compare the gains and state what the comparison means.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pair of gains: $0.2$ near $\omega=0$ and $1.1$ for large $\omega$. Read them as the low-frequency gain and the high-frequency gain, then compare them directly.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| \approx 0.2,\qquad |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| \approx 1.1
$$

The high-frequency gain is larger because $1.1 > 0.2$. The low-frequency side is well below $1$, so low frequencies are attenuated, while the high-frequency side is near $1$ and passes more strongly.

That means the magnitude response rises from low to high frequency, so the filter behaves like a high-pass filter.

<a id="classifying-a-magnitude-response-as-high-pass"></a>
## Classifying a Magnitude Response as High-Pass

**Example:** A filter has $|H(j\omega)|$ small near $\omega=0$ and near $1$ for large $\omega$. Classify the filter from its magnitude response.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pair of ends of the magnitude response: the low-frequency side is small and the high-frequency side is near $1$. Compare those two ends directly.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| \ll 1,\qquad |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| \approx 1
$$

Because the low-frequency gain is much smaller than the high-frequency gain, low frequencies are attenuated while high frequencies pass. The response is not flat, so it is not all-pass.

That overall pattern is high-pass: the magnitude response suppresses low frequencies and preserves high frequencies, so the correct classification is high-pass rather than low-pass or band-pass.
