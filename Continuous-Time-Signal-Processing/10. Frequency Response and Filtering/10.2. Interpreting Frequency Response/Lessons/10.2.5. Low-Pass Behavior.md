## Introduction to Low-Pass Behavior

A system has low-pass behavior when its magnitude response is larger at low frequencies than at high frequencies. The cue is the comparison of $|H(j\omega)|$ across frequency, not a phase pattern.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| > |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})|
$$

In the common base case, the low-frequency gain is close to $1$ while the high-frequency gain is much smaller. That means the same sinusoid is passed with little attenuation when it is slow and with much more attenuation when it is fast.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| \approx 1,\quad |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| \ll 1
$$

For a sinusoidal input at frequency $\omega$, the output amplitude is the input amplitude multiplied by $|H(j\omega)|$. So the system keeps slow sinusoidal components prominent and suppresses rapid ones.

$$
A_{\text{out}} = A_{\text{in}}\,|H(j\omega)|
$$

That is the low-pass rule you will reuse: look at the magnitude response, compare low and high frequency gain, and classify the system by whether it preserves slow components more strongly than fast ones.

## Recognizing Strong Low-Frequency Passage from |$H(j\omega)$|

**Example:** A system has $|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| = 0.95$ at a small frequency and $|H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| = 0.08$ at a much larger frequency. What does that tell you about how the system treats slow and rapid sinusoidal components?

**Explanation**

The cue is the comparison of the two magnitudes, not the phase. Read the low-frequency value and the high-frequency value as gains for slow and rapid sinusoidal components.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| = 0.95,\quad |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| = 0.08
$$

Since $0.95$ is close to $1$ and $0.08$ is much smaller, the low-frequency component is passed with little attenuation while the high-frequency component is strongly reduced.

$$
A_{\text{out}} = A_{\text{in}}\,|H(j\omega)|
$$

So the slow sinusoidal part stays prominent in the output, while the rapid sinusoidal part is suppressed. That is exactly the low-pass pattern.

## Recognizing High-Frequency Attenuation from |$H(j\omega)$|

**Example:** At $\omega = 40$, the magnitude response is $|H(j40)| = 0.05$. What happens to a sinusoidal input at that frequency?

**Explanation**

The cue is the small magnitude at a high frequency. Read $|H(j40)| = 0.05$ as the gain applied to a rapid sinusoidal component.

$$
|H(j40)| = 0.05
$$

That means the output keeps the same frequency $40$ but its amplitude is only $0.05$ of the input amplitude. The frequency stays the same; the size shrinks.

$$
A_{\text{out}} = 0.05\,A_{\text{in}}
$$

So the fast component is strongly attenuated. That is the high-frequency side of low-pass behavior.

<a id="comparing-low-and-high-frequency-magnitudes"></a>
## Comparing Low- and High-Frequency Magnitudes

**Example:** A system has $|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| = 1.1$ and $|H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| = 0.2$. Which frequency is passed more strongly, and what filter behavior does that imply?

**Explanation**

The cue is the comparison itself: read $|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})|$ and $|H(j\omega_{\text{high}})|$ side by side, then decide which one is larger.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| = 1.1,\quad |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})| = 0.2
$$

Since $1.1 > 0.2$, the low-frequency component is passed more strongly. The larger low-frequency magnitude means the slow sinusoidal part survives much better than the fast one.

$$
|H(j\omega_{\text{low}})| > |H(j\omega_{\text{high}})|
$$

That contrast identifies low-pass behavior: the system preserves slow components and suppresses rapid components.

<a id="interpreting-low-pass-behavior-for-sinusoidal-components"></a>
## Interpreting Low-Pass Behavior for Sinusoidal Components

**Example:** An input is $x(t) = \cos(\omega_{\text{slow}} t) + \cos(\omega_{\text{fast}} t)$ with $\omega_{\text{slow}}$ small and $\omega_{\text{fast}}$ large. If $|H(j\omega_{\text{slow}})| \approx 1$ and $|H(j\omega_{\text{fast}})| \approx 0.1$, what does the output look like component by component?

**Explanation**

The cue is to separate the input into its slow and fast sinusoidal parts, then attach the corresponding gain to each one.

$$
x(t) = \cos(\omega_{\text{slow}} t) + \cos(\omega_{\text{fast}} t),\quad |H(j\omega_{\text{slow}})| \approx 1,\quad |H(j\omega_{\text{fast}})| \approx 0.1
$$

Apply the low-pass rule to each component: the slow cosine keeps nearly full size, while the fast cosine is multiplied by a much smaller factor.

$$
y(t) \approx \cos(\omega_{\text{slow}} t) + 0.1\,\cos(\omega_{\text{fast}} t)
$$

So the output is dominated by the slow variation, and the rapid variation is still present but strongly suppressed. That is the low-pass pattern in sinusoidal form.
