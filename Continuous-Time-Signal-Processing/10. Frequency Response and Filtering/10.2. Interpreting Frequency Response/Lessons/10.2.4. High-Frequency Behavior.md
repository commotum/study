## Introduction to High-Frequency Behavior

When $\omega$ is large, the question is not the full shape of $H(j\omega)$ but what $|H(j\omega)|$ and $\angle H(j\omega)$ approach. Those two limits are the high-frequency signature of the system, because they tell you whether a rapid sinusoidal component is passed, attenuated, or shifted.

$$
\lim_{\omega\to\infty}|H(j\omega)|,\qquad \lim_{\omega\to\infty}\angle H(j\omega)
$$

For a sinusoidal component $A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)$, the output keeps the same frequency; only the amplitude and phase change. So once the high-frequency gain and phase are known, you can read the fate of the rapid component directly from $|H(j\omega)|$ and $\angle H(j\omega)$.

$$
y(t)=A|H(j\omega_0)|\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi+\angle H(j\omega_0))
$$

A simple first-order response such as $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ shows the base case: the dominant power of $j\omega$ controls the limit, the magnitude shrinks toward $0$, and the phase settles to a constant lag. That is the pattern you will reuse in the later high-frequency examples.

<a id="evaluating-high-frequency-magnitude-limits"></a>
## Evaluating High-Frequency Magnitude Limits

**Example:** Evaluate $\lim_{\omega\to\infty}|H(j\omega)|$ for $H(j\omega)=\frac{3j\omega+2}{2j\omega-5}$ and decide whether very high-frequency components are attenuated, passed, or amplified.

**Explanation**

This is a high-frequency magnitude question, so the cue is the limit as $\omega\to\infty$ of $|H(j\omega)|$. For a rational transfer function, the lower-order terms disappear at high frequency, so the leading powers of $j\omega$ determine the gain.

$$
H(j\omega)=\frac{3j\omega+2}{2j\omega-5}\sim \frac{3j\omega}{2j\omega}
$$

Now take the magnitude of the dominant coefficient ratio. Because the numerator and denominator have the same highest power of $j\omega$, the asymptotic magnitude is just the ratio of the leading coefficients.

$$
\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\left|\frac{3j\omega+2}{2j\omega-5}\right|=\left|\frac{3}{2}\right|=\frac{3}{2}
$$

Since $\frac{3}{2}>1$, the system amplifies very high-frequency sinusoidal components. This example stays on magnitude only; the phase limit is the next step.

## Evaluating High-Frequency Phase Limits

**Example:** Evaluate $\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\angle H(j\omega)$ for $H(j\omega)=\frac{1-j\omega}{1+j\omega}$ and interpret the asymptotic phase shift on a rapid sinusoid.

**Explanation**

This is the phase version of the high-frequency test, so the cue is $\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\angle H(j\omega)$, not the magnitude. For a rational transfer function, the highest-power terms control the limiting angle because lower-order terms fade away.

$$
H(j\omega)=\frac{1-j\omega}{1+j\omega}\sim \frac{-j\omega}{j\omega}
$$

Read the asymptotic angle of each leading factor: the numerator approaches $-\pi/2$ and the denominator approaches $\pi/2$. Subtracting the denominator phase from the numerator phase gives the constant high-frequency phase shift.

$$
\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\angle\frac{1-j\omega}{1+j\omega}=-\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}=-\pi
$$

So the high-frequency phase limit is $-\pi$, which is a sign inversion of the rapid sinusoid. This example stays on phase only; the magnitude story was the previous step.

<a id="using-dominant-powers-of-jomega-in-high-frequency-limits"></a>
## Using Dominant Powers of $j\omega$ in High-Frequency Limits

**Example:** Use dominant powers of $j\omega$ to evaluate the high-frequency magnitude limit of $H(j\omega)=\frac{3(j\omega)^2-2j\omega+1}{(j\omega)^3+4(j\omega)^2-5}$.

**Explanation**

This is a high-frequency magnitude question with extra lower-order clutter, so the cue is the degree mismatch in the rational transfer function. When $\omega$ is large, keep the highest powers of $j\omega$ and ignore the terms that vanish after factoring.

$$
H(j\omega)=\frac{(j\omega)^2\left(3-\frac{2}{j\omega}+\frac{1}{(j\omega)^2}\right)}{(j\omega)^3\left(1+\frac{4}{j\omega}-\frac{5}{(j\omega)^3}\right)}
$$

Cancel the common $(j\omega)^2$ factor. One power of $j\omega$ remains in the denominator, so the magnitude must decay toward zero no matter what the lower-order terms do.

$$
\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\left|H(j\omega)\right|=\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{j\omega}\right|=0
$$

So the high-frequency magnitude limit is $0$, which means the system attenuates rapidly varying sinusoidal components. The dominant-power shortcut is the point: the degree gap decides the limit before any full expansion is needed.

## Predicting Rapid-Sinusoid Output from High-Frequency Gain and Phase

**Example:** For very large $\omega_0$, predict the output of $x(t)=3\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/6)$ when the high-frequency limit of $H(j\omega)$ is $-2$.

**Explanation**

This is the output-prediction version of the high-frequency test, so the cue is a rapid sinusoid together with the asymptotic gain and phase of $H(j\omega)$. At very large $\omega_0$, the output keeps the same frequency and only the amplitude and phase change.

$$
A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)\mapsto A|H(j\omega_0)|\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi+\angle H(j\omega_0))
$$

Here $H(j\omega)\to -2$, so the high-frequency gain is $2$ and the phase shift is $\pi$. That means the rapid sinusoid is amplified by a factor of $2$ and shifted by a half cycle.

$$
x(t)=3\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/6)\quad\Rightarrow\quad y(t)\approx 3\cdot 2\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/6+\pi)=6\cos(\omega_0 t+5\pi/6)
$$

So the output is $6\cos(\omega_0 t+5\pi/6)$, which is the same-frequency sinusoid with doubled amplitude and a $\pi$ phase shift. This completes the transfer from asymptotic gain and phase to a concrete rapid-sinusoid output.
