## Introduction to Comparing Direct Convolution and Frequency-Domain Multiplication

For a continuous-time LTI system, there are two valid output routes: direct convolution in time and multiplication in frequency. Use this step to compare the work each route requires before you choose a method.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

The decision is about workload, not about which formula is more correct. In the time-domain route, you find the overlap interval, set the bounds, and evaluate the integral piecewise if the overlap changes with $t$.

In the frequency-domain route, you look up or derive transform pairs, multiply the spectra, and then check whether the inverse transform is immediate or messy. Start with a case where the overlap is one simple interval and the frequency-domain route is also available, so the choice comes from counting steps instead of from hidden tricks.

Choose the shorter symbolic path. Let a difficult overlap, a hard transform lookup, or a messy inverse transform tip the decision toward the other method.

<a id="comparing-workloads-when-the-time-domain-overlap-is-simple"></a>
## Comparing Workloads When the Time-Domain Overlap Is Simple

**Example:** For $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$, decide whether direct convolution or frequency-domain multiplication is the shorter path for finding $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue here is compact support: both signals are short rectangular pulses, so the decision is about which symbolic route has less work, not about which formula is more valid.

$$
x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1),\qquad h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)
$$

The time-domain route is short because the overlap is just one simple interval at a time. You set the convolution bounds once, integrate a simple product, and avoid extra transform bookkeeping.

The frequency-domain route is longer here because you still need both transform pairs, the product $X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$, and an inverse transform to get back to $y(t)$. That is more symbolic work than the single overlap integral.

So the shorter path is direct convolution. The important point is not that convolution always wins, but that a simple overlap can make the time-domain integral cheaper than lookup, multiplication, and inversion.

## Comparing Workloads When Transform Pairs Are Available

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, decide whether direct convolution or frequency-domain multiplication is the shorter path for finding $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue here is a pair of standard causal exponentials. Because the transform pairs are already available, the decision is about which route needs fewer symbolic steps overall.

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}u(t),\qquad h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)
$$

The time-domain route would still require the full convolution setup: find the overlap interval, write the integral, and simplify the result. That is valid, but it is not the shortest route when the supports are already easy to describe and the transform tables are ready to use.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega},\qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

The frequency-domain route starts from those known pairs, multiplies them to get $Y(j\omega)$, and then uses a routine inverse transform. Here the lookup plus product is shorter than the overlap integral, so frequency-domain multiplication is the better choice.

So the correct decision is to use frequency-domain multiplication. The key point is that the method choice comes from counting the work: when the transform pairs are already known, the frequency-domain path can be cheaper than direct convolution.

## Comparing Workloads When the Inverse Transform Is Hard

**Example:** For $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)$ and $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, decide whether direct convolution or frequency-domain multiplication is the shorter path for finding $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite pulse feeding a causal exponential. Both routes are available, so the choice comes down to symbolic workload, not to whether the transforms exist.

$$
x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1),\qquad h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)
$$

The direct-convolution route stays inside one finite overlap interval, so it is just one manageable integral to set up and simplify.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega},\qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

The frequency-domain route also starts from known pairs, but the product is $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega(1+j\omega)}$. That product does not collapse to a single simple inverse-transform pair, so the inverse step needs extra algebra before $y(t)$ appears.

So the shorter path here is direct convolution. The important comparison is not just whether transform pairs are available, but whether the inverse transform is simple enough to justify switching domains.

## Choosing the Easier Output Method from Both Workflows

**Example:** For $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$ and $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, compare direct convolution and frequency-domain multiplication and choose the easier route for finding $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite pulse, $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$, feeding a causal exponential, $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$. Both routes are available, so the decision is about the total symbolic workload.

$$
x(t)=u(t)-u(t-2),\qquad h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)
$$

The direct-convolution route stays inside one finite overlap interval. Once the overlap is identified, the result comes from a single piecewise integral and its simplification, so the time-domain path stays compact.

$$
y(t)=\int_{0}^{2} e^{-(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

The frequency-domain route starts from known pairs, but it adds two lookups, a product, and then an inverse transform that does not fall out of one table entry.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j2\omega}}{j\omega},\qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega},\qquad Y(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j2\omega}}{j\omega(1+j\omega)}
$$

That product can be simplified, but only after extra algebra such as partial fractions or a shift decomposition. So even though the frequency-domain route is available, it is not the shorter one here.

The easier method is direct convolution. The decision rule is to count the whole workflow, not just the first familiar step: here the finite overlap integral is cheaper than transform lookup, multiplication, and inversion.
