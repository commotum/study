## Introduction to Complex Exponentials as LTI System Inputs

When you analyze an LTI system one frequency at a time, start with the complex exponential input $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j\omega t}
$$

Here, $\omega$ is the angular frequency being probed. It is the frequency parameter in the exponent, not the time variable.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

Euler's formula shows that this probe is a compact single-frequency representation, not a mixture of frequencies.

Later steps will reuse this same input to read how the LTI system changes the signal's scaling and phase at the same $\omega$.

## Identifying the Angular Frequency in $e^{j\omega t}$

**Example:** Given $x(t)=e^{j7t}$, identify the angular frequency and separate it from the imaginary unit and the time variable.

**Explanation**

Compare the signal to the standard probe form $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j7t}
$$

The coefficient multiplying $t$ inside the exponent is the angular frequency, so here $\omega=7$.

$$
\omega=7
$$

The $j$ is the imaginary unit and $t$ is the time variable; neither one is the frequency parameter.

## Interpreting $e^{j\omega t}$ as a Single-Frequency Input

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$, decide whether the input is single-frequency or multi-frequency and say what makes it a probe signal.

**Explanation**

The cue is the standard probe form $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$. This signal is already written with one angular frequency parameter, $\omega$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j\omega t}
$$

Because there is only one exponential frequency present, this is a single-frequency complex exponential, not a mixture of frequencies.

That is why it works as a probe input for an LTI system: it isolates one angular frequency at a time.

## Using a Complex Exponential to Probe an LTI System

**Example:** If you want to probe an LTI system at angular frequency $\omega_0$, what input should you apply and why is that choice useful?

**Explanation**

The cue is the target frequency $\omega_0$. Use the matching complex exponential input $x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t}$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t}
$$

This choice is useful because it contains one angular frequency only, so it tests the system one frequency at a time.

The result is a clean probe input: the response at $\omega_0$ can be examined directly next, without mixing in any other frequencies.

## Connecting Probe Frequency to Output Scaling and Phase

**Example:** Given a probe input $e^{j\omega_0 t}$, identify which frequency-dependent system response determines the output's scaling and phase.

**Explanation**

The cue is the probe frequency $\omega_0$. The quantity you want is the system response evaluated at that same frequency, written as the frequency response $H(j\omega_0)$.

$$
H(j\omega_0)=|H(j\omega_0)|e^{j\angle H(j\omega_0)}
$$

The magnitude $|H(j\omega_0)|$ sets the output scaling, and the angle $\angle H(j\omega_0)$ sets the phase shift.

So the probe frequency tells you where to look, and $H(j\omega_0)$ tells you how the system changes that tone without changing the frequency.
