## Introduction to High-Pass Differential Equation

In the high-pass RC derivation, treat the circuit as an input-output system with input $x(t)$ and resistor-voltage output $y(t)$. The capacitor voltage is the remaining drop in the series branch, so the setup begins by naming the voltage split and the shared current.

$$
x(t)=v_C(t)+y(t)
$$

That voltage-sum relation is the first cue to recognize. Once $y(t)$ is known, the capacitor voltage can be rewritten as $v_C(t)=x(t)-y(t)$, which is the form that will later let the capacitor law use the input and output directly.

$$
i_C(t)=i_R(t)
$$

Because the capacitor and resistor are in series, they carry the same branch current. That shared-current relation is the second cue in the derivation, and it is what lets the capacitor law and resistor law meet in one equation.

$$
i_C(t)=C\,\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}
$$

$$
i_R(t)=\frac{y(t)}{R}
$$

So the setup is complete: one voltage-sum equation, one current-equality equation, and one law for each element. The next step will substitute $v_C(t)=x(t)-y(t)$ into the capacitor law and combine it with the resistor law to reach $RC\,\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=RC\,\frac{dx(t)}{dt}$.

$$
RC\,\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=RC\,\frac{dx(t)}{dt}
$$

## Writing the Input Voltage as Capacitor Voltage Plus Output Voltage

**Example:** For the high-pass RC circuit, start from $x(t)=v_C(t)+y(t)$ and solve for the capacitor voltage $v_C(t)$.

**Explanation**

Use the voltage-sum relation from the circuit: $x(t)=v_C(t)+y(t)$. The cue here is that the capacitor voltage is the quantity to isolate before the capacitor current law can be rewritten in terms of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$.

$$
x(t)=v_C(t)+y(t)
$$

Subtract $y(t)$ from both sides so the capacitor voltage stands alone on the left. This keeps the sign convention aligned with the series branch: the capacitor voltage is the input minus the output.

$$
v_C(t)=x(t)-y(t)
$$

That isolated form is the substitution-ready result for the next step. Keep it in this exact shape so the capacitor law can be rewritten directly in terms of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$.

## Relating the Series Capacitor Current and Resistor Current

**Example:** For the same high-pass RC circuit, write the capacitor current and resistor current, then set them equal because the elements are in series.

**Explanation**

The cue is the series branch: the capacitor and resistor lie on the same current path, so they must carry the same current. Start by writing that shared-current relation explicitly.

$$
i_C(t)=i_R(t)
$$

Now write each current using the element law that matches it. The capacitor current comes from the derivative of $v_C(t)$, and the resistor current comes from the voltage across the resistor, $y(t)$.

$$
i_C(t)=C\,\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}
$$

$$
i_R(t)=\frac{y(t)}{R}
$$

With both laws written in the same notation, the shared current is ready to be reused in the next step. Keep $i_C(t)=i_R(t)$ explicit and leave $v_C(t)$ unsolved here.

<a id="substituting-output-voltage-into-the-capacitor-current-law"></a>
## Substituting Output Voltage into the Capacitor Current Law

**Example:** Substitute $v_C(t)=x(t)-y(t)$ into $i_C(t)=C\,\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}$ and simplify the capacitor current in terms of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

Start from the capacitor law for the shared branch current. The cue is the isolated capacitor voltage from the previous step, so replace $v_C(t)$ before doing any further algebra.

$$
i_C(t)=C\,\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}
$$

Substitute $v_C(t)=x(t)-y(t)$ directly into the derivative. Keep the subtraction intact so the sign structure stays visible through the next step.

$$
i_C(t)=C\,\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(x(t)-y(t)\bigr)
$$

Differentiate term by term. The derivative of a difference is the difference of the derivatives, so the capacitor current stays separated into the input and output rates.

$$
i_C(t)=C\,\bigl(x'(t)-y'(t)\bigr)
$$

This is the form you need next: the capacitor current is now written entirely in $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, with the substitution chain kept explicit.

<a id="rearranging-into-the-high-pass-input-output-differential-equation"></a>
## Rearranging into the High-Pass Input-Output Differential Equation

**Example:** Combine $i_C(t)=C\,\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(x(t)-y(t)\bigr)$ with $i_R(t)=\frac{y(t)}{R}$ and rearrange the result into the standard high-pass input-output differential equation.

**Explanation**

Start from the current-balance equation from the previous step. Both branch currents are already written in $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, so the remaining job is to clear the resistor denominator and isolate the output derivative.

$$
C\,\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(x(t)-y(t)\bigr)=\frac{y(t)}{R}
$$

Expand the derivative of the difference term by term. This keeps the input rate and the output rate visible before the algebraic rearrangement.

$$
C\,\bigl(x'(t)-y'(t)\bigr)=\frac{y(t)}{R}
$$

Multiply through by $R$ to remove the denominator. That gives one equation with only the current scale factor and the two time-derivative terms.

$$
RC\,\bigl(x'(t)-y'(t)\bigr)=y(t)
$$

Now move the output-derivative term to the left and keep the input-derivative term on the right. The result is the standard high-pass input-output differential equation.

$$
RC\,\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=RC\,\frac{dx(t)}{dt}
$$

This is the reusable ODE form for the high-pass RC filter, and it is the form the later frequency-response lesson will start from.
