## Introduction to Euler’s Formula

Euler's formula is the bridge between complex exponential notation and rectangular form. It tells you that $e^{j\theta}$ can be rewritten as $\cos\theta+j\sin\theta$.

$$
e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta
$$

Use $\theta=0$ as the anchor case. Then both sides collapse to $1$, so the identity is consistent in the simplest possible case.

$$
e^{j0}=1
$$

Once the identity is in place, read the parts directly: the real part is $\cos\theta$ and the imaginary part is $\sin\theta$.

This is the conversion rule that later examples will reuse when they move between exponential form and sinusoidal components in continuous-time signals.

Keep that bridge in mind: the same expression can be treated either as $e^{j\theta}$ or as $\cos\theta+j\sin\theta$ depending on which form is more useful.

## Converting $e^{j\theta}$ to $\cos\theta + j\sin\theta$

**Example:** Convert $e^{j\pi/3}$ to rectangular form.

**Explanation**

Use Euler's formula as the conversion rule. The cue is the complex exponential $e^{j\pi/3}$, so rewrite it as $\cos(\pi/3)+j\sin(\pi/3)$.

$$
e^{j\pi/3}=\cos(\pi/3)+j\sin(\pi/3)
$$

Now substitute the exact unit-circle values. Since $\cos(\pi/3)=\tfrac12$ and $\sin(\pi/3)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, the expression becomes a rectangular complex number.

$$
\cos(\pi/3)+j\sin(\pi/3)=\tfrac12+j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}
$$

So the result is $\tfrac12+j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The real part is $\tfrac12$ and the imaginary part is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, which is exactly the rectangular form we wanted.

## Converting $\cos\theta + j\sin\theta$ to $e^{j\theta}$

**Example:** Rewrite $\cos(\pi/6)+j\sin(\pi/6)$ as a single complex exponential.

**Explanation**

Use Euler's formula as the recognition rule. The cue is the cosine-plus-sine pair $\cos(\pi/6)+j\sin(\pi/6)$, so rewrite it as the matching exponential $e^{j\pi/6}$.

$$
\cos(\pi/6)+j\sin(\pi/6)=e^{j\pi/6}
$$

Match the angle in both trig terms exactly. Because both terms use $\pi/6$, the pair is already Euler's formula written in rectangular form.

$$
e^{j\pi/6}
$$

So the result is $e^{j\pi/6}$. The same complex number is now expressed in the shorter exponential form, which is the point of the conversion.

## Applying Euler’s Formula at Specific Angles

**Example:** Convert $e^{j\pi/2}$ to rectangular form.

**Explanation**

Use Euler's formula as the conversion rule. The cue is the special-angle exponential $e^{j\pi/2}$, so rewrite it as $\cos(\pi/2)+j\sin(\pi/2)$.

$$
e^{j\pi/2}=\cos(\pi/2)+j\sin(\pi/2)
$$

Now substitute the exact unit-circle values. Since $\cos(\pi/2)=0$ and $\sin(\pi/2)=1$, the expression collapses immediately.

$$
\cos(\pi/2)+j\sin(\pi/2)=j
$$

So the result is $j$. This special angle lands on the imaginary axis, which is why the angle choice matters.

## Connecting Euler’s Formula to Real and Imaginary Sinusoidal Parts

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$, identify the real and imaginary parts of $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

Use Euler's formula as the conversion rule. The cue is the complex exponential signal $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$, so rewrite it as $\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

Now read off the parts directly. The real part is $\cos(\omega t)$ and the imaginary part is $\sin(\omega t)$.

$$
\Re\{x(t)\}=\cos(\omega t),\qquad \Im\{x(t)\}=\sin(\omega t)
$$

So the signal can be viewed either as a complex exponential or as its sinusoidal real and imaginary components. This is the signal-processing interpretation that later examples will reuse.
