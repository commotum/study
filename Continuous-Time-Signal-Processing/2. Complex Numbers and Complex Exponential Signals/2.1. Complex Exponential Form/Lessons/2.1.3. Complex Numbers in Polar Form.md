## Introduction to Complex Numbers in Polar Form

Polar form packages a complex number's size and direction in one representation. In this course, a complex number is written as $z=a+jb$, where $j$ is the imaginary unit, and the goal is to rewrite it so the magnitude and angle are explicit.

$$
z=a+jb
$$

The two polar forms you will reuse are $r(\cos\theta + j\sin\theta)$ and the equivalent exponential form $re^{j\theta}$. They say the same thing: magnitude first, then direction.

$$
z=r(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=re^{j\theta}
$$

The magnitude comes from the rectangular coordinates: compute $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. This is the distance from the origin, so it is always nonnegative.

$$
r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
$$

For the simplest base case, start in the first quadrant, where both $a$ and $b$ are positive. Then the angle is the acute arctangent $\theta=\tan^{-1}(b/a)$ before any quadrant correction is needed.

$$
\theta=\tan^{-1}(b/a)
$$

Once you know $r$ and the correct $\theta$, you can write the number in polar form and later keep the same modulus while adjusting only the angle for the correct quadrant.

## Converting a First-Quadrant Complex Number to Polar Form

**Example:** Convert $3+4j$ to polar form.

**Explanation**

For this first-quadrant example, read $a=3$ and $b=4$ from the rectangular form $z=3+4j$. Because both parts are positive, the acute arctangent gives the correct angle directly, so there is no quadrant correction yet.

$$
r=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5
$$

Compute the modulus $r=5$ and the angle $\theta=\tan^{-1}(4/3)$. Then write the number as $5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)$, or equivalently $5e^{j\theta}$.

$$
5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=5e^{j\theta}
$$

## Converting a Second-Quadrant Complex Number to Polar Form

**Example:** Convert $-3+4j$ to polar form.

**Explanation**

For this second-quadrant example, read $a=-3$ and $b=4$ from the rectangular form $z=-3+4j$. The modulus rule stays the same; the only new step is correcting the angle because the real part is negative.

$$
r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=5
$$

Compute the modulus $r=5$ and the reference angle $\alpha=\tan^{-1}(4/3)$. Because the point is in quadrant II, correct the angle to $\theta=\pi-\alpha$ before writing the polar form.

$$
\theta=\pi-\alpha
$$

Now write the result as $5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)$, or equivalently $5e^{j\theta}$. The modulus is unchanged; only the angle is adjusted for the second quadrant.

$$
5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=5e^{j\theta}
$$

## Converting a Third-Quadrant Complex Number to Polar Form

**Example:** Convert $-3-4j$ to polar form.

**Explanation**

For this third-quadrant example, read $a=-3$ and $b=-4$ from the rectangular form $z=-3-4j$. The modulus workflow stays the same; the only new step is moving the angle into quadrant III.

$$
r=\sqrt{(-3)^2+(-4)^2}=5
$$

Compute the modulus $r=5$ and the reference angle $\alpha=\tan^{-1}(4/3)$ using the absolute values of the coordinates. Because the point is in quadrant III, correct the angle to $\theta=-\pi+\alpha$ before writing the polar form.

$$
\theta=-\pi+\alpha
$$

Now write the result as $5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)$, or equivalently $5e^{j\theta}$. The modulus is unchanged; only the angle is shifted into the third quadrant.

$$
5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=5e^{j\theta}
$$

## Converting a Fourth-Quadrant Complex Number to Polar Form

**Example:** Convert $4-3j$ to polar form.

**Explanation**

For this fourth-quadrant example, read $a=4$ and $b=-3$ from the rectangular form $z=4-3j$. The modulus workflow stays the same; the only new step is assigning a negative principal angle.

$$
r=\sqrt{4^2+(-3)^2}=5
$$

Compute the modulus $r=5$ and the reference angle $\alpha=\tan^{-1}(3/4)$ using the absolute values of the coordinates. Because the point is in quadrant IV, correct the angle to $\theta=-\alpha$ before writing the polar form.

$$
\theta=-\alpha
$$

Now write the result as $5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)$, or equivalently $5e^{j\theta}$. The modulus is unchanged; only the angle is moved to the negative principal angle for the fourth quadrant.

$$
5(\cos\theta+j\sin\theta)=5e^{j\theta}
$$

## Converting an Axis-Aligned Complex Number to Polar Form

**Example:** Convert $-6+0j$ to polar form.

**Explanation**

For this axis-aligned example, read $a=-6$ and $b=0$ from the rectangular form $z=-6+0j$. One coordinate is zero, so the reference-angle workflow stops and the axis angle can be read exactly.

$$
r=\sqrt{(-6)^2+0^2}=6
$$

Compute the modulus $r=6$, then recognize that the point lies on the negative real axis. That axis has the exact angle $\theta=\pi$, so no reference-angle correction is needed.

$$
\theta=\pi
$$

Now write the result as $6(\cos\pi+j\sin\pi)$, or equivalently $6e^{j\pi}$. The modulus is the distance from the origin, and the angle is the exact cardinal direction on the axis.

$$
6(\cos\pi+j\sin\pi)=6e^{j\pi}
$$

```quiz
  correct: true
  feedback: |-
    The point $0+6j$ lies on the positive imaginary axis, so $r=\sqrt{0^2+6^2}=6$ and the exact axis angle is $\theta=\pi/2$.

    $$
    6(\cos \pi/2+j\sin \pi/2)
    $$

    So the polar form is $6(\cos \pi/2+j\sin \pi/2)$. No reference-angle correction is needed because the number is already axis-aligned.

- id: b
  content: |-

- id: c
  content: |-
    $6(\cos \pi+j\sin \pi)$

- id: d
  content: |-
    $5(\cos \frac{\pi}{2}+j\sin \frac{\pi}{2})$

- id: e
  content: |-
    $0+6j$
```
