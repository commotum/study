## Introduction to Pure Complex Exponentials

Generalized exponential signals use $e^{st}$. Here $s$ can carry both growth or decay and oscillation.

In the pure complex case, set $s=j\omega$. The real part is zero, so the exponential envelope disappears. Use the concrete base case $e^{jt}$ first so the learner sees a unit-magnitude rotating signal before returning to general $\omega$.

Now apply Euler's formula to rewrite the signal in rectangular form.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

This rewrite is a point on the unit circle: the cosine gives the real part, the sine gives the imaginary part, and the angle changes with time while the magnitude stays fixed.

$$
|e^{j\omega t}|=1
$$

So $e^{j\omega t}$ is the pure complex exponential case: it rotates like a phasor, but it does not grow or decay.

<a id="setting-sjomega-in-the-generalized-exponential"></a>
## Setting $s=j\omega$ in the Generalized Exponential

**Example:** Starting from $e^{st}$, substitute $s=j\omega$ and rewrite the signal.

**Explanation**

The cue is the generalized exponential $e^{st}$ with a fixed complex exponent. Here the move is to set $s=j\omega$.

That substitution locks the real part of $s$ at zero, so the growth-or-decay envelope disappears and only the oscillatory part remains.

$$
e^{st}=e^{(j\omega)t}=e^{j\omega t}
$$

So the signal becomes $e^{j\omega t}$. It is still oscillatory, but it is no longer growing or decaying.

## Expanding $e^{j\omega t}$ with Euler's Formula

**Example:** Expand $e^{j\omega t}$ using Euler's formula and identify its real and imaginary parts.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pure complex exponential $e^{j\omega t}$. To expose its structure, rewrite it with Euler's formula.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

From this form, the real part is $\cos(\omega t)$ and the imaginary part is $\sin(\omega t)$.

That is the direct cosine-sine decomposition of $e^{j\omega t}$, and it is the form you will reuse in the next step.

## Interpreting $e^{j\omega t}$ as a Rotating Phasor

**Example:** Use the complex-plane picture to explain how $e^{j\omega t}$ traces a rotating phasor.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pair $\cos(\omega t)$ and $\sin(\omega t)$ from the Euler expansion. Put that pair on the complex plane as a point.

![](<../Source/2.4.4. Pure Complex Exponentials/Images/l004-s004-te-section-001.png>)

$$
\big(\Re\{e^{j\omega t}\},\Im\{e^{j\omega t}\}\big)=\big(\cos(\omega t),\sin(\omega t)\big),\qquad |e^{j\omega t}|=1
$$

The point stays on the unit circle because its magnitude is $1$. Its angle from the positive real axis is $\omega t$.

For $\omega>0$, increasing $t$ moves the point counterclockwise. That moving unit-length vector is the phasor interpretation of $e^{j\omega t}$.

<a id="recognizing-constant-magnitude-instead-of-growth-or-decay"></a>
## Recognizing Constant Magnitude Instead of Growth or Decay

**Example:** Compare $e^{j\omega t}$ with $e^{\sigma t}$ and decide whether the signal grows, decays, or stays at constant magnitude.

**Explanation**

The cue is the exponent: $e^{j\omega t}$ has real part $0$. Only a nonzero real part creates the envelope $e^{\sigma t}$ that makes a signal grow or decay.

$$
\left|e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}\right|=e^{\sigma t},\qquad \sigma=0\Rightarrow \left|e^{j\omega t}\right|=1
$$

Because $\sigma=0$, the magnitude stays at $1$ for all $t$. There is no growth and no decay.

So the correct classification is constant magnitude. Pure complex exponentials are the zero-growth, zero-decay case.
