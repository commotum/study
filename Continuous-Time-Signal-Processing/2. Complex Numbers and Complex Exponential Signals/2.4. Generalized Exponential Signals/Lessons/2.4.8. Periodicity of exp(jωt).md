## Introduction to Periodicity of $e^{j\omega t}$

The first question is simple: when does the pure complex exponential repeat, and what does it mean for a shift to leave the signal unchanged? Start with the simplest nontrivial case, $x(t)=e^{jt}$, and treat periodicity as the existence of a nonzero shift $T$ that leaves the signal exactly the same.

$$
x(t+T)=x(t)
$$

For $e^{j\omega t}$, that definition becomes the test $e^{j\omega(t+T)}=e^{j\omega t}$. The goal is to see that this equality will collapse to a condition on the product $\omega T$, so the next steps can turn repetition into a period formula.

$$
e^{j\omega(t+T)}=e^{j\omega t}
$$

Keep the $\omega=0$ case in mind as the constant-signal exception; here, the main task is to recognize the repetition test and set up the algebra that will determine the period when $\omega\neq0$.

<a id="setting-up-the-repetition-condition-for-ejomega-t"></a>
## Setting Up the Repetition Condition for $e^{j\omega t}$

**Example:** Show that shifting $e^{j\omega t}$ by $T$ leads to the condition $e^{j\omega T}=1$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the repetition test from the previous step: a periodic signal must satisfy $x(t+T)=x(t)$ for some nonzero $T$. For $x(t)=e^{j\omega t}$, write the shifted signal and set it equal to the original signal.

$$
x(t+T)=e^{j\omega(t+T)}=x(t)=e^{j\omega t}
$$

Now cancel the common factor $e^{j\omega t}$ on both sides. What remains is the phase-only condition that must hold for repetition.

$$
e^{j\omega T}=1
$$

Because $e^{j\theta}=1$ exactly when $\theta$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$, this is equivalent to $\omega T=2\pi k$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. The next step will use the smallest positive choice of $k$ to extract the fundamental period when $\omega>0$.

## Finding the Fundamental Period for Positive $\omega$

**Example:** Find the fundamental period of $e^{j5t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the phase condition from the previous step: a repeated complex exponential must satisfy $\omega T=2\pi k$. Here $\omega=5$, so the smallest positive period comes from the smallest positive integer choice of $k$.

$$
5T=2\pi
$$

Choose $k=1$ to get the first positive repetition, then solve for $T$. This gives the fundamental period of the signal.

$$
T_0=\frac{2\pi}{5}
$$

So $e^{j5t}$ repeats every $\frac{2\pi}{5}$ units of time. The period is positive, and this is the smallest shift that leaves the signal unchanged.

## Using $|\omega|$ for Negative Angular Frequency

**Example:** Find the fundamental period of $e^{-j5t}$ and express it using $|\omega|$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same repetition test as before: a periodic complex exponential must satisfy $\omega T=2\pi k$. Here $\omega=-5$, so the sign changes the phase direction but the period still has to come out positive.

$$
\omega T=2\pi k \quad \Rightarrow \quad -5T=-2\pi
$$

This gives the smallest positive shift directly. The negative sign in $\omega$ only changes which phase multiple you land on; it does not change the magnitude of the period.

$$
T_0=\frac{2\pi}{5}=\frac{2\pi}{|-5|}=\frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}
$$

So $e^{-j5t}$ repeats every $\frac{2\pi}{5}$ units of time. The minus sign does not change the fundamental period; writing the answer with $|\omega|$ keeps it explicitly positive.

## Recognizing the Constant Case $\omega=0$

**Example:** Decide what happens when $\omega=0$ in $e^{j\omega t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the endpoint of the same family. Set $\omega=0$ in $e^{j\omega t}$ and simplify; the signal becomes the constant $1$.

$$
e^{j0t}=1
$$

A constant signal does not change under any time shift, so the repetition test holds for every $T>0$.

$$
e^{j0(t+T)}=1=e^{j0t}
$$

So the nonzero-period formula $T_0=\frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}$ does not apply at $\omega=0$. The signal is periodic, but it has no unique fundamental period because every positive shift works.
