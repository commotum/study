## Introduction to Phasor Representation of a Cosine

A phasor is the magnitude-angle version of a cosine. Use $A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \mapsto A\angle\phi$ when the cosine will be handled through same-frequency phasor addition. The amplitude stays $A$, the phase shift becomes the angle, and the shared angular frequency $\omega$ stays separate.

$$
A\cos(\omega t) \rightarrow A\angle 0
$$

The simplest base case is $A\cos(\omega t)$, which has zero phase. Its phasor is $A\angle 0$, so a cosine with no phase shift becomes a phasor with angle $0$.

$$
A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \rightarrow A\angle\phi
$$

For $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, keep the amplitude unchanged and carry the phase shift into the phasor angle. The mapping preserves the information needed for later addition: the phasor stores the amplitude and phase, not the time variable.

Keep $\omega$ outside the phasor value. That separation matters later because same-frequency cosines are added only after they have the same $\omega$, and the phasors then carry only the magnitude and angle.

## Writing a Zero-Phase Cosine as a Phasor

**Example:** Convert $6\cos(\omega t)$ into phasor form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine with no phase shift: $A\cos(\omega t)$. Use the phasor convention that keeps the amplitude and converts the phase to an angle, so the base form is $A\angle 0$.

$$
A\cos(\omega t) \rightarrow A\angle 0
$$

For this example, the amplitude is $6$ and the phase shift is $0$, so the cosine $6\cos(\omega t)$ becomes the phasor $6\angle 0$. Nothing changes except the notation: the time-varying cosine is now written as a magnitude-angle pair.

$$
6\cos(\omega t) \rightarrow 6\angle 0
$$

Keep the shared angular frequency $\omega$ outside the phasor. The phasor stores only amplitude and phase, which is the form needed later when same-frequency terms are added.

## Writing a Positive-Phase Cosine as a Phasor

**Example:** Convert $4\cos(\omega t+\pi/3)$ into phasor form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine with a positive phase shift: $4\cos(\omega t+\pi/3)$. Use the phasor rule $A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \mapsto A\angle\phi$, so the amplitude stays $A$ and the phase becomes the angle.

$$
A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \rightarrow A\angle\phi
$$

For this example, the amplitude is $4$ and the phase shift is $\pi/3$, so the cosine $4\cos(\omega t+\pi/3)$ becomes the phasor $4\angle\pi/3$. The positive sign stays in the angle; it does not change the amplitude.

$$
4\cos(\omega t+\pi/3) \rightarrow 4\angle\pi/3
$$

Keep the shared angular frequency $\omega$ outside the phasor. The phasor stores only magnitude and phase, which is the format needed later when same-frequency terms are added.

## Writing a Negative-Phase Cosine as a Phasor

**Example:** Convert $10\cos(\omega t-\pi/6)$ into phasor form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine with a negative phase shift: $10\cos(\omega t-\pi/6)$. Use the phasor rule $A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \mapsto A\angle\phi$, so the amplitude stays $A$ and the phase stays attached to the angle.

$$
A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \rightarrow A\angle\phi
$$

For this example, the amplitude is $10$ and the phase shift is $-\pi/6$, so the cosine $10\cos(\omega t-\pi/6)$ becomes the phasor $10\angle-\pi/6$. The minus sign stays in the angle; it does not move to the amplitude.

$$
10\cos(\omega t-\pi/6) \rightarrow 10\angle-\pi/6
$$

Keep the shared angular frequency $\omega$ outside the phasor. The phasor stores only magnitude and phase, which is the format needed later when same-frequency terms are added.

## Separating the Shared Angular Frequency from the Phasor

**Example:** Convert $12\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/4)$ into phasor form and keep $\omega_0$ separate from the phasor value.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine with shared angular frequency $\omega_0$ and phase shift $-\pi/4$: $12\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/4)$. Use the phasor rule $A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \mapsto A\angle\phi$, and keep the angular frequency outside the phasor.

$$
A\cos(\omega t+\phi) \rightarrow A\angle\phi
$$

For this example, the amplitude is $12$ and the phase shift is $-\pi/4$, so the cosine $12\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/4)$ becomes the phasor $12\angle-\pi/4$. The symbol $\omega_0$ stays separate because the phasor holds only magnitude and phase.

$$
12\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/4) \rightarrow 12\angle-\pi/4
$$

That separation matters later when same-frequency cosines are added. The phasor carries the amplitude and phase, while the shared angular frequency is handled outside the phasor value.
