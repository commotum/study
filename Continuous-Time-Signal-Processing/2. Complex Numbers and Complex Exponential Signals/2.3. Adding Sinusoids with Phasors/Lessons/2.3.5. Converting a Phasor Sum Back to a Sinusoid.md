## Introduction to Converting a Phasor Sum Back to a Sinusoid

When a polar phasor sum is written as $A\angle\phi$ at a common angular frequency $\omega_0$, the conversion target is one same-frequency cosine. Read the magnitude as amplitude, read the angle as phase, and keep the angular frequency unchanged.

$$
1\angle 0 \leftrightarrow \cos(\omega_0 t)
$$

The base case shows the defaults: magnitude $1$ becomes amplitude $1$, and angle $0$ becomes zero phase. Nothing changes in the frequency term, so the same $\omega_0$ stays outside the phasor-to-cosine mapping.

$$
A\angle\phi \leftrightarrow A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)
$$

So the rule is simple: magnitude becomes amplitude, angle becomes phase, and the shared $\omega_0$ stays fixed. The next examples in this topic will keep that same mapping while isolating amplitude, phase, frequency, and then the full conversion.

## Identifying the Sinusoid Amplitude from Phasor Magnitude

**Example:** A phasor sum is $7\angle 25^\circ$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0$. Identify the amplitude of the equivalent same-frequency cosine.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the phasor magnitude. Here the phasor sum is $7\angle 25^\circ$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0$, so the number to read first is the $7$.

$$
A = 7
$$

Match that magnitude to the amplitude of the equivalent same-frequency cosine. The angle $25^\circ$ stays in the background for this step, and the common angular frequency $\omega_0$ is unchanged.

So the amplitude is $7$. This example isolates only the magnitude-to-amplitude mapping; the phase and full cosine form are handled in later steps.

## Identifying the Sinusoid Phase from Phasor Angle

**Example:** A phasor sum is $4\angle -\pi/3$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0$. Identify the phase of the equivalent same-frequency cosine.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the phasor angle. Here the phasor sum is $4\angle -\pi/3$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0$, so the angle to read is $-\pi/3$.

$$
\phi = -\pi/3
$$

Match that angle to the phase of the equivalent same-frequency cosine. Preserve the sign exactly, and keep the amplitude $4$ and the common angular frequency $\omega_0$ unchanged for this step.

So the phase is $-\pi/3$. This example isolates only the angle-to-phase mapping; the full cosine form is handled later.

## Preserving the Common Angular Frequency in the Sinusoid

**Example:** A phasor sum is $3\angle \pi/6$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0=4$. Write the matching cosine and keep the frequency exactly as given.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the common angular frequency. Here the phasor sum is $3\angle \pi/6$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0=4$, so the frequency term stays fixed at $4$.

$$
x(t) = 3\cos(4t+\pi/6)
$$

Keep that $4$ unchanged in the cosine argument. The magnitude $3$ gives the amplitude and the angle $\pi/6$ gives the phase; this step only checks that the frequency is preserved exactly as stated.

So the matching cosine is $x(t)=3\cos(4t+\pi/6)$. No new frequency appears, and no existing frequency term is changed.

## Writing the Complete Cosine from a Polar Phasor Sum

**Example:** A phasor sum is $9\angle -\pi/4$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0=2$. Write the complete equivalent cosine.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the complete conversion. Read the magnitude as the amplitude, read the angle as the phase, and carry the common angular frequency through unchanged.

$$
x(t) = 9\cos(2t-\pi/4)
$$

Here the phasor sum is $9\angle -\pi/4$ at common angular frequency $\omega_0=2$, so the amplitude is $9$, the phase is $-\pi/4$, and the frequency term stays $2t$.

So the complete equivalent cosine is $x(t)=9\cos(2t-\pi/4)$. This final example combines the three isolated mappings from the earlier steps into one answer.
