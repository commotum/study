## Introduction to Amplitude-Phase Form of a Sinusoidal Sum

A same-frequency sinusoidal sum should be read as a single cosine of the form $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ after the component phasors are added. This lesson is about turning the sum into that one cosine and reading off the combined amplitude and phase.

$$
A_1\cos(\omega t+\phi_1)+A_2\cos(\omega t+\phi_2)
$$

Do not add the amplitudes and phases directly. Keep the shared $\omega$ fixed, convert each cosine to its phasor, and use phasor addition as the only combination step.

The phasor bridge is the same for each term: a cosine becomes a phasor with the same magnitude and angle, and those phasors add as vectors.

$$
A_1\angle\phi_1 + A_2\angle\phi_2 = A\angle\phi
$$

In the base two-cosine case, the resultant phasor gives the answer: its magnitude becomes the new amplitude and its angle becomes the new phase. Later examples will keep the same angular frequency and add only one new wrinkle to this phasor-to-cosine loop.

So the reusable rule is simple: hold the common $\omega$ fixed, add the component phasors, and read the resultant magnitude and angle as the single cosine's amplitude and phase.

## Combining Two Same-Frequency Cosines into One Cosine

**Example:** Combine $4\cos(8t+\pi/3)$ and $2\cos(8t-\pi/6)$ into one cosine in amplitude-phase form.

**Explanation**

Both terms share the same angular frequency $8$, so the right move is to convert each cosine to a phasor, add the phasors, and then read the resultant magnitude and angle back as one cosine.

$$
4\cos(8t+\pi/3)+2\cos(8t-\pi/6)
$$

Convert each cosine to a phasor first. Then add the phasors in rectangular form so the shared frequency stays fixed and the arithmetic stays component-wise.

$$
\begin{aligned}
4\angle\frac{\pi}{3} &= 2 + j2\sqrt{3} \\
2\angle\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) &= \sqrt{3} - j \\
4\angle\frac{\pi}{3} + 2\angle\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) &= (2+\sqrt{3}) + j(2\sqrt{3}-1)
\end{aligned}
$$

The resultant phasor has magnitude $2\sqrt{5}$, and its angle is the quadrant-I angle of $(2+\sqrt{3})+j(2\sqrt{3}-1)$. That magnitude and angle are the new amplitude and phase of the combined cosine.

$$
2\sqrt{5}\cos\left(8t+\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{3}-1}{2+\sqrt{3}}\right)\right)
$$

So the complete loop is: keep the common $\omega$ unchanged, add the component phasors, and convert the resultant magnitude and angle back into one cosine.

## Finding the Resultant Amplitude and Phase from Component Phasors

**Example:** The component phasors are $3+j4$ and $2-j1$. Find the amplitude and phase of the combined sinusoid.

**Explanation**

The component phasors are already in rectangular form, so the first move is to add the real parts and the imaginary parts separately.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(3+j4) + (2-j1) &= (3+2) + j(4-1) \\
&= 5+j3
\end{aligned}
$$

That rectangular result is the resultant phasor. Because both parts are positive, the angle is the quadrant-I angle of the point $(5,3)$.

$$
5+j3 = \sqrt{5^2+3^2}\angle\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = \sqrt{34}\angle\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)
$$

So the combined sinusoid has amplitude $\sqrt{34}$ and phase $\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)$ at the shared angular frequency. Later examples can reuse the same rectangular-addition workflow and only change the component values.

## Keeping the Correct Quadrant for the Resultant Phase

**Example:** Convert the phasor sum $-2+j3$ into the amplitude-phase form of the corresponding cosine.

**Explanation**

The cue is the rectangular phasor $-2+j3$. Treat it as a vector: first find its magnitude, then choose the phase from the signs of the real and imaginary parts.

$$
|Z| = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{13}
$$

Use the absolute values to get the reference angle. That gives $\theta_{\text{ref}} = \tan^{-1}(3/2)$, but this is not the final phase yet.

$$
-2 < 0,\; 3 > 0 \Rightarrow \theta \text{ is in Quadrant II } \Rightarrow \theta = \pi - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)
$$

So the phasor is $\sqrt{13}\angle\left(\pi - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\right)$, and the corresponding cosine is $x(t)=\sqrt{13}\cos\left(\omega t+\pi-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\right)$.

## Combining More Than Two Same-Frequency Cosines

**Example:** Combine $2\cos(6t)+2\cos(6t+\pi/3)+2\cos(6t+2\pi/3)$ into one cosine in amplitude-phase form.

**Explanation**

The cue is three same-frequency cosines at angular frequency $6$. Put each term into a phasor, add the phasors, and then convert the result back to amplitude-phase form.

$$
Z = 2\angle 0 + 2\angle \frac{\pi}{3} + 2\angle \frac{2\pi}{3}
$$

Convert each phasor to rectangular form so the real and imaginary parts combine directly. Here the $1$ and $-1$ real parts cancel, which makes the sum easy to read.

$$
Z = (2+j0) + \left(1+j\sqrt{3}\right) + \left(-1+j\sqrt{3}\right) = 2 + j2\sqrt{3}
$$

Now read off the magnitude and phase from the rectangular sum. Since $Z$ is in Quadrant I, the principal angle is the correct angle.

$$
|Z| = \sqrt{2^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2} = 4,\qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3},\qquad x(t)=4\cos\left(6t+\frac{\pi}{3}\right)
$$

So the three cosines combine to one cosine at the same frequency $6$, with amplitude $4$ and phase $\pi/3$.
