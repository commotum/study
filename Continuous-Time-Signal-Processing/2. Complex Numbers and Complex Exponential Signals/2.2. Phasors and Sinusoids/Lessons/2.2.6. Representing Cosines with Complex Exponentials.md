## Introduction to Representing Cosines with Complex Exponentials

A cosine like $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ can be rewritten so the time variation stays in $e^{j\omega t}$ and the amplitude-phase information stays in a fixed complex factor. That fixed factor is the phasor $Ae^{j\phi}$.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

Taking the real part of that identity gives the base case $\cos(\omega t)=\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j\omega t}\}$. So the unit cosine is already the real part of a rotating complex exponential.

$$
\operatorname{Re}\{Ae^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}\}=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
$$

If we multiply by $A$ and place the phase $\phi$ in the phasor factor, the same rule becomes $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\operatorname{Re}\{Ae^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}\}$. The amplitude and phase stay fixed; only $e^{j\omega t}$ carries time.

That is the form to recognize in later steps: the cosine is read from a real-part expression, not rebuilt from scratch. The same idea can also be written as a mirrored conjugate-exponential pair, which the next examples will unpack.

Keep the two roles separate: $Ae^{j\phi}$ is the fixed phasor, and $e^{j\omega t}$ is the time-varying factor. When you see $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, the conversion target is the real part of that product.

## Writing a Unit Cosine as a Real Part

**Example:** Rewrite $\cos(4t)$ as a real part.

**Explanation**

The cue is a unit cosine with no phase shift, so the phasor factor should be just $1$. That means the target form is the real part of the matching complex exponential.

$$
e^{j4t}=\cos(4t)+j\sin(4t)
$$

Taking the real part of both sides gives $\cos(4t)=\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j4t}\}$. This is the zero-phase base case, and later examples will keep the same real-part structure while changing only amplitude or phase.

## Including Amplitude in the Real-Part Form

**Example:** Rewrite $5\cos(4t)$ as a real part with the amplitude inside the phasor factor.

**Explanation**

Start from the unit-cosine identity from the previous example. Multiplying by $5$ keeps the time factor $e^{j4t}$ unchanged and moves the amplitude into the phasor factor.

$$
5\cos(4t)=5\,\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j4t}\}=\operatorname{Re}\{5e^{j4t}\}
$$

So the canonical real-part form is $5\cos(4t)=\operatorname{Re}\{5e^{j4t}\}$. The only change from the unit-cosine case is the amplitude inside the phasor factor; the oscillation in $e^{j4t}$ stays the same.

<a id="including-phase-in-the-complex-phasor-factor"></a>
## Including Phase in the Complex Phasor Factor

**Example:** Rewrite $4\cos(3t+\pi/6)$ as a real part with the phase isolated in the phasor factor.

**Explanation**

Identify the parts first: $A=4$, $\omega=3$, and $\phi=\pi/6$. The phase belongs in the fixed phasor factor $4e^{j\pi/6}$, while $e^{j3t}$ remains the time factor.

$$
4\cos(3t+\pi/6)=4\,\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j(3t+\pi/6)}\}=4\,\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j\pi/6}e^{j3t}\}=\operatorname{Re}\{4e^{j\pi/6}e^{j3t}\}
$$

So the canonical form is $4\cos(3t+\pi/6)=\operatorname{Re}\{4e^{j\pi/6}e^{j3t}\}$. The new ingredient is the phase factor inside the phasor; the time dependence stays in $e^{j3t}$.

## Writing a Cosine as a Conjugate Exponential Sum

**Example:** Rewrite $2\cos(5t)$ as a sum of conjugate complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

From Euler's formula, a cosine is the average of a complex exponential and its conjugate. For $2\cos(5t)$, the amplitude splits evenly, so each term gets a coefficient of $1$.

$$
2\cos(5t)=2\cdot\frac{e^{j5t}+e^{-j5t}}{2}=e^{j5t}+e^{-j5t}
$$

So the conjugate-sum form is $2\cos(5t)=e^{j5t}+e^{-j5t}$. The key pattern is symmetry: opposite exponential signs and equal half-amplitude coefficients.

<a id="converting-between-real-part-form-and-conjugate-sum-form"></a>
## Converting Between Real-Part Form and Conjugate-Sum Form

**Example:** Convert $\frac{5}{2}e^{j(4t-\pi/6)}+\frac{5}{2}e^{-j(4t-\pi/6)}$ into a real-part form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a conjugate pair with equal coefficients, so it should collapse to a single cosine. Here the pair has amplitude $5$ and phase $-\pi/6$, with $e^{j4t}$ carrying the time dependence.

$$
\frac{5}{2}e^{j(4t-\pi/6)}+\frac{5}{2}e^{-j(4t-\pi/6)}=5\,\operatorname{Re}\{e^{j(4t-\pi/6)}\}=\operatorname{Re}\{5e^{-j\pi/6}e^{j4t}\}
$$

So the canonical real-part form is $\operatorname{Re}\{5e^{-j\pi/6}e^{j4t}\}$. The phase sits in the phasor factor, and the time factor stays $e^{j4t}$.

$$
\operatorname{Re}\{5e^{-j\pi/6}e^{j4t}\}=5\cos(4t-\pi/6)=\frac{5}{2}e^{j(4t-\pi/6)}+\frac{5}{2}e^{-j(4t-\pi/6)}
$$
