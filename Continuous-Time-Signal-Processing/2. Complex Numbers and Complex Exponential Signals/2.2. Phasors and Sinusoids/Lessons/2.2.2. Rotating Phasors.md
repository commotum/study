## Introduction to Rotating Phasors

A rotating phasor is a complex number with fixed magnitude whose angle advances uniformly with time. The cue is that only the angle changes; the length stays constant.

$$
\Delta\theta=\omega t
$$

Start with the simplest base case, $A=1$ and $\phi=0$. Then elapsed time alone determines the angle, so the rotation after time $t$ is $\theta(t)=\omega t$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t
$$

A nonzero initial phase $\phi$ just shifts the starting angle. The instantaneous angle is the initial phase plus the elapsed rotation.

$$
\theta(t)=\phi+\omega t
$$

Write the phasor itself by putting that angle into the complex exponential. This keeps the magnitude fixed and lets the angle advance uniformly.

$$
z(t)=Ae^{j\theta(t)}=Ae^{j(\phi+\omega t)}
$$

At $t=0$, the formula returns the initial phasor $z(0)=Ae^{j\phi}$. That confirms the rule: the angle starts at $\phi$ and then increases by $\omega t$.

$$
z(0)=Ae^{j\phi}
$$

## Computing Rotation Angle from Elapsed Time

**Example:** A phasor starts at angle $0$ and rotates at angular frequency $\omega=2$ rad/s. What angle has it rotated through after $t=3$ s?

**Explanation**

The cue is a phasor with zero initial phase, so the angle after elapsed time comes only from the rotation amount. The rule is the product $\omega t$.

$$
\Delta\theta=\omega t
$$

Substitute the given angular frequency and elapsed time: $\omega=2$ rad/s and $t=3$ s. Then multiply to find the rotation amount.

$$
\Delta\theta=(2)(3)=6
$$

Because $\phi=0$, the instantaneous angle is the same as the elapsed rotation. So after $3$ s, the phasor has rotated through $6$ radians.

## Adding Initial Phase to the Rotation Angle

**Example:** A phasor has initial phase $\phi=\pi/3$ and rotates at angular frequency $\omega=2\pi$ rad/s. What is its instantaneous angle after $t=\tfrac{1}{4}$ s?

**Explanation**

The cue is a nonzero initial phase, so the instantaneous angle must include both the starting angle and the elapsed rotation. Use the same rule from the previous step: $\theta(t)=\phi+\omega t$.

$$
\theta(t)=\phi+\omega t
$$

Substitute the given values $\phi=\pi/3$, $\omega=2\pi$, and $t=\tfrac{1}{4}$. Then compute the elapsed rotation first so the addition stays clean.

$$
\theta(t)=\frac{\pi}{3}+(2\pi)\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)=\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{\pi}{2}
$$

Add the angles to get a single instantaneous angle. The phasor is at $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ after $\tfrac{1}{4}$ s.

$$
\theta(t)=\frac{5\pi}{6}
$$

## Writing the Instantaneous Phasor from Its Angle

**Example:** A phasor has magnitude $A=3$ and instantaneous angle $5\pi/6$. Write the phasor in complex exponential form.

**Explanation**

The cue is the magnitude and angle of the phasor. For a phasor, use the standard complex exponential form $z=Ae^{j\theta}$.

$$
z=Ae^{j\theta}
$$

Substitute the given magnitude $A=3$ and angle $\theta=5\pi/6$ directly into the form.

$$
z=3e^{j5\pi/6}
$$

That is the instantaneous phasor in complex exponential form.

## Reducing a Phasor Angle After Full Rotations

**Example:** A phasor has angle $11\pi/3$. Reduce it to an equivalent angle in $[0,2\pi)$ and write the corresponding phasor.

**Explanation**

The cue is an angle that is larger than one full turn. Phasor angles repeat every $2\pi$, so subtract full $2\pi$ turns until the angle lies in $[0,2\pi)$.

$$
\frac{11\pi}{3}-2\pi=\frac{11\pi}{3}-\frac{6\pi}{3}=\frac{5\pi}{3}
$$

The reduced angle $\frac{5\pi}{3}$ is in the requested interval and is coterminal with $\frac{11\pi}{3}$. The phasor itself does not change; only the angle label changes.

$$
z=e^{j11\pi/3}=e^{j5\pi/3}
$$

So the equivalent angle is $\frac{5\pi}{3}$, and the same phasor can be written with that reduced angle.
