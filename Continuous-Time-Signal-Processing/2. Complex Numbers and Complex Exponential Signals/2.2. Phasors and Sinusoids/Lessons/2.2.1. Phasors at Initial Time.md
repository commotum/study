## Introduction to Phasors at Initial Time

A phasor at initial time is the fixed complex snapshot of a sinusoid at $t=0$. For a sinusoid written as $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, the amplitude $A$ becomes the phasor's magnitude and the phase $\phi$ becomes its angle.

$$
x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
$$

Use the zero-phase case first: if $\phi=0$, the initial phasor sits on the positive real axis. In that base case, the snapshot is just the positive real number $A$, so the learner can see directly how amplitude sets the length before any phase rotation is added.

$$
X_0=Ae^{j\phi}
$$

For an arbitrary phase, the same initial phasor is written in exponential form as $X_0=Ae^{j\phi}$. That representation keeps the magnitude in $A$ and the angle in $\phi$, and later lessons will use that same phasor to show how rotation and axis projections come from the same fixed starting point.

$$
X_0=A\cos\phi+jA\sin\phi
$$

In rectangular form, the real part is $A\cos\phi$ and the imaginary part is $A\sin\phi$. So the initial phasor is the same object in three views: a sinusoid's amplitude and phase, a complex exponential $Ae^{j\phi}$, and a rectangular complex number with real and imaginary parts set by $\phi$.

<a id="writing-an-initial-phasor-with-zero-phase"></a>
## Writing an Initial Phasor with Zero Phase

**Example:** Given $x(t)=6\cos(\omega t)$, write its initial phasor.

**Explanation**

The cue here is zero phase, so the sinusoid is already in the simplest base case. The amplitude is $6$, and at $t=0$ that amplitude becomes the phasor's magnitude while the phase stays at angle $0$.

$$
x(t)=6\cos(\omega t)
$$

Because the phase is $0$, the initial phasor sits on the positive real axis. Write the phasor in exponential form first, using the amplitude as the magnitude and $0$ as the angle.

$$
X_0=6e^{j0}
$$

Since $e^{j0}=1$, the initial phasor simplifies to a real number. The result is the complex snapshot $6$, which is the same phasor written on the positive real axis.

$$
X_0=6
$$

## Writing an Initial Phasor with Positive Phase

**Example:** Given $x(t)=8\cos(\omega t+\pi/3)$, write its initial phasor.

**Explanation**

The cue here is positive phase, so the only change from the zero-phase case is the angle of the phasor. The amplitude is $8$, and the phase $+\pi/3$ becomes the phasor angle at $t=0$.

$$
x(t)=8\cos(\omega t+\pi/3)
$$

Write the initial phasor in exponential form by keeping $8$ as the magnitude and using the positive phase as the angle. A positive angle places the phasor above the positive real axis.

$$
X_0=8e^{j\pi/3}
$$

If you expand that phasor into rectangular form, the real part is $8\cos(\pi/3)$ and the imaginary part is $8\sin(\pi/3)$. That confirms the same initial phasor sits in the upper half-plane and keeps the phase sign visible in the complex snapshot.

$$
X_0=8\cos(\pi/3)+j8\sin(\pi/3)
$$

$$
x(t)=10\cos(\omega t-\pi/4)
$$

X_0=10e^{-j\pi/4}

$$
X_0=10\cos(\pi/4)-j10\sin(\pi/4)
$$

<a id="converting-an-initial-phasor-to-rectangular-form"></a>
## Converting an Initial Phasor to Rectangular Form

**Example:** Convert the initial phasor $8e^{j\pi/3}$ to rectangular form.

**Explanation**

Start with the initial phasor in exponential form and use $Ae^{j\phi}=A\cos\phi+jA\sin\phi$ to read off the real and imaginary parts.

$$
X_0=8e^{j\pi/3}
$$

Rewrite the exponential with cosine and sine, keeping the same amplitude $A=8$ and phase $\phi=\pi/3$.

$$
X_0=8\cos(\pi/3)+j8\sin(\pi/3)
$$

Now evaluate the trig values: $\cos(\pi/3)=1/2$ and $\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt{3}/2$. That gives the rectangular form with a real part of $4$ and an imaginary part of $4\sqrt{3}$.

$$
X_0=4+j4\sqrt{3}
$$

```quiz
  correct: true
  feedback: |-
    Use $Ae^{j\phi}=A\cos\phi+jA\sin\phi$ with $A=6$ and $\phi=-\pi/3$.

    $$
    6e^{-j\pi/3}=6\cos(-\pi/3)+j6\sin(-\pi/3)=3-j3\sqrt{3}
    $$

    So the rectangular form is $3-j3\sqrt{3}$.

- id: b
  content: |-
    $-3\sqrt{3}+j3$

- id: c
  content: |-
    $3+j3\sqrt{3}$

- id: d
  content: |-
    $6e^{-j\pi/3}$

- id: e
  content: |-
    $-3\sqrt{3}-j3\sqrt{3}$
```
