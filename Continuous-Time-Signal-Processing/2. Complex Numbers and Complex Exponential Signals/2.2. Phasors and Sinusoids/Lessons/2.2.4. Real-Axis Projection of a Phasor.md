## Introduction to Real-Axis Projection of a Phasor

A phasor is a vector in the complex plane whose tip moves as it rotates. Its real-axis projection is the horizontal shadow on the real axis, and that shadow is the cosine-valued signal we want to read from the motion.

![](<../Source/2.2.4. Real-Axis Projection of a Phasor/Images/l004-s001-te-section-001.png>)

Start with the simplest base case: a unit phasor on the positive real axis projects to $1$. If you scale that same picture to magnitude $A$ and tilt it to angle $\theta$, the real-axis shadow is the adjacent side, so its length is $A\cos\theta$.

$$
\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j\theta}\}=A\cos\theta
$$

The same rule is written compactly by taking the real part of $A e^{j\theta}$. That notation says to keep the horizontal component of the phasor and ignore the vertical one.

Now let the angle vary with time. For counterclockwise rotation at angular frequency $\omega$ with initial phase $\phi$, the angle is $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi,\qquad x(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
$$

So the real-axis projection of the rotating phasor is exactly the cosine signal $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$. Later sections keep the same projection rule and vary only the rotation direction or the time at which you evaluate it.

## Projecting a Fixed Phasor onto the Real Axis

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ sits at a fixed angle $\phi$ above the positive real axis. What is its real-axis projection?

**Explanation**

The cue here is a fixed phasor: its tip is not rotating yet, so we only read its current angle $\phi$ and its magnitude $A$. The real-axis projection is the horizontal component of that vector.

For any phasor at angle $\phi$ from the positive real axis, the horizontal component is the adjacent side of the right triangle. That means the projection is the magnitude times $\cos\phi$.

$$
\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j\phi}\}=A\cos\phi
$$

So the real-axis projection is $A\cos\phi$. In complex-number language, this is just the real part of the phasor $A e^{j\phi}$, which is the same rule the next steps will reuse when the angle begins to depend on time.

## Writing the Real-Axis Projection of a Counterclockwise Rotating Phasor

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts at phase $\phi$ and rotates counterclockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write the real-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is counterclockwise rotation, so the phasor angle is not fixed. It starts at $\phi$ and increases linearly with time at rate $\omega$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi
$$

Use the same real-axis projection rule from the fixed-phasor case: keep the magnitude $A$ and take the cosine of the angle. That gives the horizontal shadow of the rotating phasor at time $t$.

$$
x(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
$$

So the real-axis projection signal is $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$. The only new wrinkle is that the angle now depends on time, while the projection rule itself stays the same.

## Writing the Real-Axis Projection of a Clockwise Rotating Phasor

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts at phase $\phi$ and rotates clockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write the real-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is clockwise rotation, so the phasor angle decreases from its starting value $\phi$ as time increases. With angular frequency $\omega$, the angle is $\theta(t)=\phi-\omega t$.

$$
\theta(t)=\phi-\omega t
$$

Use the same real-axis projection rule as in the previous example: keep the magnitude $A$ and take the cosine of the angle. The only thing that changes is the sign in the angle progression.

$$
x(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j(\phi-\omega t)}\}=A\cos(\phi-\omega t)
$$

So the real-axis projection signal is $x(t)=A\cos(\phi-\omega t)$. The only new wrinkle is the direction of rotation, which makes the angle fall instead of rise.

## Computing Real-Axis Projection Values at Specific Times

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts at phase $\phi$ and rotates counterclockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. What is its real-axis projection at time $t=t_0$?

**Explanation**

The cue is a specific time. For counterclockwise rotation, the phasor angle grows from its starting phase, so write the angle first as $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi
$$

Now substitute the requested time before evaluating the cosine: $\theta(t_0)=\omega t_0+\phi$.

$$
\theta(t_0)=\omega t_0+\phi
$$

The real-axis projection keeps the magnitude $A$ and takes the cosine of the angle at that time.

$$
x(t_0)=A\cos(\theta(t_0))=A\cos(\omega t_0+\phi)
$$

So the exact projection value is $A\cos(\omega t_0+\phi)$. If later data make the angle a special trig angle, simplify it from there.
