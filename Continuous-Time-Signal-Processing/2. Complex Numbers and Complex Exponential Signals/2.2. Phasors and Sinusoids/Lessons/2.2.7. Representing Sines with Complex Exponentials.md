## Introduction to Representing Sines with Complex Exponentials

A sine wave is the imaginary-axis projection of a rotating phasor, so the algebraic form we want is the one that exposes that projection directly. Start from Euler's formulas and subtract the conjugate pair; that is the shortest path from phasor motion to a sine identity.

$$
\begin{aligned}\ne^{j\theta} &= \cos\theta + j\sin\theta \\\ne^{-j\theta} &= \cos\theta - j\sin\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtract the second formula from the first. The cosine terms cancel, and the sine terms combine into a factor of $2j$, which is the algebraic sign that the imaginary-axis projection is being isolated.

$$
e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta} = 2j\sin\theta
$$

Now solve for $\sin\theta$ to get the base identity used throughout this topic. This is the unit, zero-phase case, and it is the form to keep in mind before amplitude and phase are added.

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}
$$

Now replace $\theta$ with $\omega t + \phi$. The same identity becomes the sine of a time-varying phase, and multiplying by $A$ gives the general amplitude-phase form that later examples will reuse.

$$
A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{A}{2j}e^{j(\omega t+\phi)} - \frac{A}{2j}e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}
$$

So the standard rewrite is one sine wave represented as a matched positive-frequency and negative-frequency pair. Later examples keep the same structure and only change the amplitude or phase, not the underlying exponential difference.

## Converting a Unit-Amplitude Sine with Zero Phase

**Example:** Convert $\sin(\omega t)$ into a difference of complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit-amplitude, zero-phase sine $\sin(\omega t)$, so we use the base identity from the tutorial step. Because the amplitude is $1$ and the phase is $0$, no extra factors are needed.

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}
$$

Now substitute $\theta=\omega t$. The conjugate pair stays intact, and the only change is the time-dependent phase inside each exponential.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin(\omega t) &= \frac{e^{j\omega t} - e^{-j\omega t}}{2j} \\
&= \frac{1}{2j}e^{j\omega t} - \frac{1}{2j}e^{-j\omega t}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the zero-phase, unit-amplitude rewrite of a sine wave as a difference of positive-frequency and negative-frequency exponentials. Later example steps will keep this same structure and only add amplitude or phase.

## Including Amplitude in the Sine Exponential Difference

**Example:** Convert $A\sin(\omega t)$ into a difference of complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same zero-phase sine from the previous example, but now with a scalar amplitude $A$ in front. That means we keep the same exponential-difference structure and carry the factor $A$ through the entire identity.

$$
\sin(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} - e^{-j\omega t}}{2j}
$$

Multiply both sides by $A$. The amplitude does not change the positive-frequency and negative-frequency pair; it only scales both terms by the same factor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\sin(\omega t) &= A\cdot\frac{e^{j\omega t} - e^{-j\omega t}}{2j} \\
&= \frac{A}{2j}e^{j\omega t} - \frac{A}{2j}e^{-j\omega t}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the amplitude-scaled sine is the same conjugate exponential difference, with $A$ attached to both sides of the pair. The structure is unchanged; only the overall scale is different.

## Including Phase in the Sine Exponential Difference

**Example:** Convert $\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ into a difference of complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is the phase shift inside the sine argument. Treat the whole argument as $\theta=\omega t+\phi$ and reuse the same sine identity from the previous examples.

$$
\sin\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}
$$

Now substitute $\theta=\omega t+\phi$ into both exponentials. The phase stays inside the argument of each term, so the positive-frequency and negative-frequency parts shift together.

$$
\sin(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}-e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}}{2j}
$$

So the phase-shifted sine has the same exponential-difference structure as before, with the same argument in both terms. The only change from the zero-phase case is that $\phi$ rides inside each exponential.

## Converting a General Amplitude-Phase Sine

**Example:** Convert $A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ into a difference of complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is both the amplitude $A$ and the phase shift $\phi$. Start from the phase-only identity and then scale the entire expression by $A$.

$$
\sin(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}-e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}}{2j}
$$

Multiplying by $A$ carries the same factor through both exponentials. The phase stays inside both arguments, and the amplitude scales both terms equally.

$$
A\sin(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}
$$

That is the full exponential-difference form for a sine with amplitude and phase. The positive-frequency and negative-frequency terms share the same phase argument; only the overall scale changes.
