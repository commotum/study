## Introduction to Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output

For an LTI system, the impulse response $h(t)$ characterizes the system. When the input is $x(t)$, the output is the convolution of $x(t)$ with $h(t)$.

$$
y(t)=(x*h)(t)
$$

![](<../Source/7.4.7. Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output/Images/l007-s001-te-section-001.png>)

The convolution formula still tells you where $y(t)$ comes from, but in this lesson the main move is interpretation. Once the convolution has already been computed, treat the resulting piecewise function as the time-domain output signal $y(t)$.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

For a finite-duration input and a finite-duration impulse response, the computed output is often a piecewise signal. A simple case has zero output before the signals overlap, a nonzero region where the output rises, reaches a peak or plateau, and then falls, and zero output again after overlap ends.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}
0, & t<a \\
y_1(t), & a\le t<b \\
y_2(t), & b\le t<c \\
0, & t\ge c
\end{cases}
$$

To interpret the result, first name the piecewise function as $y(t)$. Then read where $y(t)$ is zero, where it is nonzero, which breakpoints divide the time axis, and how the output changes on each interval.

Those breakpoints come from changes in overlap between $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$. Here, you use that overlap idea to explain the output behavior, not to recompute the convolution integral from scratch.

## Identifying the Convolution Result as the Output $y(t)$

**Example:** An LTI system has input $x(t)$, impulse response $h(t)$, and the convolution has already been computed as (x*h)$(t)=g(t)$. Identify the system output $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

For an LTI system, the output rule is that the output equals the convolution of the input with the impulse response.

$$
y(t)=(x*h)(t)
$$

Here, the convolution has already been computed and named $g(t)$. Match the given statement to the output rule.

$$
(x*h)(t)=g(t)
$$

Since $y(t)$ is the convolution result, the system output is $g(t)$.

$$
y(t)=g(t)
$$

The output is not $x(t)$ alone, $h(t)$ alone, or the product $x(t)$$h(t)$. It is the computed convolution of the input and impulse response.

## Reading Zero and Nonzero Output Regions

**Example:** A plotted convolution output $y(t)$ is zero before $t=a$, nonzero from $t=a$ to $t=b$, and zero after $t=b$. Identify the zero and nonzero output regions.

**Explanation**

The plotted curve is the computed convolution output, so read it as $y(t)$. The task is not to recompute the convolution integral; it is to read where the output exists over time.

![](<../Source/7.4.7. Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output/Images/l007-s003-te-section-003.png>)

Before the first breakpoint a, the graph lies on the zero line. Therefore the output is zero for times before a.

$$
y(t)=0, \qquad t<a
$$

From a to b, the graph leaves the zero line. That interval is the nonzero output region.

$$
y(t)\ne 0, \qquad a<t<b
$$

After the second breakpoint b, the graph returns to the zero line. Therefore the output is zero for times after b.

$$
y(t)=0, \qquad t>b
$$

So the output is nonzero from a to b, and it is zero before a and after b. The endpoint convention is not the main issue here; a and b are the support boundaries of the output.

## Connecting Piecewise Breakpoints to Changing Overlap

**Example:** A finite-duration input and impulse response produce a piecewise output $y(t)$ with breakpoints at $t=a$, $t=b$, and $t=c$. Explain what changes in the overlap between $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$ at each breakpoint.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $y(t)$ is piecewise. A breakpoint in $y(t)$ marks a time when the overlap relationship between $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$ changes. We will read the breakpoints in increasing order.

$$
a<b<c
$$

![](<../Source/7.4.7. Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output/Images/l007-s004-te-section-004.png>)

At $t=a$, the shifted support of $h(t-\tau)$ first touches the support of $x(\tau)$. This is the start of overlap, so it explains why $y(t)$ changes from zero to a nonzero piece after a.

$$
t=a:\quad \text{overlap begins}
$$

At $t=b$, the endpoint alignment changes inside the nonzero region. In the sketch, the supports are aligned at their maximum overlap, so after b the overlap no longer grows in the same way. This creates a new piece of $y(t)$.

$$
t=b:\quad \text{overlap changes from growing to shrinking}
$$

At $t=c$, the shifted support of $h(t-\tau)$ reaches the last contact with the support of $x(\tau)$. After that time there is no overlap, so $y(t)$ returns to zero.

$$
t=c:\quad \text{overlap ends}
$$

So the breakpoints are not arbitrary features of the formula. They come from support events in the convolution: overlap begins at a, the overlap pattern changes at b, and overlap ends at c.

## Explaining Output Shape from the Input and Impulse Response

**Example:** Plots of a finite-duration input $x(t)$, impulse response $h(t)$, and computed output $y(t)$ are shown. Explain why $y(t)$ first rises, then levels off, and finally falls.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shape of the computed output $y(t)$. To explain that shape, connect each interval of $y(t)$ to how $x(\tau)$ overlaps the shifted impulse response $h(t-\tau)$ as t moves.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

![](<../Source/7.4.7. Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output/Images/l007-s005-te-section-005.png>)

From the plot, the nonzero output is divided into three behavior intervals: $y(t)$ rises from a to b, stays level from b to c, and falls from c to d.

$$
a<t<b:\ \text{rising},\qquad b<t<c:\ \text{level},\qquad c<t<d:\ \text{falling}
$$

On the first interval, the shifted $h(t-\tau)$ is entering the support of $x(\tau)$. More of the two signals overlap as t increases, so the weighted overlap in the integral increases. That is why $y(t)$ rises.

$$
a<t<b:\quad \text{overlap increases}\Rightarrow y(t)\text{ rises}
$$

On the middle interval, the shorter pulse is fully inside the longer pulse. The amount of overlap stays the same, so the convolution output stays level instead of continuing to rise.

$$
b<t<c:\quad \text{overlap stays constant}\Rightarrow y(t)\text{ levels off}
$$

On the final nonzero interval, $h(t-\tau)$ is leaving the support of $x(\tau)$. The overlap shrinks as t increases, so the value of the integral decreases. That is why $y(t)$ falls back to zero.

$$
c<t<d:\quad \text{overlap decreases}\Rightarrow y(t)\text{ falls}
$$

So the output shape is not explained by $x(t)$ alone or $h(t)$ alone. It comes from their joint overlap inside the convolution integral.

## Summarizing the LTI System Response from the Piecewise Output

**Example:** A computed piecewise output $y(t)$ is shown for an LTI system with input $x(t)$ and impulse response $h(t)$. Summarize the system's time-domain output behavior.

**Explanation**

The plotted piecewise function is the system output $y(t)$. Since the system is LTI, this output is the convolution of the given input $x(t)$ with the impulse response $h(t)$.

$$
y(t)=(x*h)(t)
$$

![](<../Source/7.4.7. Interpreting Convolution as LTI System Output/Images/l007-s006-te-section-006.png>)

Start the summary by stating the support. The output is zero before the first breakpoint a, nonzero from a to d, and zero again after d.

$$
y(t)=0\text{ for }t<a\text{ and }t>d,\qquad y(t)\ne 0\text{ for }a<t<d
$$

Next, name the breakpoints that divide the response into time regions. Here, the relevant breakpoints are a, b, c, and d.

$$
a<b<c<d
$$

Now describe the behavior inside the nonzero interval. The output rises on the first nonzero interval, stays level in the middle interval, and falls on the final nonzero interval.

$$
a<t<b:\ \text{rises},\qquad b<t<c:\ \text{stays level},\qquad c<t<d:\ \text{falls}
$$

A concise response summary is: for this input and impulse response, the LTI system output is zero until a, turns on and rises from a to b, remains level from b to c, falls from c to d, and is zero after d.

The final phrase matters: this is the response to the given $x(t)$ through the system with impulse response $h(t)$, not a behavior of $x(t)$ alone, $h(t)$ alone, or the system independent of its input.
