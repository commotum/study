## Introduction to Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution

For a direct convolution, the output at one fixed time t is found by integrating over the horizontal variable $\tau$. The value of t stays fixed while $\tau$ moves across the overlap between $x(\tau)$ and $h(t - \tau)$.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

After the convolution has been split into time regions, each region has its own overlap interval. On one such region, the nonzero contribution comes only from the $\tau$-values where both factors are active.

$$
y(t)=\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

The geometric shortcut is to look at the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$, not either signal alone. The integral is the signed area under that product over the current overlap interval.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s001-te-section-001.png>)

In the simplest case, the product is constant over the whole overlap interval. Then the shaded product region is a rectangle: its width is the overlap length in $\tau$, and its height is the constant product value.

$$
\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)} C\,d\tau=C\bigl(b(t)-a(t)\bigr)
$$

This is the recurring move for the lesson: identify the product shape for the current time region, read its geometric dimensions, compute the area, and attach that area formula to the correct piece of $y(t)$. Later sections use the same idea for rectangles, triangles, trapezoids, composite regions, and final piecewise assembly.

## Finding a Constant-Height Overlap Area

**Example:** In the shown overlap region for $0 \le t \le 1$, the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$ is a rectangle with $\tau$-width t and constant height 3. Use geometry to find the convolution contribution for this region.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the shaded product region is a rectangle. For this time region, the convolution contribution is the area under the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$, so use rectangle area instead of setting up a new algebraic integral.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s002-te-section-002.png>)

Read the two dimensions from the product diagram: the $\tau$-width is t, and the constant product height is 3.

$$
\text{area}=(\text{width})(\text{height})=t\cdot 3
$$

Multiplying the width by the height gives the value of the regional convolution integral.

$$
\int_{\text{overlap}}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau=3t
$$

So the convolution contribution on the stated region is 3t for $0 \le t \le 1$.

## Finding a Triangular Overlap Area

**Example:** In the shown overlap region for $0 \le t \le 1$, the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$ is a right triangle with base t and height 2t. Use geometry to find the convolution contribution for this region.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the shaded product region is a triangle. The convolution contribution is still the area under the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$, but this time the product has one sloped boundary, so use triangle area instead of rectangle area.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s003-te-section-003.png>)

Read the two triangle dimensions from the product diagram: the base along the $\tau$ axis is t, and the maximum product height is 2t.

$$
\text{area}=\frac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height})
$$

Substitute the base and height from the diagram, then simplify.

$$
\frac{1}{2}(t)(2t)=t^2
$$

So the convolution contribution on the stated region is $t^2$ for $0 \le t \le 1$.

<a id="finding-a-trapezoidal-overlap-area"></a>
## Finding a Trapezoidal Overlap Area

**Example:** In the shown overlap region for $1 \le t \le 2$, the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$ is a trapezoid with $\tau$-width 1, left height 2, and right height t + 1. Use geometry to find the convolution contribution for this region.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the shaded product region is a trapezoid. The convolution contribution is still the area under $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$, but now the product has two nonzero parallel heights, so average the heights and multiply by the $\tau$-width.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s004-te-section-004.png>)

Read the trapezoid dimensions from the product diagram: the $\tau$-width is 1, the left height is 2, and the right height is t + 1.

$$
\text{area}=\frac{1}{2}(\text{left height}+\text{right height})(\text{width})
$$

Substitute the two heights and the width, then simplify.

$$
\frac{1}{2}\bigl(2+(t+1)\bigr)(1)=\frac{t+3}{2}
$$

So the convolution contribution on the stated region is (t + 3)/2 for $1 \le t \le 2$.

<a id="splitting-an-overlap-region-into-geometric-pieces"></a>
## Splitting an Overlap Region into Geometric Pieces

**Example:** In the shown overlap region for $1 \le t \le 2$, the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$ is split into a rectangle of width 1 and height 2 plus a right triangle of base t - 1 and height t - 1. Use geometry to find the convolution contribution for this region.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the shaded product region is not one standard shape. Split it along the marked line, compute the area of each simple piece, and add those areas to get the convolution contribution.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s005-te-section-005.png>)

Rectangle piece: the rectangle has width 1 and height 2, so its area is width times height.

$$
A_{\text{rect}}=(1)(2)=2
$$

Triangle piece: the right triangle has base t - 1 and height t - 1, so use one-half times base times height.

$$
A_{\text{tri}}=\frac{1}{2}(t-1)(t-1)=\frac{1}{2}(t-1)^2
$$

Add the component areas because the two pieces partition the same product region without overlapping interiors.

$$
A_{\text{total}}=2+\frac{1}{2}(t-1)^2=\frac{1}{2}t^2-t+\frac{5}{2}
$$

So the convolution contribution on the stated region is 2 + (1/2)(t - 1)^2, equivalently (1/2)$t^2$ - t + 5/2, for $1 \le t \le 2$.

<a id="assembling-the-piecewise-output-from-geometric-areas"></a>
## Assembling the Piecewise Output from Geometric Areas

**Example:** A convolution has been split into ordered time regions. The region summary gives $A_1(t)=3t$ on $0 \le t < 1$, $A_2(t)=t^2$ on $1 \le t < 2$, and $A_3(t)=4 - t$ on $2 \le t < 3$, with zero overlap for $t < 0$ and $t \ge 3$. Assemble the complete piecewise output $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the geometric area formulas have already been computed. This step is not about finding new areas; it is about placing each area formula on its matching time interval and adding zero outside the active support.

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Using Geometry for Piecewise Convolution/Images/l005-s006-te-section-006.png>)

List the regions in increasing order and keep the no-overlap regions. That gives two zero pieces and three active pieces.

$$
t<0,\quad 0\le t<1,\quad 1\le t<2,\quad 2\le t<3,\quad t\ge 3
$$

Now assign each computed area formula to the interval where it was produced. The exterior regions have no overlap, so $y(t)$ is zero there.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0,\\3t, & 0\le t<1,\\t^2, & 1\le t<2,\\4-t, & 2\le t<3,\\0, & t\ge 3.\end{cases}
$$

This piecewise expression is the full LTI system output for the summarized convolution because every ordered region is covered exactly once.
