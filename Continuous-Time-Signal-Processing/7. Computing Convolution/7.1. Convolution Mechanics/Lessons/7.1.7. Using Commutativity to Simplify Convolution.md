## Introduction to Using Commutativity to Simplify Convolution

In a convolution integral, the usual setup is to keep one factor fixed as a function of $\tau$ and shift the other factor as a function of $t-\tau$. Then the support of the fixed factor, the support of the shifted factor, and their overlap determine the bounds for the integral.

Convolution is commutative. That means swapping the two factors does not change the output value.

$$
(x*h)(t)=(h*x)(t)
$$

The useful point is that commutativity can change the work setup. In the first form below, $x(\tau)$ is the fixed factor and $h(t-\tau)$ is the shifted factor. In the second form, $h(\tau)$ is fixed and $x(t-\tau)$ is shifted.

$$
(x*h)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

Use the swapped form when it makes the support work simpler. For example, if one signal has a simple finite support, it is often easier to keep that signal fixed in $\tau$ and let the other signal be the shifted factor.

Swapping is optional. The result is the same either way, so the choice is about reducing the burden of solving inequalities, finding overlap cases, or evaluating the piecewise integral.

## Swapping the Factors in a Convolution Integral

**Example:** Rewrite $y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ using commutativity so that $h(\tau)$ is the fixed factor.

**Explanation**

In the given integral, $x(\tau)$ is the fixed factor because it is written directly as a function of $\tau$. The factor $h(t-\tau)$ is the shifted factor because its argument contains $t-\tau$.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

To make $h(\tau)$ the fixed factor, use commutativity and swap the roles of $x$ and $h$. The output is still $y(t)$, and $\tau$ stays the integration variable.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

This is the commuted form. The value of $y(t)$ is unchanged because convolution is commutative; only the setup has changed, with $h(\tau)$ fixed and $x(t-\tau)$ shifted.

## Choosing the Easier Shifted Signal

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$, decide whether to use $\int x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ or $\int h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ to make the overlap setup simpler.

**Explanation**

The cue is the support type of each factor. The signal $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ is one-sided, so its support starts at $0$ and extends to infinity. The signal $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$ is a finite rectangular pulse, so its support is the interval from $0$ to $2$.

$$
\operatorname{supp} x=[0,\infty),\qquad \operatorname{supp} h=[0,2]
$$

In the original order, $x(\tau)$ is fixed and $h(t-\tau)$ is shifted. That leaves the one-sided exponential as the fixed condition, but it makes the finite rectangular support move with $t$.

$$
\int x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau:\qquad \tau\ge 0,\quad t-2\le \tau\le t
$$

In the commuted order, $h(\tau)$ is fixed and $x(t-\tau)$ is shifted. Now the finite rectangular support stays as the simple fixed interval, and the shifted one-sided exponential contributes only a one-sided inequality.

$$
\int h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau:\qquad 0\le \tau\le 2,\quad \tau\le t
$$

Use the commuted form. It gives the same convolution value by commutativity, but it makes the overlap setup simpler because the finite interval remains fixed in $\tau$ while the one-sided exponential is the shifted factor.

<a id="setting-bounds-after-swapping-the-factors"></a>
## Setting Bounds After Swapping the Factors

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$, use the swapped form $\int h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ to set the integration bounds for $0<t<2$.

**Explanation**

Use the swapped order chosen in the previous section. In this order, $h(\tau)$ is the fixed factor and $x(t-\tau)$ is the shifted factor.

$$
\int h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

The rectangular pulse $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$ is nonzero from $0$ to $2$. Since it is fixed as $h(\tau)$, this gives the fixed support condition in the integration variable.

$$
0\le \tau\le 2
$$

The exponential factor is one-sided: $x(u)=e^{-u}u(u)$ is nonzero when $u\ge 0$. In the shifted factor $x(t-\tau)$, replace $u$ with $t-\tau$ and solve for $\tau$.

$$
t-\tau\ge 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau\le t
$$

Now intersect the fixed support with the shifted support condition. Before using the stated time region, the allowed $\tau$ values must satisfy both constraints.

$$
[0,2]\cap(-\infty,t]
$$

On the region $0<t<2$, the value of $t$ lies inside the fixed interval $[0,2]$. Therefore the overlap starts at $0$ and ends at $t$.

$$
0\le \tau\le t
$$

So for $0<t<2$, the lower integration bound is $0$ and the upper integration bound is $t$. This sets up the integral; evaluation comes after the bounds are known.

$$
y(t)=\int_0^t h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad 0<t<2
$$

## Evaluating the Simpler Piecewise Convolution

**Example:** Use commutativity to compute $y(t)=(x*h)(t)$ for $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$, giving the answer as a piecewise function of t.

**Explanation**

Use the commuted form so the rectangular pulse stays fixed in $\tau$. Since $h(\tau)$ is nonzero on $0\le \tau\le 2$ and $x(t-\tau)$ is nonzero when $t-\tau\ge 0$, the shifted exponential contributes the condition $\tau\le t$.

$$
y(t)=\int h(\tau)x(t-\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad 0\le \tau\le 2,\quad \tau\le t
$$

These two support conditions split the calculation into three regions. For $t<0$, the condition $\tau\le t$ lies entirely to the left of the fixed interval $[0,2]$, so there is no overlap.

$$
t<0:\qquad y(t)=0
$$

For $0\le t<2$, the overlap runs from $0$ up to $t$. On this interval, $h(\tau)=1$ and $x(t-\tau)=e^{-(t-\tau)}$, so the integral is evaluated with bounds $0$ to $t$.

$$
0\le t<2:\qquad y(t)=\int_0^t e^{-(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=e^{-t}\int_0^t e^\tau\,d\tau=1-e^{-t}
$$

For $t\ge 2$, the condition $\tau\le t$ includes the entire fixed interval $[0,2]$. The overlap is the full rectangular-pulse interval, so the bounds are $0$ to $2$.

$$
t\ge 2:\qquad y(t)=\int_0^2 e^{-(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=e^{-t}(e^2-1)=e^{2-t}-e^{-t}
$$

Assemble the three regional results into one piecewise output. The two nonzero formulas agree at $t=2$ because both give $1-e^{-2}$ there.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<0,\\1-e^{-t},&0\le t<2,\\e^{2-t}-e^{-t},&t\ge 2\end{cases}
$$
