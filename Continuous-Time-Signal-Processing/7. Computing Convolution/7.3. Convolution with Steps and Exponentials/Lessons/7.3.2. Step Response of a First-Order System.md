## Introduction to Step Response of a First-Order System

The step response of an LTI system is the output produced when the input is the unit step. In this lesson, compute that output directly by convolution: the step input turns the system on, and the impulse response determines the shape of the output.

$$
x(t)=u(t), \quad h(t)=a e^{-a t}u(t), \quad a>0
$$

This is the simplest normalized first-order case. The factor $u(t)$ makes the impulse response causal, and the factor $e^{-a t}$ makes it decay after the impulse. The step response is the convolution $y(t)=(u*h)(t)$.

$$
y(t)=(u*h)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(\tau)a e^{-a(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

The unit steps set the active part of the integral. The factor $u(\tau)$ requires $\tau \ge 0$, and the factor $u(t-\tau)$ requires $\tau \le t$. These two conditions overlap only when $t \ge 0$.

$$
t\ge 0:\quad y(t)=\int_0^t a e^{-a(t-\tau)}\,d\tau
$$

For $t \ge 0$, the integral accumulates more of the decaying impulse response as $t$ grows. So the output starts at 0 and rises toward its steady-state value instead of staying flat or decaying from 1.

$$
y(t)=(1-e^{-a t})u(t)
$$

Use this pattern for the rest of the lesson: set the causal overlap bounds, evaluate the exponential integral, attach the final unit-step gate, and interpret the result as a rising exponential step response.

## Setting Up the Unit-Step Input Convolution

**Example:** Set up the convolution integral for the step response of a first-order LTI system with input $x(t)=u(t)$ and impulse response $h(t)=2e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that this is a step response of an LTI system, so the output is the convolution of the unit-step input with the impulse response. Start from the convolution formula and keep $t$ fixed while $\tau$ is the integration variable.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

Now substitute the two functions into the integrand. The input becomes $x(\tau)=u(\tau)$, and the shifted impulse response keeps the shifted argument $t-\tau$.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(\tau)\,2e^{-2(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

The unit-step factors determine the bounds. The factor $u(\tau)$ is nonzero only when $\tau \ge 0$, and $u(t-\tau)$ is nonzero only when $\tau \le t$. There is no overlap when $t<0$; when $t\ge 0$, the overlap interval is $0\le \tau \le t$.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0,\\ \displaystyle \int_0^t 2e^{-2(t-\tau)}\,d\tau, & t\ge 0.\end{cases}
$$

This is the setup only. The integrand and bounds are now correct, and the exponential integral is left unevaluated for the next step.

## Evaluating the First-Order Step Response Integral

**Example:** Evaluate the step response for $x(t)=u(t)$ and $h(t)=2e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

Setup: the previous step already found the causal overlap interval. For $t<0$, there is no overlap, so the output is 0. For $t\ge 0$, evaluate the integral over $0\le \tau \le t$.

$$
t\ge 0:\quad y(t)=\int_0^t 2e^{-2(t-\tau)}\,d\tau
$$

Integration: rewrite the exponent so the dependence on the integration variable is clear. Since $t$ is fixed during the integration, $e^{-2t}$ can be treated as a constant factor.

$$
2e^{-2(t-\tau)}=2e^{-2t}e^{2\tau}
$$

Now substitute that form into the integral and integrate with respect to $\tau$ from 0 to $t$.

$$
\begin{aligned} y(t)&=\int_0^t 2e^{-2t}e^{2\tau}\,d\tau \\ &=2e^{-2t}\left[\frac{1}{2}e^{2\tau}\right]_0^t \\ &=e^{-2t}(e^{2t}-1) \\ &=1-e^{-2t}. \end{aligned}
$$

Causal form: the calculation gives the positive-time branch. Attach $u(t)$ to include the zero-output region for $t<0$ in the final step response.

$$
y(t)=(1-e^{-2t})u(t)
$$

The key move is to keep the causal bounds from the setup, pull out the fixed factor $e^{-2t}$, and simplify the evaluated integral into the rising-exponential form.

## Writing the Causal Step Response

**Example:** A first-order step-response calculation gives $y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t})$ for $t\ge 0$. Write the full causal response in unit-step form and piecewise form.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the calculation only gives the positive-time branch. Do not redo the convolution or change the expression; the task is to add the missing causal information.

$$
y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t}),\quad t\ge 0
$$

For compact causal notation, multiply the entire positive-time response by $u(t)$. The unit-step factor gates the whole expression, so the response is zero before the step turns on.

$$
y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t})u(t)
$$

The equivalent piecewise form states the same causal behavior explicitly: zero before $t=0$, and the given positive-time formula after the step turns on.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0,\\ 2(1-e^{-3t}), & t\ge 0.\end{cases}
$$

Both forms preserve the same positive-time step response. The unit-step form is compact, while the piecewise form makes the zero-output region visible.

## Interpreting the Rising Exponential Toward Steady State

**Example:** For the step response $y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t})u(t)$, identify the initial value, steady-state value, time constant, and qualitative behavior.

**Explanation**

The cue is the first-order step-response form $K(1-e^{-a t})u(t)$. For interpretation after the step turns on, use the positive-time branch and read the multiplier, exponential rate, and limiting behavior from that formula.

$$
t\ge 0:\quad y(t)=2(1-e^{-3t})
$$

First find the initial value just after the step turns on. Substitute $t=0^+$ into the positive-time expression.

$$
y(0^+)=2(1-e^0)=2(1-1)=0
$$

Next find the steady-state value by taking the limit as $t$ grows. The decaying exponential term goes to 0.

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} y(t)=2\left(1-\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-3t}\right)=2
$$

The time constant is the reciprocal of the exponential rate. Here the rate is 3, so the time constant is $1/3$.

$$
\text{time constant}=\frac{1}{3}
$$

So the response has initial value 0, steady-state value 2, and time constant $1/3$. Qualitatively, it rises exponentially toward 2 rather than decaying to 0.
