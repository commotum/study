## Introduction to Time Compression

When the signal is written as $y(t)=x(at)$ with $a>1$, read the factor $a$ as a horizontal compression. Every time feature at $t_0$ in $x(t)$ appears at $t_0/a$ in $y(t)$, and the amplitudes stay the same.

$$
y(t)=x(at)
$$

Start with the base case $y(t)=x(2t)$. Doubling the input time makes every feature happen at half its original time, so the waveform moves closer to $t=0$.

$$
y(t)=x(2t)
$$

The same rule works for any $a>1$: solve $at=t_0$ for the new time, which gives $t=t_0/a$. That is the location of every feature in the compressed waveform.

$$
t=t_0/a
$$

So the result is a compressed version of $x(t)$ squeezed toward $t=0$, with the horizontal spacing reduced and the amplitudes unchanged.

## Recognizing $x(at)$ as Time Compression for $a>1$

**Example:** Given $y(t)=x(4t)$, determine whether the signal is compressed or expanded and state what happens to the feature at $t_0=6$.

**Explanation**

Given $y(t)=x(4t)$, the factor $4>1$ means the signal is time-compressed toward $t=0$. The rule from the introduction applies: each time feature at $t_0$ moves to $t_0/4$ and keeps its amplitude.

$$
y(t)=x(4t)
$$

For the marked feature at $t_0=6$, solve the matching time equation $4t=6$.

$$
4t=6
$$

That gives $t=6/4=1.5$, so the feature appears earlier in time but has the same height.

$$
t=t_0/4
$$

So $x(4t)$ is a compressed version of $x(t)$, with every time coordinate divided by $4$ and every amplitude unchanged.

## Mapping a Single Time Feature Under Compression

**Example:** If $x(t)$ has a peak at $t=6$, where does that peak appear in $x(3t)$?

**Explanation**

The cue is the factor inside the argument: $x(3t)$ means $a=3>1$, so the feature moves toward $t=0$ by dividing its time coordinate by $3$.

$$
x(3t)
$$

For the peak at $t_0=6$ in $x(t)$, match the same feature by setting the input-time equation $3t=6$.

$$
3t=6
$$

Solving gives $t=2$, so the peak now appears at $t=2$ in $x(3t)$.

$$
t=2
$$

The peak height does not change; only its time location changes, so this is the same compression rule applied to one marked feature.

<a id="compressing-support-intervals-and-durations"></a>
## Compressing Support Intervals and Durations

**Example:** If $x(t)$ is nonzero on $[2,10]$, what is the support of $y(t)=x(2t)$?

**Explanation**

The cue is the support interval of $x(t)$ and the factor $2$ in $y(t)=x(2t)$. Because $a=2>1$, each support endpoint moves toward $t=0$ by dividing its time coordinate by $2$, and the amplitudes stay the same.

$$
y(t)=x(2t)
$$

Start from the original support $[2,10]$. Map the left endpoint to $2/2=1$ and the right endpoint to $10/2=5$.

$$
2/2=1,\qquad 10/2=5
$$

So the support of $y(t)$ is $[1,5]$. The duration changes from $10-2=8$ to $5-1=4$, which is half the original length, while the amplitudes stay unchanged.

$$
10-2=8,\qquad 5-1=4
$$

This is the same compression rule applied to an entire interval: the support squeezes toward $t=0$, and only the horizontal spacing changes.

## Reading a Compressed Waveform with Unchanged Amplitudes

**Example:** A waveform $x(t)$ has a peak at $t=4$ and is nonzero on $[1,7]$. Describe the waveform $y(t)=x(2t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the factor inside $y(t)=x(2t)$. Since $2>1$, read it as compression toward $t=0$: each time coordinate in $x(t)$ moves to half its original value, and the amplitudes stay the same.

$$
y(t)=x(2t)
$$

Apply that rule to the peak at $t=4$: solve $2t=4$, so the peak moves to $t=2$. Apply the same rule to the support endpoints $1$ and $7$: they move to $1/2$ and $7/2$.

$$
2t=4,\qquad t=2
$$

So the full compressed waveform has the same peak height as $x(t)$, but its nonzero interval is now $[1/2,7/2]$. Everything has moved inward by the same horizontal factor.

$$
1\mapsto 1/2,\qquad 7\mapsto 7/2
$$

In one sentence, $y(t)=x(2t)$ is the original waveform squeezed toward $t=0$ with unchanged amplitudes, a peak at $t=2$, and support $[1/2,7/2]$.
