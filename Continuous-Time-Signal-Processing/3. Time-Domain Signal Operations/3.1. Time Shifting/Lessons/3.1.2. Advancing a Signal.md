## Introduction to Advancing a Signal

Advancing a signal means moving the entire waveform left by $T$ units. The rule is simple: subtract $T$ from every time coordinate, but leave the waveform values, widths, and overall shape unchanged.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. Advancing a Signal/Images/l002-s001-te-section-001.png>)

In the simplest base case, a single labeled feature at $t_0$ moves to $t_0-T$. That is the whole idea of an advance: the feature stays the same, and only its time location changes.

$$
t_0\mapsto t_0-T
$$

The same left shift applies to every key time point, breakpoint, and endpoint in the graph. If a signal is described by a landmark time, the advanced signal uses the same landmark moved left by $T$.

$$
t_{\text{new}}=t_{\text{old}}-T
$$

So an interval $[a,b]$ becomes $[a-T,b-T]$. The important check is that only the time locations change; the amplitudes, widths, and ordering stay the same.

$$
[a,b]\mapsto[a-T,b-T]
$$

## Moving a Key Time Point Left by an Advance Amount

**Example:** Advance a signal with one labeled feature at $t=7$ by $3$ units and identify the new feature time.

**Explanation**

The cue is one labeled feature at $t=7$. For an advance, subtract the shift amount from the time coordinate and keep the feature's value unchanged.

$$
t_0-T=7-3=4
$$

So the new feature time is $t=4$. The feature stays the same; only its time position moves left by $3$ units.

## Advancing a Signal Graph Without Changing Its Shape

**Example:** Advance the plotted signal left by $2$ units and sketch the result on the same axes.

**Explanation**

The cue is a full signal graph with several visible landmarks. For an advance, treat the waveform as one rigid shape and move every time location left by $2$ units.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. Advancing a Signal/Images/l002-s003-te-section-003.png>)

Mark each endpoint, peak, corner, and flat region on the original graph. Then subtract $2$ from each time coordinate: a feature at $t=a$ moves to $t=a-2$, and a segment from $a$ to $b$ moves to $[a-2,b-2]$.

$$
t_{new}=t_{old}-2
$$

Now redraw the same heights and widths at the shifted times. The advanced graph keeps the same shape, the same values, and the same relative order of features; only the horizontal position changes.

## Advancing Breakpoints and Support Intervals

**Example:** A finite-duration signal is nonzero on $[1,6]$ with breakpoints at $t=2$ and $t=4$. Advance it by $3$ units and identify the new breakpoint times and support interval.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-duration signal described by its support interval and interior breakpoints. For an advance, subtract the same shift from every endpoint and breakpoint, then keep the piece order unchanged.

$$
[1,6]\mapsto[-2,3], 2\mapsto-1, 4\mapsto1
$$

So the new support interval is $[-2,3]$, and the new breakpoints are $t=-1$ and $t=1$. The original three subintervals $[1,2]$, $[2,4]$, and $[4,6]$ become $[-2,-1]$, $[-1,1]$, and $[1,3]$ after the shift.

That is the whole move: every boundary moves left by $3$, and the signal remains zero outside the shifted support interval.

## Recognizing the Advance Amount from an Original and Advanced Signal

**Example:** Use the original and advanced signal plots to determine the advance amount $T$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a pair of aligned plots showing the same waveform before and after a shift. To find $T$, match one visible landmark on the original signal with the same landmark on the advanced signal and read the leftward time difference.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. Advancing a Signal/Images/l002-s005-te-section-005.png>)

The peak moves from $t=5$ to $t=3$, so the leftward shift is $5-3=2$ units. That gives the advance amount directly.

$$
T = 5 - 3 = 2
$$

Check the support boundary too: $t=1$ moves to $t=-1$, which is the same $2$-unit difference. The advance amount is $T=2$, and the direction stays explicit: the signal moved left.
