## Introduction to Energy Signals vs. Power Signals

Classify a continuous-time signal by its total energy and its time-average power. Do not decide from the shape alone; decide from the two calculations.

$$
E_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt

P_x=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

These two quantities measure different ideas of size. $E_x$ accumulates the signal's squared magnitude over all time, while $P_x$ measures the long-run average squared magnitude. The cleanest base case is a finite-duration nonzero pulse: it has finite, nonzero energy and zero average power.

$$
0<E_x<\infty \Rightarrow \text{energy signal}

0<P_x<\infty \Rightarrow \text{power signal}

\text{otherwise} \Rightarrow \text{neither}
$$

Use that rule exactly as written: finite nonzero energy gives an energy signal, finite nonzero average power gives a power signal, and anything else is neither. The worked examples in this topic will test one finite-energy case, one finite-power case, and two neither cases.

## Classifying Signals with Finite Nonzero Energy

**Example:** Classify $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ as an energy signal, a power signal, or neither by computing total energy and average power.

**Explanation**

The cue is a one-sided decaying transient: $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ is nonzero for $t\ge 0$ and decays as $t$ increases. Use the energy and power definitions from the introduction, and keep the notation unchanged.

$$
E_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt=\frac12
$$

That result is finite and nonzero, so the signal meets the energy-signal criterion. The remaining check is the long-run average power.

$$
P_x=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_0^T e^{-2t}\,dt=0
$$

Since $0<E_x<\infty$ and $P_x=0$, this signal is an energy signal, not a power signal. The decaying transient has finite total energy even though it extends forever to the right.

<a id="classifying-signals-with-finite-nonzero-average-power"></a>
## Classifying Signals with Finite Nonzero Average Power

**Example:** Classify $x(t)=\cos(\omega_0 t)$ as an energy signal, a power signal, or neither by computing total energy and average power.

**Explanation**

The cue is a bounded periodic waveform: $x(t)=\cos(\omega_0 t)$ never turns off, so the right test is average power, not finite-duration energy. Use the definitions from the introduction and keep the notation unchanged.

$$
P_x=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\cos^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt=\frac12
$$

That value is finite and nonzero, so the signal satisfies the power-signal criterion. The remaining check is total energy.

$$
E_x=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_0^{N\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}}\cos^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\frac{\pi}{\omega_0}=\infty
$$

Since $0<P_x<\infty$ and $E_x=\infty$, this signal is a power signal, not an energy signal. A non-decaying periodic waveform can therefore have finite average power even though its total energy diverges.

<a id="classifying-signals-with-infinite-energy-and-infinite-average-power"></a>
## Classifying Signals with Infinite Energy and Infinite Average Power

**Example:** Classify $x(t)=t\,u(t)$ as an energy signal, a power signal, or neither by computing total energy and average power.

**Explanation**

The cue is a growing causal signal: $x(t)=t\,u(t)$ is zero for $t<0$ and then increases without bound. The shape alone is not enough, so compute both $E_x$ and $P_x$.

$$
E_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty t^2\,dt=\infty
$$

Since the energy integral diverges, the signal cannot be an energy signal. The remaining test is the long-run average power.

$$
P_x=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_0^T t^2\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{T^2}{6}=\infty
$$

Because $E_x=\infty$ and $P_x=\infty$, the signal is neither an energy signal nor a power signal. A growing signal can fail both criteria at once.

<a id="classifying-signals-with-infinite-energy-and-zero-average-power"></a>
## Classifying Signals with Infinite Energy and Zero Average Power

**Example:** Classify $x(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}u(t)$ as an energy signal, a power signal, or neither by computing total energy and average power.

**Explanation**

The cue is a slowly decaying causal tail: $x(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}u(t)$ is nonzero for $t\ge 0$ and shrinks slowly enough that the energy test is not obvious from the shape alone. Compute both $E_x$ and $P_x$.

$$
E_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{t+1}\,dt=\infty
$$

Since the energy integral diverges logarithmically, the signal cannot be an energy signal. The remaining test is the long-run average power.

$$
P_x=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_0^T \frac{1}{t+1}\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\ln(T+1)}{2T}=0
$$

Because $E_x=\infty$ and $P_x=0$, the signal is neither an energy signal nor a power signal. Slow decay is not enough to make the energy finite, and zero average power does not override infinite energy.
