## Introduction to Harmonics of a Fundamental Frequency

In a periodic continuous-time signal, the harmonic test is based on the fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0$. A sinusoidal component is a harmonic when its angular frequency fits the same spacing pattern as the fundamental frequency.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0
$$

Write the harmonic family as $\omega_n = n\omega_0$, where $n$ is a positive integer. The harmonic number is the integer $n$, not the amplitude, the phase, or the sign of the sinusoid.

$$
n = \frac{\omega_n}{\omega_0}
$$

The simplest case is $\omega = \omega_0$, which gives $\omega/\omega_0 = 1$ and makes the component the first harmonic. The next integer multiples, such as $2\omega_0$ and $3\omega_0$, give the second and third harmonics by the same test.

$$
\omega_1=\omega_0,\; \omega_2=2\omega_0,\; \omega_3=3\omega_0
$$

So the rule is simple: compute $\omega/\omega_0$. If the result is a positive integer, the component is a harmonic; if it is not, the component is not a harmonic. In the next examples, you will apply this integer-multiple test to single terms, numeric ratios, nonharmonic ratios, and multiple components.

## Identifying Integer-Multiple Angular Frequencies

**Example:** For the periodic signal $x(t)=5\cos(4\omega_0 t-\pi/3)$, identify the angular frequency and state which harmonic the sinusoid is.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient of $t$ inside the sinusoid. The amplitude $5$ and the phase $-\pi/3$ do not affect the harmonic label, so the only part to read is the angular-frequency factor multiplying $t$.

$$
\omega = 4\omega_0
$$

From the coefficient on $t$, the angular frequency is $4\omega_0$. To classify the component, divide by the fundamental angular frequency and check whether the ratio is a positive integer.

$$
\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}=\frac{4\omega_0}{\omega_0}=4
$$

Because the ratio is the positive integer $4$, the sinusoid is the fourth harmonic. The amplitude and phase are just clutter; they do not change the harmonic number.

## Finding the Harmonic Number of a Sinusoidal Component

**Example:** A periodic signal has fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0=2\pi$ rad/s and contains the component $4\cos(10\pi t+\pi/6)$. Determine the harmonic number of the component.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient of $t$ inside the sinusoid. The amplitude $4$ and the phase $\pi/6$ do not affect the harmonic number, so the only job is to compare the component angular frequency to $\omega_0$.

$$
\omega = 10\pi
$$

Here the fundamental angular frequency is $\omega_0=2\pi$. Form the ratio $\omega/\omega_0$ and simplify it before naming the harmonic.

$$
\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}=\frac{10\pi}{2\pi}=5
$$

Because the ratio is the positive integer $5$, the component is the fifth harmonic. The amplitude and phase are just clutter; they do not change the harmonic number.

## Recognizing Frequencies That Are Not Harmonics

**Example:** A periodic signal has fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0=4\pi$ rad/s and contains the component $3\sin(10\pi t-\pi/4)$. Decide whether the sinusoid is a harmonic.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient of $t$ inside the sinusoid. The amplitude $3$ and the phase $-\pi/4$ do not affect the classification, so the only job is to compare the component angular frequency to $\omega_0$.

$$
\omega = 10\pi,\qquad \omega_0 = 4\pi
$$

Form the ratio $\omega/\omega_0$ and simplify it. The ratio can be a fraction only if the component is not a harmonic, because the harmonic test requires a positive integer.

$$
\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}=\frac{10\pi}{4\pi}=\frac{5}{2}
$$

Because $5/2$ is not a positive integer, the component is not a harmonic. Even though the ratio is a neat fraction, it does not satisfy the integer-multiple test, and the amplitude and phase still do not matter.

<a id="classifying-multiple-sinusoidal-components-by-harmonic-number"></a>
## Classifying Multiple Sinusoidal Components by Harmonic Number

**Example:** For $x(t)=3\cos(2\pi t-\pi/5)+\sin(6\pi t)+4\cos(7\pi t)$ with fundamental angular frequency $2\pi$ rad/s, classify each sinusoidal component by harmonic number or as not a harmonic.

**Explanation**

Scan the terms one at a time. The amplitude and phase do not affect harmonic status; only the angular frequency in front of $t$ matters.

$$
x(t)=3\cos(2\pi t-\pi/5)+\sin(6\pi t)+4\cos(7\pi t),\qquad \omega_0=2\pi
$$

Read off each angular frequency and divide by $\omega_0$.

$$
\frac{2\pi}{2\pi}=1,\qquad \frac{6\pi}{2\pi}=3,\qquad \frac{7\pi}{2\pi}=\frac{7}{2}
$$

So $3\cos(2\pi t-\pi/5)$ is the first harmonic, $\sin(6\pi t)$ is the third harmonic, and $4\cos(7\pi t)$ is not a harmonic because $7/2$ is not a positive integer. The classification is term-by-term, not based on amplitude or phase.
