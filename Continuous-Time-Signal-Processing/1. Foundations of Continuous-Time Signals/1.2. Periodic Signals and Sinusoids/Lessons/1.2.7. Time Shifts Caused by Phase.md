## Introduction to Time Shifts Caused by Phase

A phase term inside a sinusoid is another way of saying the waveform happens earlier or later in time. Start from the zero-phase case $x(t)=\cos(\omega t)$, where there is no horizontal shift yet.

Now add phase to the argument. If the signal is $x(t)=\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, factor out $\omega$ so the phase becomes part of the time variable instead of staying as a separate angle.

$$
\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\cos\left(\omega\left(t+\frac{\phi}{\omega}\right)\right)
$$

That rewrite means a positive phase advances the signal by $\phi/\omega$ seconds. If you write shifts as $x(t-\tau)$, then the same positive phase corresponds to $\tau=-\phi/\omega$.

$$
\cos(\omega t-\phi)=\cos\left(\omega\left(t-\frac{\phi}{\omega}\right)\right)
$$

A negative phase does the opposite: it delays the signal by $\phi/\omega$ seconds, and in $x(t-\tau)$ notation that is $\tau=\phi/\omega$. The same conversion works for sine signals too, so later examples can switch between $\cos$ and $\sin$ without changing the rule.

The target move is always the same: identify $\omega$, divide the phase by $\omega$, and read the result as a time advance or delay with the correct sign.

<a id="rewriting-a-positive-phase-term-as-a-time-advance"></a>
## Rewriting a Positive Phase Term as a Time Advance

**Example:** Rewrite $x(t)=2\cos(8t+\pi/2)$ as a cosine with no explicit phase term and identify the time advance.

**Explanation**

The cue is the positive phase term in $x(t)=2\cos(8t+\pi/2)$. Keep the cosine and the amplitude $2$, and rewrite only the time argument.

Here the angular frequency is $\omega=8$ and the phase is $\phi=\pi/2$. The advance amount comes from dividing the phase by the angular frequency: $\Delta t=\phi/\omega=\pi/16$ seconds.

$$
8t+\pi/2=8\left(t+\pi/16\right)
$$

That rewrite shows the signal is the same cosine shifted left by $\pi/16$ seconds. In shift notation, this is a time advance, so the waveform appears earlier than $2\cos(8t)$.

$$
x(t)=2\cos\left(8\left(t+\pi/16\right)\right)
$$

So the final interpretation is a time advance of $\pi/16$ seconds, and the amplitude stays $2$. The same divide-by-$\omega$ rule is what later positive-phase examples will reuse.

## Rewriting a Negative Phase Term as a Time Delay

**Example:** Rewrite $x(t)=5\sin(6t-\pi/3)$ as a sine with no explicit phase term and identify the time delay.

**Explanation**

The cue is the negative phase term in $x(t)=5\sin(6t-\pi/3)$. Keep the amplitude and sine form fixed, and rewrite only the angle so it looks like $\omega(t-\Delta t)$.

Here $\omega=6$ and $\phi=-\pi/3$, so the delay magnitude is $\Delta t=|\phi|/\omega=(\pi/3)/6=\pi/18$ seconds.

$$
6t-\pi/3 = 6\left(t-\pi/18\right)
$$

$$
x(t)=5\sin\left(6\left(t-\pi/18\right)\right)
$$

A negative phase term becomes a right shift, so this signal is delayed by $\pi/18$ seconds. The sine follows the same phase-to-time rule as cosine: use the sign to decide advance or delay, then use $|\phi|/\omega$ for the amount.

## Computing Shift Size from Phase and Angular Frequency

**Example:** How far and in which direction is $x(t)=2\cos(5t+3)$ shifted relative to $2\cos(5t)$?

**Explanation**

The cue is the positive phase term in $x(t)=2\cos(5t+3)$. Keep the cosine and amplitude $2$ fixed, and rewrite only the angle so it looks like $\omega(t+\Delta t)$.

Here $\omega=5$ and $\phi=3$, so the shift size is $\Delta t=\phi/\omega=3/5$ seconds. The units work because radians divided by radians per second give seconds.

$$
5t+3=5\left(t+3/5\right)
$$

$$
x(t)=2\cos\left(5\left(t+3/5\right)\right)
$$

So the signal is advanced by $3/5$ seconds relative to $2\cos(5t)$. The same divide-by-$\omega$ rule used in the earlier phase examples still applies when the phase is a plain number instead of a multiple of $\pi$.

## Interpreting Phase-Caused Shifts in Sine Signals

**Example:** Interpret $x(t)=3\sin(7t-2)$ as a time shift and rewrite it without an explicit phase term.

**Explanation**

The cue is the negative phase term in $x(t)=3\sin(7t-2)$. Keep the sine form and amplitude $3$ fixed, and rewrite only the angle so it looks like $\omega(t-\Delta t)$.

Here $\omega=7$ and $\phi=-2$, so the delay magnitude is $\Delta t=|\phi|/\omega=2/7$ seconds. The units work because radians divided by radians per second give seconds.

$$
7t-2 = 7\left(t-2/7\right)
$$

$$
x(t)=3\sin\left(7\left(t-2/7\right)\right)
$$

So the signal is delayed by $2/7$ seconds relative to $3\sin(7t)$. The same phase-to-time rule used for cosine applies to sine as well: identify $\omega$, divide the phase by $\omega$, and read the sign as an advance or delay.
