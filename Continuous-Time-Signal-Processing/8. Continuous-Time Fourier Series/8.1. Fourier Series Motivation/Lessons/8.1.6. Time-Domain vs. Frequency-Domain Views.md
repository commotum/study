## Introduction to Time-Domain vs. Frequency-Domain Views

A periodic signal can be read in two equivalent ways: as a time-domain waveform and as a frequency-domain harmonic description. The signal does not change; only the representation changes.

In the time-domain view, you look at $x(t)$ as a waveform across one period. In the frequency-domain view, you list the DC term and the harmonic components at integer multiples of $\omega_0$.

Start with the simplest base case: one DC term and one fundamental harmonic. That base case lets you match the constant offset to the zero-frequency term and the oscillating part to the first harmonic without extra algebra.

$$
x(t)=a_0+a_1\cos(\omega_0 t)+b_1\sin(\omega_0 t)
$$

The shared landmarks are $a_0$, $\omega_0$, and $k\omega_0$: $a_0$ is the DC component, $\omega_0$ sets the spacing between harmonics, and $k\omega_0$ identifies the $k$th harmonic. The sine and cosine terms are the basis pieces that encode the same periodic signal in frequency form.

$$
x(t)=a_0+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big[a_k\cos(k\omega_0 t)+b_k\sin(k\omega_0 t)\big]
$$

Read the later sections as translations between these views. Each time you move from waveform to harmonic list, you are describing the same signal in a different language, not introducing a different signal.

## Reading DC and Fundamental Frequency Across Views

**Example:** A periodic signal has $x(t)=3+2\cos(\omega_0 t)$. Identify its DC term and fundamental angular frequency, then state how those show up in the frequency-domain description.

**Explanation**

The cue is the constant term in $x(t)=3+2\cos(\omega_0 t)$. A constant offset does not oscillate, so it is the DC term, and here $a_0=3$.

The oscillating part is the cosine at angular frequency $\omega_0$. That makes $\omega_0$ the fundamental angular frequency, the first harmonic spacing in the frequency-domain view.

Read the same signal as a frequency-domain description with a zero-frequency term and a harmonic at $\omega_0$. The constant part becomes the DC component, and the cosine part becomes the first harmonic.

So the frequency-domain side says: DC term $a_0=3$ and fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0$. The amplitude 2 is the weight on that fundamental cosine component.

This is the key translation: the waveform's baseline and first oscillation are not separate ideas, just the same signal read in two representations.

## Reading Harmonic Frequencies Across Views

**Example:** A periodic signal is given by $x(t)=1+4\cos(\omega_0 t)-2\sin(3\omega_0 t)$. Which harmonic frequencies appear in its frequency-domain description, and which term corresponds to each frequency?

**Explanation**

The cue is the frequency inside each sinusoid, not the size of its coefficient. The constant term $1$ is the DC component, so $a_0=1$.

The term $4\cos(\omega_0 t)$ is the first harmonic, because its angular frequency is $\omega_0$. The term $-2\sin(3\omega_0 t)$ is the third harmonic, because its angular frequency is $3\omega_0$.

$$
x(t)=1+4\cos(\omega_0 t)-2\sin(3\omega_0 t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \{0,\omega_0,3\omega_0\}
$$

So the frequency-domain description contains a zero-frequency term, a line at $\omega_0$, and a line at $3\omega_0$. The coefficient values 4 and -2 change the weights on those lines, not the harmonic locations.

There is no $2\omega_0$ line, because no term in the waveform uses that harmonic. Reading the frequency-domain view is just naming the frequencies actually present in the time-domain sum.

## Matching Sine and Cosine Basis Components Across Views

**Example:** A periodic signal is described by the frequency-domain coefficients $a_1=2$, $b_1=-1$, and $a_2=3$ with $a_0=0$. Write the equivalent time-domain Fourier-series form.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient label attached to each harmonic, not the waveform shape. Since $a_0=0$, there is no DC term. The coefficients $a_k$ belong to cosine terms and the coefficients $b_k$ belong to sine terms at the same harmonic index.

So $a_1=2$ becomes $2\cos(\omega_0 t)$, $b_1=-1$ becomes $-\sin(\omega_0 t)$, and $a_2=3$ becomes $3\cos(2\omega_0 t)$. The sign stays with the coefficient, and the harmonic index stays on the angle.

$$
a_0=0,\quad a_1=2,\quad b_1=-1,\quad a_2=3 \;\Longrightarrow\; x(t)=2\cos(\omega_0 t)-\sin(\omega_0 t)+3\cos(2\omega_0 t)
$$

The assembled time-domain form uses the same harmonic content in basis-function form: first harmonic cosine, first harmonic sine, and second harmonic cosine. Nothing changes except the representation.

This is the matching rule students should reuse: read the coefficient label, choose cosine or sine, place it at the correct $k\omega_0$, and preserve the sign. That reconstructs the time-domain Fourier-series expression directly from the frequency-domain coefficients.

<a id="translating-a-full-periodic-signal-description-across-views"></a>
## Translating a Full Periodic Signal Description Across Views

**Example:** A periodic signal is given by $x(t)=2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)-\sin(\omega_0 t)+4\cos(2\omega_0 t)$. State the matching frequency-domain harmonic description and then read the same signal back from that description.

**Explanation**

The cue is to read each coefficient together with its harmonic and basis type. In $x(t)=2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)-\sin(\omega_0 t)+4\cos(2\omega_0 t)$, the constant term is the DC component, the $\cos(\omega_0 t)$ and $\sin(\omega_0 t)$ terms belong to the first harmonic, and the $\cos(2\omega_0 t)$ term belongs to the second harmonic.

$$
x(t)=2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)-\sin(\omega_0 t)+4\cos(2\omega_0 t)\;\Longrightarrow\; a_0=2,\quad a_1=3,\quad b_1=-1,\quad a_2=4
$$

So the frequency-domain reading keeps the same information but names it by coefficient labels: DC is $a_0=2$, first-harmonic cosine is $a_1=3$, first-harmonic sine is $b_1=-1$, and second-harmonic cosine is $a_2=4$.

$$
a_0=2,\quad a_1=3,\quad b_1=-1,\quad a_2=4\;\Longrightarrow\; x(t)=2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)-\sin(\omega_0 t)+4\cos(2\omega_0 t)
$$

The result is a one-to-one translation: preserve the DC term, preserve the harmonic index, choose sine or cosine correctly, and keep the sign on the coefficient. That is the full cross-view match the student should imitate.
