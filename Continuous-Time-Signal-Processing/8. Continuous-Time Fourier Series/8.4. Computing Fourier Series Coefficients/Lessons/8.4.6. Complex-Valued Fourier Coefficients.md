## Introduction to Complex-Valued Fourier Coefficients

A complex Fourier series writes a periodic signal as $x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}$. The index $k$ labels the harmonic, and $\omega_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency.

$$
x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
$$

To find one coefficient, multiply by the matching exponential $e^{-jk\omega_0 t}$ and average over one period $T$. That one-period projection isolates the $k$th harmonic and gives its complex weight $c_k$.

$$
c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Start with a constant signal: only the $k=0$ term survives, so $c_0$ is the DC coefficient. Then look at a single cosine harmonic at the fundamental frequency; the same projection rule isolates that harmonic before any mixed signal adds extra bookkeeping.

You can read each coefficient as $c_k=|c_k|e^{j\angle c_k}$. The magnitude tells you how strong the harmonic is, and the angle tells you its phase shift.

$$
c_k=|c_k|e^{j\angle c_k}
$$

## Evaluating One Complex Coefficient Integral

**Example:** Given $x(t)=4\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)$, compute $c_1$ from $c_1=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\,dt$.

**Explanation**

The cue here is a single shifted cosine at the fundamental frequency, so the coefficient integral should isolate the matching $k=1$ harmonic. Rewrite the cosine in complex-exponential form first, then multiply by the matching exponential $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$.

$$
x(t)=\frac{4}{2}\left(e^{j(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)}+e^{-j(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)}\right)
$$

After multiplication by $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$, the $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ term becomes constant, while the other term becomes a $-2\omega_0$ exponential that averages to zero over one period. That is the orthogonality step that leaves only the matching harmonic behind.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1 &= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T 4\cos(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\,dt \\
&= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T 2\left(e^{j(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)}+e^{-j(\omega_0 t-\pi/3)}\right)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\,dt \\
&= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T 2\left(e^{-j\pi/3}+e^{-j(2\omega_0 t-\pi/3)}\right)\,dt \\
&= 2e^{-j\pi/3}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the integral isolates the $k=1$ harmonic and returns $c_1=2e^{-j\pi/3}$. That coefficient is the complex weight of the fundamental cosine term: its magnitude is $2$, and its angle is $-\pi/3$.

## Simplifying a Coefficient into Rectangular Form

**Example:** Starting from $c_1=2e^{-j\pi/3}$, simplify the coefficient into rectangular form.

**Explanation**

The only new wrinkle is the representation change. Use Euler's formula to rewrite the coefficient as a real part plus an imaginary part, then keep the magnitude $2$ outside the trigonometric terms.

$$
c_1=2\bigl(\cos(-\pi/3)+j\sin(-\pi/3)\bigr)
$$

Now substitute $\cos(-\pi/3)=\frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin(-\pi/3)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. That turns the exponential coefficient into a rectangular expression with the same harmonic weight.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1 &= 2\bigl(\cos(-\pi/3)+j\sin(-\pi/3)\bigr) \\
&= 2\left(\frac{1}{2}-j\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \\
&= 1-j\sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the coefficient in rectangular form is $c_1=1-j\sqrt{3}$. It is the same $k=1$ harmonic weight as before, just written as $a+jb$ instead of exponential form.

<a id="converting-a-complex-coefficient-to-magnitude-and-phase"></a>
## Converting a Complex Coefficient to Magnitude and Phase

**Example:** Given $c_1=1-j\sqrt{3}$, convert it to magnitude and phase.

**Explanation**

Start from the rectangular coefficient $c_1=1-j\sqrt{3}$. The task is to read off its magnitude and its angle, because that polar pair is the amplitude-and-phase weight of the $k=1$ harmonic.

$$
|c_1|=\sqrt{1^2+(-\sqrt{3})^2}=\sqrt{1+3}=2
$$

The real part is positive and the imaginary part is negative, so $c_1$ lies in quadrant IV. That means the phase is negative, with reference angle $\pi/3$ from the right triangle.

$$
\begin{aligned}\angle c_1&=-\pi/3\\c_1&=2e^{-j\pi/3}\end{aligned}
$$

So the same harmonic weight is now written as magnitude $2$ with phase $-\pi/3$. This is the form used when you want to see how strongly the harmonic appears and what phase shift it carries.

## Interpreting Positive and Negative Harmonic Weights

**Example:** For a real periodic signal with $c_2=1-j$ and $c_{-2}=1+j$, interpret what the positive and negative harmonic weights mean in the reconstruction.

**Explanation**

Because the signal is real, the coefficients at $+2$ and $-2$ must be conjugates. The cue is the pair, and the rule is that one real harmonic stores its amplitude-and-phase weight across both signs.

$$
\begin{aligned}c_2&=1-j=\sqrt{2}e^{-j\pi/4}\\c_{-2}&=1+j=\sqrt{2}e^{j\pi/4}=c_2^*\end{aligned}
$$

Use that pair inside the synthesis sum. The two complex exponentials combine into one real oscillation, so they are not two separate harmonics.

$$
\begin{aligned}c_2e^{j2\omega_0 t}+c_{-2}e^{-j2\omega_0 t}&=(1-j)e^{j2\omega_0 t}+(1+j)e^{-j2\omega_0 t}\\&=2\sqrt{2}\cos(2\omega_0 t-\pi/4)\end{aligned}
$$

So the positive-index coefficient carries the amplitude-phase information: magnitude $\sqrt{2}$ and phase $-\pi/4$. The negative-index coefficient is the conjugate partner that makes the reconstruction real, and together they represent one $k=2$ harmonic with amplitude $2\sqrt{2}$.
