## Introduction to Sine Coefficients

The sine coefficient $b_k$ measures how much of the $k$th sine basis function is present in a periodic continuous-time signal $x(t)$. To find it, project $x(t)$ onto $\sin(k\omega_0 t)$ over one full period from $t_0$ to $t_0+T$.

$$
b_k = \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(k\omega_0 t)\,dt
$$

Start with the simplest base case, $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t)$, so the learner can see what happens when the signal already contains the matching sine harmonic. In that case, the coefficient with $k=1$ should return the amplitude $A$.

$$
\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\sin(m\omega_0 t)\sin(k\omega_0 t)\,dt = 0 \quad \text{for } m \ne k
$$

That orthogonality is why cosine terms and nonmatching sine harmonics disappear in the projection integral. Only the matching sine term survives, so the coefficient isolates the contribution from the $k$th sine harmonic.

$$
\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\sin^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt = \frac{T}{2}
$$

So the rule to carry forward is simple: match the signal against the correct sine harmonic, integrate over one full period, and read $b_k$ as the size of that harmonic in the signal. The next examples will reuse this same projection step with different signals and harmonic indices.

## Computing One Sine Coefficient Over One Period

**Example:** Compute $b_1$ for $x(t)=2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)+5\sin(\omega_0 t)$ over one period.

**Explanation**

The cue is the matching sine factor $\sin(\omega_0 t)$ in the coefficient formula, so project the signal onto that harmonic and keep only the part that survives orthogonality.

$$
b_1 = \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt
$$

Substitute the signal and split the integral into the constant, cosine, and sine pieces. The constant term and the cosine term are nonmatching clutter, so they cancel when the signal is projected onto $\sin(\omega_0 t)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_1 &= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} \bigl(2+3\cos(\omega_0 t)+5\sin(\omega_0 t)\bigr)\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt \\
&= \frac{2}{T}\left[\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 2\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt + \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 3\cos(\omega_0 t)\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt + \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 5\sin^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt\right] \\
&= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 5\sin^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt
\end{aligned}
$$

Now use the matching-harmonic identity $\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\sin^2(\omega_0 t)\,dt=\frac{T}{2}$ over one period. Only the target sine term survives, so the coefficient returns the amplitude $5$.

$$
b_1 = \frac{2}{T}\cdot 5 \cdot \frac{T}{2} = 5
$$

The habit to reuse is simple: write the projection integral, drop the nonmatching terms for the correct orthogonality reason, and read the surviving sine amplitude as $b_1$. Later examples will repeat the same split-and-cancel procedure with different harmonic indices and signals.

## Computing a Sine Coefficient for a Higher Harmonic

**Example:** Compute $b_2$ for $x(t)=1+2\sin(\omega_0 t)-3\cos(2\omega_0 t)+4\sin(2\omega_0 t)$ over one period.

**Explanation**

The cue is the harmonic index inside the basis function: project the signal onto $\sin(2\omega_0 t)$ to isolate $b_2$. The goal is to keep only the part of $x(t)$ that matches that sine harmonic.

$$
b_2 = \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt
$$

Substitute the full signal and split the projection into four pieces. The DC term, the lower-harmonic sine term, and the cosine term all cancel for orthogonality reasons, so only the matching $2\omega_0$ sine term should remain.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_2 &= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} \bigl(1+2\sin(\omega_0 t)-3\cos(2\omega_0 t)+4\sin(2\omega_0 t)\bigr)\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt \\
&= \frac{2}{T}\left[\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 1\cdot\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt + \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 2\sin(\omega_0 t)\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt + \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} (-3\cos(2\omega_0 t))\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt + \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 4\sin^2(2\omega_0 t)\,dt\right] \\
&= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} 4\sin^2(2\omega_0 t)\,dt
\end{aligned}
$$

Now use the same one-period sine-square average as before: $\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\sin^2(2\omega_0 t)\,dt=\frac{T}{2}$. The matching harmonic survives with its amplitude intact, so the coefficient is the value on the $2\omega_0$ sine term.

$$
b_2 = \frac{2}{T}\cdot 4 \cdot \frac{T}{2} = 4
$$

The pattern is the same at a higher harmonic: write the projection onto the correct $\sin(k\omega_0 t)$, discard the nonmatching pieces by orthogonality, and read the surviving sine amplitude as $b_k$. The next examples will keep this same split-and-cancel method while changing what harmonic index is being tested.

<a id="recognizing-a-zero-sine-coefficient-from-cancellation"></a>
## Recognizing a Zero Sine Coefficient from Cancellation

**Example:** Recognize that $b_1$ is zero for the even periodic signal $x(t)=3+2\cos(\omega_0 t)+\cos(2\omega_0 t)$ over $[-T/2,T/2]$.

**Explanation**

The cue is symmetry. To find $b_1$, project $x(t)$ onto $\sin(\omega_0 t)$ over the symmetric interval $[-T/2,T/2]$. For an even signal, this is where cancellation can show up before any expansion.

$$
b_1 = \frac{2}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} x(t)\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt
$$

Here $x(t)=3+2\cos(\omega_0 t)+\cos(2\omega_0 t)$ is even, and $\sin(\omega_0 t)$ is odd. That makes the product $x(t)\sin(\omega_0 t)$ odd, so the positive and negative halves of the interval cancel.

$$
x(-t)\sin(-\omega_0 t)=x(t)\big(-\sin(\omega_0 t)\big)=-x(t)\sin(\omega_0 t)
$$

Therefore the integral is zero, so $b_1=0$. No term-by-term expansion is needed; the symmetry alone determines the coefficient.

## Computing Several Sine Coefficients for One Signal

**Example:** Compute $b_1$, $b_2$, and $b_3$ for $x(t)=2+6\cos(2\omega_0 t)+5\sin(\omega_0 t)-3\sin(2\omega_0 t)+4\sin(3\omega_0 t)$ over one period.

**Explanation**

The cue is to repeat the same projection at each harmonic and record the answers in order. Compute $b_1$, then $b_2$, then $b_3$ for the same signal.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_1 &= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(\omega_0 t)\,dt \\
b_2 &= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(2\omega_0 t)\,dt \\
b_3 &= \frac{2}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\sin(3\omega_0 t)\,dt
\end{aligned}
$$

In each integral, the DC term and the cosine term vanish by orthogonality. Only the matching sine term survives at each index: $5\sin(\omega_0 t)$ for $b_1$, $-3\sin(2\omega_0 t)$ for $b_2$, and $4\sin(3\omega_0 t)$ for $b_3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_1 &= 5 \\
b_2 &= -3 \\
b_3 &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

So the ordered coefficient list is $b_1=5$, $b_2=-3$, $b_3=4$. The pattern stays the same across the signal: project onto the right sine harmonic, drop the nonmatching terms, and read the result by index.
