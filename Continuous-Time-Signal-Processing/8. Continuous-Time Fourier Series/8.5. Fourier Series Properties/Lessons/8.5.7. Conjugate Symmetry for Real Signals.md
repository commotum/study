## Introduction to Conjugate Symmetry for Real Signals

For a real periodic signal, the complex Fourier series coefficients come in conjugate pairs. The negative-harmonic side is not independent: once the positive-harmonic coefficients are known, the matching negative-harmonic coefficients are determined by the real-signal condition.

$$
c_{-k}=c_k^*
$$

The smallest complete case is one harmonic pair such as $c_1$ and $c_{-1}$, together with the DC term $c_0$. If the signal is real, then $c_{-1}$ is the complex conjugate of $c_1$, and $c_0$ stays real, so the learner does not recompute the coefficient integral for the negative side.

In rectangular form, conjugation keeps the real part and flips the sign of the imaginary part. So if $c_k=a_k+j b_k$, then the matching negative-harmonic coefficient is $c_{-k}=a_k-j b_k$. That is the same rule written component by component.

$$
|c_{-k}|=|c_k|
$$

This is why the magnitude spectrum is even in $k$, while the phase spectrum is odd in $k$. For a real periodic signal, one side of the spectrum determines the other, and $c_0$ remains the real center term.

## Relating Positive and Negative Fourier Series Coefficients

**Example:** A real periodic signal has $c_2 = 3 - 4j$. Find $c_{-2}$ and confirm what the rule says about the pair.

**Explanation**

A real periodic signal uses the conjugate-symmetry rule from the previous section, so the negative-harmonic coefficient is determined by the positive-harmonic coefficient. The cue here is a real signal with a known positive-index coefficient, which means the matching negative-index coefficient is not independent.

$$
c_{-k}=c_k^*
$$

For the concrete pair in this example, the given coefficient is $c_2 = 3 - 4j$. The matching negative-harmonic coefficient is its complex conjugate, so we switch the sign of the imaginary part and keep the real part the same.

$$
c_{-2} = (3 - 4j)^* = 3 + 4j
$$

This pair has the same magnitude and opposite phase, which is exactly what conjugate symmetry predicts for a real periodic signal. If a DC term $c_0$ were present, it would remain real as well.

$$
|c_{-2}| = |c_2| = 5
$$

## Inferring Missing Negative-Harmonic Coefficients

**Example:** A coefficient table gives $c_0 = 4$, $c_1 = 2 - j$, and $c_3 = -1 + 2j$. Fill in the missing negative-harmonic coefficients.

**Explanation**

The cue is a real periodic signal with several known positive-harmonic coefficients. That means each missing negative-harmonic entry comes from mirroring the known positive side with conjugation, one index at a time.

$$
c_{-k}=c_k^*
$$

Start with $c_1 = 2 - j$. The matching negative coefficient is its complex conjugate, so the real part stays the same and the imaginary part changes sign.

$$
c_{-1}=(2-j)^*=2+j
$$

Do the same for $c_3 = -1 + 2j$. Apply the same rule again instead of inventing a new pattern.

$$
c_{-3}=(-1+2j)^*=-1-2j
$$

Keep $c_0 = 4$ unchanged because the DC coefficient of a real signal is real. The completed table now has consistent pairs across $\pm 1$ and $\pm 3$.

## Finding Even Magnitude Symmetry

**Example:** A real periodic signal has $c_2 = 3 - 4j$ and $c_{-2} = 3 + 4j$. Find $|c_2|$ and explain why the magnitude spectrum is even.

**Explanation**

The cue is a conjugate pair from a real signal. For magnitude, conjugates give the same absolute value, so the task is to compute one magnitude and reuse it on the negative side.

$$
|c_{-k}| = |c_k|
$$

For $c_2 = 3 - 4j$, take the absolute value of the complex number. The magnitude is $|c_2| = 5$.

$$
|c_2| = |3 - 4j| = 5
$$

Because $c_{-2} = 3 + 4j$ is the conjugate of $c_2$, its magnitude is the same. That is the even-magnitude pattern across $+2$ and $-2$. A DC term, if present, stays real and one-sided.

$$
|c_{-2}| = |c_2| = 5
$$

## Finding Odd Phase Symmetry

**Example:** A real periodic signal has $c_2 = 1 + j$. If the phase of $c_2$ is $45$ degrees, what is the phase of $c_{-2}$?

**Explanation**

The cue is a real-signal conjugate pair, but the question is asking for phase. For a conjugate pair, the negative-harmonic phase is the negative of the positive-harmonic phase, while the magnitude stays the same as a check.

$$
\phi_{-k} = -\phi_k
$$

Here the positive-harmonic phase is $45$ degrees, so flip the sign for the negative-harmonic coefficient.

$$
\phi_{-2} = -45^\circ
$$

The magnitude is unchanged, so the phase result is consistent with the same conjugate pair. That is the odd-phase symmetry across $+2$ and $-2$.

$$
|c_{-2}| = |c_2|
$$

## Using Conjugate Symmetry to Complete a Spectrum

**Example:** A real periodic signal gives $c_0 = 2$, $c_1 = 1 - j$, and $c_2 = -2j$. Which completed coefficient table and symmetry statement are correct?

**Explanation**

This is a spectrum-completion task. The cue is a real periodic signal with a partial coefficient table, so the missing negative-side entries come from conjugating each positive-side coefficient, and the magnitude and phase symmetries come from the same mirrored pairs.

$$
c_{-k}=c_k^*
$$

Mirror each given positive-harmonic coefficient: $c_1 = 1 - j$ becomes $c_{-1} = 1 + j$, and $c_2 = -2j$ becomes $c_{-2} = 2j$. Keep $c_0 = 2$ unchanged.

$$
c_0 = 2, c_{-1} = 1 + j, c_{-2} = 2j, c_1 = 1 - j, c_2 = -2j
$$

The completed spectrum shows the same magnitudes on each mirrored pair and opposite phase signs on each mirrored pair. That is the direct combination of even magnitude symmetry and odd phase symmetry.

$$
|c_{-1}| = |c_1|, |c_{-2}| = |c_2|
$$

$$
\phi_{-1} = -\phi_1, \phi_{-2} = -\phi_2
$$
