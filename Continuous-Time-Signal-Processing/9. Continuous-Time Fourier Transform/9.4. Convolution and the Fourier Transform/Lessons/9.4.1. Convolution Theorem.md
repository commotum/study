## Introduction to Convolution Theorem

When an LTI output is written as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$, the convolution theorem tells you how to read its Fourier-domain form: the output spectrum is the product $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

This is the same system described two ways. On the time-domain side, the input and impulse response combine by convolution; on the frequency-domain side, their spectra combine by multiplication. The theorem gives the matching between those two descriptions.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

So the operational rule is: if you know the LTI output in the form $x(t)*h(t)$, you can move to frequency analysis by taking the Fourier transform and replacing convolution with multiplication of spectra.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)*h(t)\}=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

In the next sections, you will use this notation to recognize each side separately and connect the theorem back to LTI output analysis.

## Recognizing the Time-Domain Convolution Side

**Example:** Given $y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$, identify the operation and rewrite it in compact convolution notation.

**Explanation**

The cue is the standard convolution pattern: one factor is written as $x(\tau)$, the other is shifted to $h(t-\tau)$, and the whole expression is integrated over $\tau$ to produce $y(t)$.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

That pattern is the time-domain side of the theorem, so the operation is convolution. In compact notation, rewrite the output as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

This is the notation the rest of the topic will reuse whenever an LTI output is being described in the time domain.

## Stating the Matching Fourier-Domain Product

**Example:** If $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$, write the Fourier-domain expression for the output spectrum.

**Explanation**

The cue is the time-domain output written in convolution form as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$. That tells you to apply the convolution theorem to the output, not to introduce a new operation.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

By the theorem, the Fourier-domain version of that same LTI output is the product of the input and impulse-response spectra. Keep the transformed output label on the left and the two spectra on the right.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

So the matching frequency-domain statement is $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$, which is the form the rest of the topic will use when it shifts from time-domain convolution to frequency-domain analysis.

<a id="interpreting-the-theorem-as-an-lti-output-relationship"></a>
## Interpreting the Theorem as an LTI Output Relationship

**Example:** For an LTI system with input $x(t)$ and impulse response $h(t)$, explain what the theorem says about the output in the time and frequency domains.

**Explanation**

The cue is one LTI system described by its input $x(t)$ and impulse response $h(t)$. The theorem says to describe that same output in both the time domain and the frequency domain.

$$
\begin{aligned} y(t)&=x(t)*h(t) \\ Y(j\omega)&=X(j\omega)H(j\omega) \end{aligned}
$$

Those two lines are not two different outputs. The first line is the time-domain view, where the output is obtained by convolution, and the second line is the frequency-domain view, where the output spectrum is the product of the input and impulse-response spectra.

So the theorem is an output relationship: use the time-domain form when direct output computation is the goal, and use the frequency-domain form when spectral analysis is the clearer way to read the same system.
