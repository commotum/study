## Introduction to Time-Domain Convolution vs. Frequency-Domain Multiplication

The same continuous-time LTI output can be written in two equivalent forms. In the time domain, it appears as the convolution of the input $x(t)$ with the impulse response $h(t)$. In the frequency domain, it appears as the product of their spectra. This step is only about recognizing which form makes the task easier.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

Use the time domain when the question is about overlap, support, shifting, or the geometry of the convolution integral. Those features are easiest to see before transforming anything.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

Use the frequency domain when the question is about algebraic combination, spectrum shaping, or the interpretation of the output spectrum. Multiplication is the direct operation in that representation.

The decision rule is simple: choose the domain that exposes the feature the question is asking about. For this lesson, the goal is to recognize the two equivalent forms and decide which one gives the clearer computation or interpretation.

## Matching $y(t)=x(t)*h(t) with Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$

**Example:** Match the time-domain output $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$ to its frequency-domain form $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$ and name which factors come from the input and impulse response.

**Explanation**

The cue is the output written as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$. That tells you the time-domain form is a convolution of the input and the impulse response, so the output is being described before any transformation.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

The matching frequency-domain form is $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$. Here the output spectrum is the product of the input spectrum and the impulse-response spectrum, so convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

So the symbols line up as $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, $h(t)\leftrightarrow H(j\omega)$, and $y(t)\leftrightarrow Y(j\omega)$. The same LTI output is being written in two different domains, and the frequency-domain version keeps the output as a simple product.

## Choosing Time-Domain Convolution for Overlap and Support Questions

**Example:** For finite-duration signals such as $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)$, decide whether the time domain is the clearest route and determine the support of $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is finite-duration pulses and a question about support and overlap. That makes this a time-domain signal-shape problem, so the first move is to slide one pulse across the other instead of trying to reason from spectra first.

$$
x(t)=u(t)-u(t-2),\qquad h(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)
$$

Here $x(t)$ is nonzero on $0 \le t < 2$ and $h(t)$ is nonzero on $0 \le t < 1$. When one pulse is shifted across the other, the output is nonzero exactly while their intervals overlap, so the support comes straight from the geometry of the two finite intervals.

$$
\operatorname{supp} y(t)=[0,3]
$$

So the time domain is the clearest route here. You do not need spectral algebra to answer the support question: the overlap starts at $t=0$ and ends after the combined pulse widths have passed, so $y(t)$ is supported on $0 \le t \le 3$.

## Choosing Frequency-Domain Multiplication for Algebraic Computation

**Example:** For spectra such as $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ and $H(j\omega)=\frac{2}{1-j\omega}$, decide whether the frequency domain is the clearer route and compute $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that both inputs are already given as algebraic spectra. That makes the frequency domain the clearest route, because the output spectrum is obtained by direct multiplication instead of by setting up a time-domain convolution integral.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}\cdot\frac{2}{1-j\omega}
$$

Now multiply the factors and simplify the denominator. The algebra stays compact, which is exactly why the frequency domain is the better choice for this kind of input.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{(1+j\omega)(1-j\omega)}=\frac{2}{1+\omega^2}
$$

So the result follows immediately from the product rule: $Y(j\omega)$ is just the simplified product of $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$. In this setting, time-domain convolution would add unnecessary work without making the answer clearer.

## Choosing the Clearer Domain for Interpreting an LTI Output

**Example:** An input $x(t)$ has several frequency components and $H(j\omega)$ strongly attenuates the high-frequency components. Decide which domain is clearer for interpreting the output and explain what the product $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$ says about $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is not a computation request but an interpretation request about filtering. That makes the frequency domain the clearest choice, because it shows directly which parts of the spectrum are passed and which parts are suppressed.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

Because $H(j\omega)$ is small at high $|\omega|$, the high-frequency components of $X(j\omega)$ are reduced in the product. The low-frequency parts survive more strongly, so the spectrum of the output is shaped by the filter rather than by a time-domain overlap picture.

$$
|H(j\omega)|\approx 1 \text{ for small } |\omega|,\qquad |H(j\omega)|\ll 1 \text{ for large } |\omega|
$$

So the frequency domain gives the clearest interpretation: $y(t)$ is the waveform whose spectrum keeps the low-frequency content and suppresses the high-frequency content. In this kind of question, the product rule explains the output more directly than time-domain convolution does.
