## Introduction to Using the Convolution Theorem to Avoid Direct Convolution

When an LTI output is written as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$, the direct route is a convolution integral in $\tau$, and that can be the expensive part of the calculation. The shortcut in this section is to move the work into the Fourier domain instead of expanding the integral by hand.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

The convolution theorem says the transform of the output is $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$. So the action to remember is simple: find the Fourier transforms of $x(t)$ and $h(t)$, multiply them to get $Y(j\omega)$, and treat that product as the frequency-domain version of the output.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

In the simplest base case, both $x(t)$ and $h(t)$ already match standard transform pairs, so the only new move is to use the theorem-driven workflow instead of direct convolution. That means you stay in one stable notation all the way through the calculation: transform first, multiply second, and then return to $y(t)$ by inverse transforming the result.

$$
y(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{Y(j\omega)\}
$$

Use this theorem whenever direct convolution is the bottleneck and the needed transform pairs are available. The result is a three-step shortcut you can reuse in later examples: transform the input and impulse response, multiply in frequency, and inverse-transform the product to recover $y(t)$.

## Translating a Convolution Output into $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$

**Example:** A system output is defined by $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$. Write the frequency-domain relation that lets you replace direct convolution with multiplication.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the output is already written as a convolution of the input and impulse response. For an LTI system, that is the signal to move into the Fourier domain and translate the convolution into a product.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

So the frequency-domain relation is written with the output spectrum $Y(j\omega)$ on the left and the two factor spectra on the right. The input becomes $X(j\omega)$, the impulse response becomes $H(j\omega)$, and the theorem tells you to multiply them.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

That is the translation to remember in this section: when the output is presented as a time-domain convolution, the matching frequency-domain statement is the product $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$. No extra algebra is needed yet; the task is only to identify the correct transform-domain form.

## Finding $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$ from Transform Pairs

**Example:** Given simple standard-pair signals $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, determine $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$ and set up the product for $Y(j\omega)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that both time-domain signals are one-sided decaying exponentials. That means you should not derive their Fourier transforms from scratch; you should match each signal to the standard transform pair for $e^{-at}u(t)$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{e^{-at}u(t)\}=\frac{1}{a+j\omega},\quad a>0
$$

Apply that pair separately to each signal. For $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, the parameter is $a=1$, so the input transform is $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$. For $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, the parameter is $a=2$, so the impulse-response transform is $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega},\qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

Now write the output spectrum as the product of those two transforms. The notation stays aligned with the theorem: $X(j\omega)$ comes from $x(t)$, $H(j\omega)$ comes from $h(t)$, and $Y(j\omega)$ is their product.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

That is the full move in this step: recognize the standard pair, identify each transform correctly, and set up the frequency-domain product without simplifying it yet. The next step can work with the product itself.

## Simplifying the Frequency-Domain Product $Y(j\omega)$

**Example:** Given $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ and $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$, simplify $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$ into a form ready for inverse transformation.

**Explanation**

The cue here is the product from the previous step. The job is algebraic cleanup, not a new theorem: multiply the known transforms and rewrite the result so it matches a familiar inverse-transform shape.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{(1+j\omega)(2+j\omega)}
$$

Because the denominator is a product of two first-order terms, split it into a sum of simple pieces. That puts each term in a standard form you can invert directly in the next step.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}-\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

This is the simplified frequency-domain form to keep: the same output spectrum, but now written as a sum of standard transform-pair terms. It is ready for the last time-domain step.

## Inverse-Transforming $Y(j\omega)$ to Recover $y(t)$

**Example:** Given the simplified frequency-domain output $Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}-\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$, use inverse-transform pairs to recover the time-domain signal $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the simplified spectrum from the previous step. The job now is recognition: match each term in $Y(j\omega)$ to a standard inverse-transform pair and then use linearity to return to the time domain.

$$
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{a+j\omega}\right\}=e^{-at}u(t),\quad a>0
$$

Each denominator already matches the pair with $a=1$ and $a=2$. Apply the inverse transform term by term, keeping the minus sign attached to the second term.

$$
y(t)=e^{-t}u(t)-e^{-2t}u(t)
$$

That is the recovered time-domain output. The frequency-domain expression has been converted back into a clean causal signal by matching the inverse pair and preserving the algebra exactly.

## Applying the Full Shortcut to an LTI System Output

**Example:** For a complete continuous-time LTI problem with $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, find the output $y(t)$ using the convolution theorem instead of evaluating the convolution integral.

**Explanation**

The cue is a complete continuous-time LTI output problem, so the shortcut should carry the whole workflow. Start by moving out of the time-domain convolution and into the Fourier domain, because the theorem lets you replace the integral with multiplication.

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}u(t), \qquad h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)
$$

Both signals match the standard one-sided exponential pair, so read off their spectra directly instead of deriving them from scratch. That gives the input transform and the impulse-response transform in the same notation used in the earlier steps.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}, \qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}, \qquad Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

Now multiply the factors and simplify the product into a form that is easy to invert. Because the denominator is a product of first-order terms, split it into simple inverse-pair terms rather than leaving it in unsimplified form.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{(1+j\omega)(2+j\omega)}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}-\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

The final step is to invert each term and keep the minus sign attached to the second component. This returns the time-domain output directly without ever evaluating the convolution integral.

$$
y(t)=e^{-t}u(t)-e^{-2t}u(t)
$$

That is the complete shortcut: transform the input and impulse response, multiply in frequency, simplify the product, and invert back to the output. The example shows the entire workflow in one pass so the same sequence can be reused on new LTI problems.
