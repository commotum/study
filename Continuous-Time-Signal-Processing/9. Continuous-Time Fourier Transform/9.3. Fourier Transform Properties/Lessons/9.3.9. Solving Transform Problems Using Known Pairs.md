## Introduction to Solving Transform Problems Using Known Pairs

The solve order in this lesson is simple: identify the closest known pair, rewrite the signal so every modification is visible, and then apply the property that matches each visible change before combining the pieces. The goal is not to start from the transform integral; it is to reuse stored pairs and the properties attached to them.

$$
x_0(t) \leftrightarrow X_0(j\omega)
$$

Start with the clean base case. If the signal already matches a known pair, you can read the spectrum directly. If a constant multiplies that pair, carry the constant through by linearity.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{a\,x_0(t)\}=a\,X_0(j\omega)
$$

The same idea extends to sums. Rewrite the signal term by term, transform each known-pair piece, and add the results.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\sum_k a_k x_k(t)\right\}=\sum_k a_k X_k(j\omega)
$$

For shifts, scaling, reversal, modulation, and differentiation, do the same thing in a slightly different order: first rewrite the signal so the modification is visible, then apply the matching property to that rewritten term. The rewrite tells you which factor or spectral change belongs to each piece.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x_0(t-t_0)\}=e^{-j\omega t_0}X_0(j\omega)
$$

That is the pattern you will reuse in the next steps: one known pair, then one small rewrite, then the property that matches it, and finally the combined spectrum.

## Identifying the Base Fourier Transform Pair

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=4e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the decaying exponential multiplied by $u(t)$, which matches the stored transform pair for $e^{-at}u(t)$. Start by recognizing that base pair, because the only new feature in this example is the constant weight 4 in front.

$$
e^{-at}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{a+j\omega}
$$

Substitute $a=2$ to get the transform of the base signal $e^{-2t}u(t)$. Then carry the outer factor 4 through by linearity; the scaling does not change the transform shape.

$$
X(j\omega)=4\left(\frac{1}{2+j\omega}\right)=\frac{4}{2+j\omega}
$$

So the final spectrum is $X(j\omega)=\frac{4}{2+j\omega}$. This is the model for the next examples: match the known pair first, then carry any visible modification through the appropriate property.

<a id="decomposing-a-signal-into-known-pair-terms"></a>
## Decomposing a Signal into Known-Pair Terms

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)+2e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a sum of two decaying exponentials multiplied by $u(t)$, and each term already matches the known pair $e^{-at}u(t)$. The move here is to split the signal into terms before doing any transform work.

$$
e^{-at}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{a+j\omega}
$$

Write the signal as two separate known-pair terms: $e^{-t}u(t)$ and $2e^{-2t}u(t)$. Then transform each term on its own and carry the coefficient on the second term through by linearity.

$$
\begin{aligned}\mathcal{F}\{e^{-t}u(t)\} &= \frac{1}{1+j\omega} \\ \mathcal{F}\{2e^{-2t}u(t)\} &= \frac{2}{2+j\omega}\end{aligned}
$$

Add the two spectra to get the final result. The important step is the decomposition: once the sum has been split into known-pair pieces, the transform is just the sum of the individual transforms.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}+\frac{2}{2+j\omega}
$$

## Selecting the Property for Each Signal Modification

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=3e^{-2(t-1)}u(t-1)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a delayed copy of the same one-sided exponential pair. Rewrite the signal as $3x_0(t-1)$ so the delay and the scale factor are both visible before you transform anything.

$$
x_0(t)=e^{-2t}u(t) \leftrightarrow X_0(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

With the signal written as $3x_0(t-1)$, the property choice is automatic: apply linearity for the factor 3 and time shifting for the delay of 1. The delay contributes the phase factor $e^{-j\omega}$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{3x_0(t-1)\}=3e^{-j\omega}X_0(j\omega)
$$

Substitute the base spectrum into that shifted form and simplify. The result is the original rational spectrum multiplied by the phase factor from the delay.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{3e^{-j\omega}}{2+j\omega}
$$

## Combining Multiple Properties on a Modified Known Pair

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=2e^{-(t-1)}u(t-1)\cos(3t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a known exponential pair that has been delayed and then multiplied by a cosine. Rewrite the signal as $2x_0(t-1)\cos(3t)$ so the two modifications are visible before you transform anything.

$$
x_0(t)=e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow X_0(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

First apply the delay to the base pair: $x_0(t-1)$ has spectrum $e^{-j\omega}X_0(j\omega)$. Then apply cosine modulation, which turns multiplication by $\cos(3t)$ into two frequency shifts. The outer factor 2 cancels the $1/2$ from the cosine rule.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{2x_0(t-1)\cos(3t)\}=e^{-j(\omega-3)}X_0(j(\omega-3))+e^{-j(\omega+3)}X_0(j(\omega+3))
$$

Now substitute $X_0(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ into each shifted copy. Keep the two terms separate so the delay phase and the modulation shift stay attached to the correct copy.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{e^{-j(\omega-3)}}{1+j(\omega-3)}+\frac{e^{-j(\omega+3)}}{1+j(\omega+3)}
$$

## Assembling the Final Spectrum from Transformed Pieces

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=2e^{-(t-1)}u(t-1)\cos(3t)+e^{-t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a sum of two known-pair pieces: one composite term from the previous example and one plain exponential. Rewrite the signal as $x(t)=x_1(t)+x_2(t)$ so you can transform each piece separately and only add the spectra at the end.

$$
x_1(t)=2e^{-(t-1)}u(t-1)\cos(3t), \qquad x_2(t)=e^{-t}u(t)
$$

Use the previous chain for $x_1(t)$: its spectrum is the sum of the two shifted copies from the delayed cosine-modulated exponential. For $x_2(t)$, direct lookup gives $e^{-t}u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$. Then add the results.

$$
X_1(j\omega)=\frac{e^{-j(\omega-3)}}{1+j(\omega-3)}+\frac{e^{-j(\omega+3)}}{1+j(\omega+3)}, \qquad X_2(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

Now assemble the full spectrum by linearity. Nothing new happens in this step: the final answer is just the transformed composite piece plus the transformed simple piece.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{e^{-j(\omega-3)}}{1+j(\omega-3)}+\frac{e^{-j(\omega+3)}}{1+j(\omega+3)}+\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$
