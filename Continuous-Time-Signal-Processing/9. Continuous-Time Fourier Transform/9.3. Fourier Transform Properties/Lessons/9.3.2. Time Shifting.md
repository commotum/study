## Introduction to Time Shifting

A time shift means you should reuse a known Fourier pair instead of recomputing the transform from scratch. Start from a pair like $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, then read the shift sign in the argument to decide which factor to attach.

$$
x(t-t_0) \leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)
$$

A delay by $t_0$ keeps the original spectrum and multiplies it by $e^{-j\omega t_0}$. The only change is the shift factor; the base transform pair stays the same.

$$
x(t+t_0) \leftrightarrow e^{+j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)
$$

An advance uses the opposite sign in the exponential. Read the sign inside the parentheses first, then match it to the sign in the Fourier factor.

$$
X_{\text{shifted}}(j\omega)=e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)
$$

So the working rule is simple: identify the shift, keep the known spectrum, and attach the correct exponential factor. That is the form you will reuse in the worked examples.

## Transforming a Delayed Signal from a Known Pair

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, find the Fourier transform of $x(t-2)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a delayed copy of a known pair. Start from $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and use the delay rule from the tutorial.

$$
x(t-t_0) \leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)
$$

Here the delay is $2$, so replace $t_0$ with $2$ and keep the base spectrum unchanged.

$$
x(t-2) \leftrightarrow e^{-j2\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

So the transform of the delayed signal is the original spectrum multiplied by the delay factor $e^{-j2\omega}$. No new transform integral is needed.

## Transforming an Advanced Signal from a Known Pair

**Example:** Given $e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}$, find the Fourier transform of $e^{-(t-3)}u(t-3)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a delayed causal exponential. Start from the known pair $e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ and recognize the shift in the time argument.

$$
e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

The given signal is the same base signal delayed by $3$, so the time-shift rule attaches $e^{-j3\omega}$ to the original spectrum.

$$
e^{-(t-3)}u(t-3) \leftrightarrow e^{-j3\omega}\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

So the transformed signal keeps the denominator $1+j\omega$ and only picks up the delay factor. The result comes from recognition plus the shift rule, not from a new transform integral.

## Choosing the Sign in the Time-Shift Factor

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, find the Fourier transform of $x(t+4)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the plus sign in the time argument. $x(t+4)$ is an advance, so rewrite it as $x(t-(-4))$ and read the shift amount as $t_0=-4$.

$$
x(t-t_0) \leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega), \quad t_0=-4
$$

Substitute the negative shift into the rule. That turns the factor into $e^{-j\omega(-4)}=e^{+j4\omega}$, so the sign flips because the signal moved left in time.

$$
x(t+4) \leftrightarrow e^{+j4\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

So the correct factor for an advance is the positive exponent. The output keeps the base spectrum $X(j\omega)$ and only changes the sign in the shift factor.

<a id="applying-time-shifting-to-a-shifted-sum-of-known-pairs"></a>
## Applying Time Shifting to a Shifted Sum of Known Pairs

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, find the Fourier transform of $3x(t-1)-2x(t+2)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a weighted sum of two shifted copies of the same base signal. Use linearity first, then apply the time-shift rule to each term on its own.

$$
3x(t-1)-2x(t+2) \leftrightarrow 3\mathcal{F}\{x(t-1)\}-2\mathcal{F}\{x(t+2)\}
$$

The first term is a delay of $1$, so it picks up $e^{-j\omega}$. The second term is an advance of $2$, so it picks up $e^{+j2\omega}$; the coefficient $-2$ stays attached to that term.

$$
3x(t-1) \leftrightarrow 3e^{-j\omega}X(j\omega), \quad -2x(t+2) \leftrightarrow -2e^{+j2\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

Combine the transformed pieces after the fact. Both terms share the same base spectrum $X(j\omega)$, so the final answer is just the sum of the two shifted contributions.

$$
G(j\omega)=\left(3e^{-j\omega}-2e^{+j2\omega}\right)X(j\omega)
$$
