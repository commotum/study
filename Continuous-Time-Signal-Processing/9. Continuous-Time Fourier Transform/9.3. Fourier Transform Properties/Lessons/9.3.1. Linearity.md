## Introduction to Linearity

Linearity matters because it lets you reuse known Fourier pairs instead of recomputing the transform integral for every new signal. If the input is built from pieces whose transforms are already known, the job is to transform each piece and keep its coefficient attached.

In the base case, a single scalar multiple stays scalar after transformation. If $x(t)$ has spectrum $X(j\omega)$, then multiplying the signal by $a$ multiplies the spectrum by the same $a$; the shape of the spectrum does not change.

$$
a x(t) \leftrightarrow a X(j\omega)
$$

The same rule also works term by term for a sum of two known signals. Start with unit weights so the structure is easy to see: $x_1(t)$ contributes $X_1(j\omega)$ and $x_2(t)$ contributes $X_2(j\omega)$, and the total spectrum is their sum.

Once weights appear, do not move them or combine them early. Each coefficient stays attached to its own time-domain term and therefore to its own frequency-domain term, so $a$ stays with $X_1(j\omega)$ and $b$ stays with $X_2(j\omega)$.

$$
a x_1(t)+b x_2(t) \leftrightarrow a X_1(j\omega)+b X_2(j\omega)
$$

So the general pattern is simple: transform each known component, preserve every coefficient, and add the resulting spectra. That is the linearity step the later examples in this lesson will reuse, whether there are two terms or many.

## Transforming a Scaled Signal

**Example:** Given that $x(t)$ has CTFT $X(j\omega)$, find the CTFT of $3x(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$. Because the CTFT is linear, multiplying the signal by a constant multiplies the spectrum by the same constant.

$$
\begin{aligned}x(t) &\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)\\3x(t) &\leftrightarrow 3X(j\omega)\end{aligned}
$$

So the CTFT of $3x(t)$ is $3X(j\omega)$. The coefficient stays attached to the spectrum term, and nothing else in the Fourier pair changes.

## Transforming a Sum of Two Signals

**Example:** If $x_1(t)$ has CTFT $X_1(j\omega)$ and $x_2(t)$ has CTFT $X_2(j\omega)$, find the CTFT of $x_1(t)+x_2(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that each summand already has a known CTFT pair. Linearity says you can transform the two terms separately and then add the spectra, keeping the notation unchanged.

$$
\begin{aligned}x_1(t) &\leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)\\x_2(t) &\leftrightarrow X_2(j\omega)\\x_1(t)+x_2(t) &\leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)+X_2(j\omega)\end{aligned}
$$

So the CTFT of $x_1(t)+x_2(t)$ is $X_1(j\omega)+X_2(j\omega)$. The only operation is term-by-term addition of the known spectra; no new CTFT property is needed.

## Transforming a Weighted Sum of Two Signals

**Example:** If $x_1(t)$ has CTFT $X_1(j\omega)$ and $x_2(t)$ has CTFT $X_2(j\omega)$, find the CTFT of $2x_1(t)-3x_2(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a two-term sum with weights already in front of each signal. Use linearity term by term: keep the $2$ with the first signal and keep the $-3$ with the second signal.

$$
\begin{aligned}x_1(t) &\leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)\\x_2(t) &\leftrightarrow X_2(j\omega)\\2x_1(t)-3x_2(t) &\leftrightarrow 2X_1(j\omega)-3X_2(j\omega)\end{aligned}
$$

So the CTFT of $2x_1(t)-3x_2(t)$ is $2X_1(j\omega)-3X_2(j\omega)$. The coefficients and the sign stay attached to their own spectrum terms.

## Transforming a Multi-Term Weighted Sum

**Example:** If $x_1(t) \leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)$, $x_2(t) \leftrightarrow X_2(j\omega)$, and $x_3(t) \leftrightarrow X_3(j\omega)$, find the CTFT of $x_1(t)-2x_2(t)+4x_3(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a three-term weighted sum. Use linearity term by term: keep each coefficient attached to its signal as you move into the CTFT domain.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1(t) &\leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)\\
x_2(t) &\leftrightarrow X_2(j\omega)\\
x_3(t) &\leftrightarrow X_3(j\omega)\\
x_1(t)-2x_2(t)+4x_3(t) &\leftrightarrow X_1(j\omega)-2X_2(j\omega)+4X_3(j\omega)
\end{aligned}
$$

The final spectrum is the same weighted sum of the individual spectra: $X_1(j\omega)-2X_2(j\omega)+4X_3(j\omega)$. Nothing changes except that each time-domain term becomes its known transform.
