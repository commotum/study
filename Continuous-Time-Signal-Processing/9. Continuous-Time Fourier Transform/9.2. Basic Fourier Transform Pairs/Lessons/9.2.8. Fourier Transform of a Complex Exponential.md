## Introduction to Fourier Transform of a Complex Exponential

A pure complex exponential $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ is a single rotating frequency component, not a mixture of frequencies. In the Fourier transform, that means its content is concentrated at one angular frequency instead of spread across the spectrum.

$$
e^{j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)
$$

Read that pair as: the time signal $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ becomes a single impulse at $\omega=\omega_0$. The transform is not a broad curve; it is one spectral line located exactly where the exponential rotates.

$$
e^{-j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega_0)
$$

So the sign in the exponent controls which side of zero the impulse sits on: positive $\omega_0$ places the impulse to the right of zero frequency, and negative $\omega_0$ places it to the left. The examples that follow will reuse this same pair first at concrete positive and negative frequencies, and then with a scale factor.

## Converting e^(j $\omega$_0 t) to a Shifted Frequency Impulse

**Example:** Given $x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t}$, find $X(j\omega)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t}$ is a pure complex exponential, so it contains one angular frequency only. That means its Fourier transform is not spread across many frequencies; it collapses to a single shifted impulse.

$$
e^{j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)
$$

Use that standard pair directly: a tone at $\omega_0$ becomes one impulse centered at the same angular frequency. There is no extra algebra here beyond recognizing the signal as the single-frequency case from the introduction.

$$
x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t} \;\Longrightarrow\; X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)
$$

The shift stays inside the impulse argument as $\omega-\omega_0$, so the spectral line is displaced from zero by exactly $\omega_0$. The $2\pi$ factor stays unchanged, and this is the form the later examples will specialize with concrete frequencies and scaling.

## Placing the Spectral Impulse at a Positive Frequency

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=e^{j4t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $x(t)=e^{j4t}$ is still a pure complex exponential, but now the angular frequency is the positive number $4$. So the transform should be the same single-impulse pattern, just centered at a positive frequency.

$$
e^{j4t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-4)
$$

Read the result directly: the spectral impulse sits at $\omega=4$, which is to the right of zero frequency. Nothing about the transform pair changes except the concrete value of $\omega_0$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j4t} \;\Longrightarrow\; X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega-4)
$$

The substitution is the whole move: replace $\omega_0$ by $4$, keep the $2\pi$ factor, and keep the impulse shift as $\omega-4$. That is the positive-frequency case the later examples will contrast with a negative frequency and with a scale factor.

## Placing the Spectral Impulse at a Negative Frequency

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=e^{-j5t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $x(t)=e^{-j5t}$ is still a pure complex exponential, but now the angular frequency is negative. So the transform is still a single spectral impulse; it just moves to the left of zero frequency.

$$
\omega_0=-5 \;\Longrightarrow\; X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)
$$

Substitute $\omega_0=-5$ into the shift rule. That gives $\delta(\omega-(-5))$, which simplifies to $\delta(\omega+5)$.

$$
x(t)=e^{-j5t} \;\Longrightarrow\; X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega+5)
$$

The result confirms the negative-frequency placement: the impulse sits at $\omega=-5$, not at a positive frequency. The only change from the positive-frequency case is the sign inside the $\delta$.

## Transforming a Scaled Complex Exponential

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $x(t)=3e^{j2t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $x(t)=3e^{j2t}$ is the same single-frequency complex exponential as before, but with a scalar factor of $3$ in front. That means the impulse stays at the same frequency and only its weight changes.

$$
e^{j2t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-2), \qquad 3x(t) \leftrightarrow 3X(j\omega)
$$

Apply linearity to carry the factor of $3$ through the transform pair. The frequency location does not move, so the impulse remains at $\omega=2$.

$$
x(t)=3e^{j2t} \;\Longrightarrow\; X(j\omega)=3\cdot 2\pi\delta(\omega-2)=6\pi\delta(\omega-2)
$$

The result shows the only effect of the scale factor: it multiplies the impulse amplitude from $2\pi$ to $6\pi$. The spectral impulse is still centered at $\omega=2$.
