## Introduction to Fourier Transform of a Cosine

When the signal is $\cos(\omega_0 t)$, the frequency-domain target is not one spike but a symmetric pair of spikes at $\omega=\pm\omega_0$. The move is to rewrite the cosine as the average of two opposite-frequency complex exponentials, then transform those exponentials one at a time.

Start from Euler's formula and keep the two frequencies visible. The cosine is the half-sum of $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ and $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$, so each term can be matched directly to the standard exponential transform pair.

$$
\cos(\omega_0 t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j\omega_0 t}+e^{-j\omega_0 t}\right)
$$

Apply the known pair $e^{j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)$ to the positive-frequency term. After the outer $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is carried through, that term contributes an impulse at $\omega=\omega_0$ with weight $\pi$.

Apply the same pair $e^{-j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega_0)$ to the negative-frequency term. It contributes the matching impulse at $\omega=-\omega_0$, again with weight $\pi$ after the half-factor is included.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{\cos(\omega_0 t)\}=\pi\big[\delta(\omega-\omega_0)+\delta(\omega+\omega_0)\big]
$$

Putting the two impulses together gives the canonical cosine transform pair: two equal spikes, one positive-frequency and one negative-frequency, with the same magnitude. Later examples will only change the algebraic wrinkle; the underlying move stays the same.

## Rewriting a Cosine as Opposite-Frequency Complex Exponentials

**Example:** Rewrite $\cos(4t)$ as a half-sum of opposite-frequency complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine written as $\cos(4t)$. Rewrite it with Euler's identity so the two frequencies stay visible and the cosine is ready for the next step.

$$
\cos(4t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j4t}+e^{-j4t}\right)
$$

Keep the factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ outside both exponentials; that is what preserves the original cosine amplitude. The two terms are the opposite-frequency conjugate pair $e^{j4t}$ and $e^{-j4t}$.

Nothing about the signal has changed except its form. The rewrite now isolates the two frequency components so the next step can transform them separately.

<a id="converting-the-exponential-terms-into-frequency-domain-impulses"></a>
## Converting the Exponential Terms into Frequency-Domain Impulses

**Example:** Convert $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{j4t}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-j4t}$ into their Fourier transforms using the complex-exponential transform pair.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pair of opposite-frequency exponentials from the previous step. Each term has a standard Fourier-transform rule, so the only job here is to convert them one at a time and keep the half-factor attached.

$$
e^{j\omega_0 t}\leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0),\qquad e^{-j\omega_0 t}\leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega_0)
$$

Apply the positive-frequency rule to $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{j4t}$. The outer $\tfrac{1}{2}$ scales the impulse weight from $2\pi$ to $\pi$, giving $\pi\delta(\omega-4)$.

Apply the negative-frequency rule to $\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-j4t}$. The same scaling gives the matching impulse $\pi\delta(\omega+4)$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{j4t}\right\}=\pi\delta(\omega-4),\qquad \mathcal{F}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-j4t}\right\}=\pi\delta(\omega+4)
$$

That is the complete conversion for the two exponential terms. Keep the two impulses separate here; the next step will combine them into the cosine transform pair.

## Combining the Positive and Negative Frequency Impulses

**Example:** Combine the two impulse terms to obtain the Fourier transform of $\cos(4t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the pair of impulses from the previous step: one at $\omega=4$ and one at $\omega=-4$. Combine them into the canonical symmetric cosine transform pair instead of leaving them as separate transformed exponentials.

$$
\pi\delta(\omega-4)+\pi\delta(\omega+4)
$$

The common factor is $\pi$, so the two terms can be written as one symmetric expression. This is the final frequency-domain form of the cosine.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{\cos(4t)\}=\pi\big[\delta(\omega-4)+\delta(\omega+4)\big]
$$

The result is two equal impulses, one at the positive cosine frequency and one at the negative cosine frequency. That symmetric pair is the standard cosine transform pair the later examples will reuse.

<a id="finding-the-fourier-transform-of-a-scaled-cosine"></a>
## Finding the Fourier Transform of a Scaled Cosine

**Example:** Find the Fourier transform of $3\cos(4t)$.

**Explanation**

The only new cue is the leading scalar $3$. Treat it as a constant multiplier and keep it outside the transform so the cosine pair stays unchanged.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{F}\{3\cos(4t)\}
&= 3\mathcal{F}\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}(e^{j4t}+e^{-j4t})\right\} \\
&= 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathcal{F}\{e^{j4t}\}+\mathcal{F}\{e^{-j4t}\}\right] \\
&= 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\left[2\pi\delta(\omega-4)+2\pi\delta(\omega+4)\right] \\
&= 3\pi\left[\delta(\omega-4)+\delta(\omega+4)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The frequency locations stay at $\omega=4$ and $\omega=-4$. Only the impulse weights change, giving the scaled cosine pair $3\pi\left[\delta(\omega-4)+\delta(\omega+4)\right]$.
