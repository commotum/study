## Introduction to Frequency Spacing in Fourier Series

In a Fourier series, the spectrum is not continuous. It appears as discrete harmonic lines, and the spacing between neighboring lines is the angular-frequency spacing $\omega_0$.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

So if you know the period $T$, you know the gap between adjacent Fourier-series lines. The $n$th harmonic sits at $\omega_n = n\omega_0$, so every line is an integer multiple of the same spacing.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi n}{T}
$$

This gives the key trend: when $T$ gets larger, $\omega_0$ gets smaller. The same harmonic numbers are still there, but the lines are packed more tightly together.

$$
\lim_{T\to\infty} \omega_0 = 0
$$

That is the bridge to continuous frequency: as $T\to\infty$, the spacing collapses toward zero, and the Fourier-series line spectrum becomes arbitrarily dense.

## Computing Frequency Spacing from the Period

**Example:** A periodic signal has period $T=0.5$ s. Compute the Fourier-series spacing $\omega_0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the period $T$. For Fourier-series line spacing, use $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ and substitute the given period directly.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{0.5} = 4\pi
$$

So the adjacent Fourier-series harmonics are separated by $4\pi$ rad/s. That is the harmonic gap for this signal, and it is the quantity that will shrink when the period grows.

## Finding Harmonic Frequencies from the Spacing

**Example:** For a signal with period $T=2$ s, compute $\omega_0$ and then list $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that once the spacing is known, each harmonic frequency is just an integer multiple of $\omega_0$. For $T=2$ s, first compute the spacing from the period.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi
$$

Now use the harmonic rule $\omega_n = n\omega_0$. That means the first three positive harmonic lines are the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd multiples of the same spacing.

$$
\omega_1 = \pi,\quad \omega_2 = 2\pi,\quad \omega_3 = 3\pi
$$

So the harmonics stay equally spaced by $\omega_0 = \pi$ rad/s, and the full line pattern is just integer multiples of that gap.

<a id="comparing-frequency-spacing-for-larger-periods"></a>
## Comparing Frequency Spacing for Larger Periods

**Example:** Compare the spacing for periods $T$ and $3T$ and identify which harmonic spectrum is denser.

**Explanation**

The cue is a period change by a factor of $3$, so the spacing rule $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ is the only thing you need. Start from the baseline period $T$ and then replace $T$ by $3T$.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T},\qquad \omega_0' = \frac{2\pi}{3T} = \frac{1}{3}\omega_0
$$

So the larger period gives one-third the spacing. The harmonic lines move closer together, which means the spectrum is denser.

$$
\frac{\omega_0'}{\omega_0} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

This is the reusable inverse scaling rule: multiply the period by a factor, and the Fourier-series spacing shrinks by the same factor.

## Interpreting Frequency Spacing as the Period Grows Without Bound

**Example:** If the period grows without bound, what happens to $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ and the spacing between harmonic lines?

**Explanation**

The cue is the limit $T\to\infty$. Use the same spacing rule $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ and watch what happens as the denominator grows.

$$
\lim_{T\to\infty} \omega_0 = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{2\pi}{T} = 0
$$

So the harmonic spacing collapses to zero. The Fourier-series lines crowd together, which is the signal that the spectrum is approaching a continuous frequency axis.
