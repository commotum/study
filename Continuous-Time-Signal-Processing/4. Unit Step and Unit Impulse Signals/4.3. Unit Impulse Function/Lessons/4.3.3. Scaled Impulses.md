## Introduction to Scaled Impulses

A scaled impulse is written as $A\delta(t-a)$. Read it as one impulse located at $t=a$, with coefficient $A$ carrying the signed area, not the height or width.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-a)\,dt=1
$$

That unit-area rule is the baseline from the prior topic. Once the impulse is shifted to $t=a$, the shift changes location only; the coefficient still controls the area weight.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-a)\,dt=A
$$

So $A\delta(t-a)$ has the same zero-width location behavior as $\delta(t-a)$, but its signed area is $A$. Positive $A$ gives positive area, negative $A$ gives negative area, and fractional $A$ scales the same way.

$$
y(t)=A\delta(t-a)
$$

Use $\delta(t)$ as the base case, then read $A\delta(t)$ and $A\delta(t-a)$ with the same rule: the coefficient sets the impulse area, and the argument shift sets where that area sits. That separation is the idea to carry into the next examples.

## Identifying the Area of a Scaled Impulse

**Example:** Identify the area of each impulse in $2\delta(t)$, $-\delta(t-3)$, and $\frac{1}{4}\delta(t+2)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient immediately in front of $\delta(\cdot)$. For a scaled impulse, that coefficient is the impulse area; the shift inside the argument only sets location.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2\delta(t)\,dt &= 2 \\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\delta(t-3)\,dt &= -1 \\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4}\delta(t+2)\,dt &= \frac{1}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read each coefficient exactly as it appears. The locations are $t=0$, $t=3$, and $t=-2$, but the areas are $2$, $-1$, and $\frac{1}{4}$ because the sign and magnitude live in the coefficient, not in the shift.

$$
\text{areas: } 2,\ -1,\ \frac{1}{4}
$$

## Writing a Scaled Impulse from a Given Area

**Example:** Write expressions for area $3$ at $t=2$ and area $-\frac{1}{2}$ at $t=-1$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the requested area and the requested location. To write a scaled impulse, put the signed area in front and write the location as $t-a$ inside the $\delta$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3\delta(t-2) \\
-\frac{1}{2}\delta(t+1)
\end{aligned}
$$

So area $3$ at $t=2$ becomes $3\delta(t-2)$, and area $-\frac{1}{2}$ at $t=-1$ becomes $-\frac{1}{2}\delta(t+1)$. The shift chooses where the impulse sits; the coefficient gives the area and its sign.

## Interpreting Negative and Fractional Impulse Weights

**Example:** For $y(t)=\delta(t)-\frac{3}{4}\delta(t-1)+2\delta(t+2)$, identify the signed area of each term and report the net area.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient in front of each $\delta(\cdot)$. Read that coefficient as the impulse area, and ignore the shift when you determine the area itself.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta(t) &\rightarrow 1 \\
-\frac{3}{4}\delta(t-1) &\rightarrow -\frac{3}{4} \\
2\delta(t+2) &\rightarrow 2
\end{aligned}
$$

So the signed areas are $1$, $-\frac{3}{4}$, and $2$. The locations are $t=0$, $t=1$, and $t=-2$, but the sign and size come from the coefficients, not from the shifts.

$$
\begin{aligned}
1-\frac{3}{4}+2 &= \frac{9}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the prompt asks for the net area, add the coefficients after reading each term separately. The result is $\frac{9}{4}$, which keeps the negative sign on the middle term and the fractional weight on the first term exactly as written.
