## Introduction to Impulse Area vs. Impulse Amplitude

Impulse terms are recognized by their behavior under integration, not by a visible height at a single point. For ordinary signals, $x(1)=2$ means the value at $t=1$ is two. For impulse notation like $A\delta(t-t_0)$, the symbol $A$ is the concentrated area weight associated with the impulse event, not an ordinary point value.

The unit impulse is a zero-width object with finite unit area, so use area statements as your anchor: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)\,dt=1$. This is the rule that defines what $\delta(t)$ represents.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)\,dt=1
$$

Shifting an impulse does not change the area: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=A$. The center $t_0$ only says where that same area is placed on the time axis.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=A
$$

Coefficients can be positive, negative, or zero. For example, $3\delta(t-2)$ contributes total area $3$, and $-\delta(t-2)$ contributes area $-1$ at $t=2$. In later steps, this same rule lets you read impulse sums quickly.

This contrasts with ordinary amplitude examples where a number may be attached to a whole waveform. To see the difference clearly, compare $x(2)=2$ for a standard signal with $2\delta(t-2)$, where $2$ is the impulse area at $t=2$.

$$
\left[\delta(t-1)+3\delta(t+2)\right]\ \rightarrow\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\delta(t-1)+3\delta(t+2)\right]dt=4
$$

Use this interpretation for every upcoming section: identify the impulse coefficient as area weight, keep the shift index with it, and then simplify expressions by matching this rule before moving to worked examples.

## Identifying Area as the Quantity Assigned to an Impulse

**Example:** Find the impulse area for each term in $x(t)=3\delta(t)+2\delta(t-1)-\delta(t+1)$.

**Explanation**

Use the impulse cue from the previous tutorial: each term is an area-weighted point contribution. A coefficient in front of a $\delta$ gives the impulse area, while the $\delta$ argument gives where that area is located.

First, rewrite each term as $A\delta(t-t_0)$ so you can read the rule immediately: $3\delta(t)=3\delta(t-0)$, $2\delta(t-1)=2\delta(t-1)$, and $-\delta(t+1)=-1\delta(t-(-1))$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=A
$$

Apply that rule to each coefficient directly. The three area values are read from the scalar in front of each impulse, then paired with each shift center.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}3\delta(t)\,dt=3,\quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2\delta(t-1)\,dt=2,\quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-\delta(t+1))\,dt=-1
$$

So the impulse areas are $3$ at $t=0$, $2$ at $t=1$, and $-1$ at $t=-1$. Preserve both sign and location, and if a term had coefficient $0$, that term would contribute no impulse area.

## Distinguishing Impulse Area from Ordinary Signal Amplitude

**Example:** Compare the interpretations of the coefficient in $3\delta(t-1)$ and $3u(t-1)$: report the area-like quantity in each and explain why they differ.

**Explanation**

The cue is a shared numeric factor and a shift, but the symbols are different models. For $3\delta(t-1)$, treat the leading $3$ as an impulse coefficient; for $3u(t-1)$, treat the same $3$ as the ordinary amplitude of a shifted step signal.

Apply the impulse rule: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}3\delta(t-1)\,dt=3$, so the term contributes a single area of $3$ at $t=1$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}3\delta(t-1)\,dt=3
$$

Read the step term as $3u(t-1)=0$ for $t<1$ and $3$ for $t\ge1$, so it is a sustained non-impulse region. It does not collapse to one impulse-area number tied to a single instant.

Conclusion: impulse coefficients are area weights on point-support terms, while ordinary amplitudes in functions like $u(t-1)$ describe region values and do not imply a one-point area weight.

## Interpreting Impulse Diagrams by Area Instead of Height

**Example:** Write the signal expression for the shown impulse diagram: impulses at $t= -1$, $t=0$, and $t=2$ with area labels $3$, $-2$, and $4$, respectively.

**Explanation**

Read each stem as one impulse event and treat the printed stem label as the area coefficient $A$, not as a peak height. This keeps the model aligned with the core move: coefficients on impulses are area weights.

![](<../Source/4.3.2. Impulse Area vs. Impulse Amplitude/Images/l002-s004-te-section-004.png>)

Record each pair $(t_0,A)$ from the diagram: $(-1,3)$, $(0,-2)$, and $(2,4)$. Convert each pair directly to $A\delta(t-t_0)$ while preserving sign.

$$
3\delta(t-(-1)),-2\delta(t-0),4\delta(t-2)
$$

Simplify the expression and order terms by increasing time location so it is readable as $3\delta(t+1)-2\delta(t)+4\delta(t-2)$. The negative sign remains attached to the center at $t=0$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[3\delta(t+1)-2\delta(t)+4\delta(t-2)\right]dt=3-2+4=5
$$

The signed sum confirms that impulses are checked by area weights and location, so a large stem with a small label would never be interpreted as a small-value impulse unless the area label says so.

## Comparing Ordinary Signal Values with Impulse Area Values

**Example:** For $y(t)=2\delta(t-1)+3u(t-1)-\delta(t+1)$, state the impulse area, and identify what changes in ordinary-value interpretation for the $u$ term.

**Explanation**

The cue is a mixed expression: two impulse terms and one ordinary step term. Treat each $\delta$ term by its area rule, and treat the $u$ term as a region-valued ordinary signal, not as a single impulse-area quantity.

Split the expression into its impulse and non-impulse parts:
$y(t)=\big[2\delta(t-1)-\delta(t+1)\big]+3u(t-1)$.
Now apply the standard impulse-area identity to the bracketed terms.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=A
$$

Apply the identity directly to each impulse: the area at $t=1$ is $2$, and the area at $t=-1$ is $-1$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}2\delta(t-1)\,dt=2,\quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(-\delta(t+1))\,dt=-1
$$

For the ordinary term, use the step interpretation: $3u(t-1)=0$ for $t<1$ and $3u(t-1)=3$ for $t\ge1$. That behavior does not collapse to one instant and is not represented by the impulse-area sum.

$$
3u(t-1)=\begin{cases}0,&t<1\\3,&t\ge1\end{cases}
$$

Final rule: for $y(t)=2\delta(t-1)+3u(t-1)-\delta(t+1)$, report impulse areas as $2$ and $-1$ (mixed at $t=1$ and $t=-1$), while $u(t-1)$ is interpreted by its interval value, not by a one-point area weight.
