## Introduction to Delaying Exponential Signals with Unit Steps

A delayed exponential keeps the same exponential shape, but it stays off until the activation time $t_0$. The shifted unit step $u(t-t_0)$ marks that turn-on point, and the shifted argument $t-t_0$ makes the exponential count time from the same delayed origin.

$$
x(t)=e^{a(t-t_0)}u(t-t_0)
$$

Read the rule as one matched shift: the step decides when the signal turns on, and the exponential decides what time the waveform is measured from after it turns on. If the start time is $t_0=3$, the delayed exponential should use $t-3$ in both places.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2(t-3)}u(t-3)
$$

The safest recognition rule is simple: do not shift only the step. If the signal starts later, the exponential argument must shift with it so the waveform and the activation time stay aligned.

## Delaying a Basic Exponential to a Positive Start Time

**Example:** Find the unit-step form for a delayed exponential that starts at $t=3$ and has $e^{-2t}$ shape when active.

**Explanation**

The cue is a delayed exponential: the signal is off before the start time and then follows the same exponential shape after the start time. From the previous step, the matching pattern is to shift both the exponential argument and the unit step by the same amount.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)\rightarrow x(t)=e^{-2(t-3)}u(t-3)
$$

Here the delay is $t_0=3$, so every $t$ in the prototype becomes $t-3$. That gives the same waveform, but its time origin moves to $t=3$ and the step turns the signal on at the same instant.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2(t-3)}u(t-3)
$$

This is the canonical delayed form: the step handles the activation time, and the shifted exponent handles the delayed time reference. Do not shift only the step, because the exponential itself must move with the same delay.

## Including Scale Factors in Delayed Exponential Signals

**Example:** Convert $x(t)=3e^{-0.5t}$ delayed by $2$ into unit-step form, and explain how the scale factor $3$ is represented.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same delayed-exponential pattern as before, but now there is a gain of $3$ in front. The delay still belongs in both the exponential argument and the unit step, while the scale factor stays outside the delayed waveform.

$$
x(t)=3e^{-0.5t}u(t)\rightarrow x(t)=3e^{-0.5(t-2)}u(t-2)
$$

Here the delay is $t_0=2$, so every $t$ in the prototype becomes $t-2$. The factor $3$ is unchanged because it is a multiplicative gain, not part of the time shift.

$$
x(t)=3e^{-0.5(t-2)}u(t-2)
$$

This keeps the active interval and the amplitude separate: for $t<2$ the step is off, and for $t\ge 2$ the signal follows the same delayed exponential shape scaled by $3$. The delay moves the time origin, but the gain stays external.

## Converting Piecewise Delayed Exponentials to Step Form

**Example:** Convert the piecewise signal $x(t)=\{e^{-t},\,2\le t<7;\,0,\,\text{elsewhere}\}$ to step form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a piecewise exponential with one finite active interval. Since the signal starts at $t=2$, re-anchor the exponential at that start time and use a step difference to gate the interval.

$$
e^{-t}=e^{-2}e^{-(t-2)}
$$

The factor $e^{-2}$ preserves the value at the start time, while $u(t-2)-u(t-7)$ is $1$ only on $2\le t<7$. That combination turns the delayed exponential on at $t=2$ and off at $t=7$.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2}e^{-(t-2)}\big[u(t-2)-u(t-7)\big]
$$

This matches the piecewise definition exactly: the signal is $0$ before $t=2$, follows the delayed exponential shape on the interval, and returns to $0$ at $t=7$ and beyond. The exponential shift and the two step boundaries are doing separate jobs, so both must appear.

<a id="distinguishing-delayed-exponentials-from-gated-unshifted-exponentials"></a>
## Distinguishing Delayed Exponentials from Gated Unshifted Exponentials

**Example:** For a signal that equals $e^{-t}$ on $2 \le t < 6$ and $0$ elsewhere, write the step form that keeps the waveform unshifted.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-time window around a formula already written in global time. Because the waveform is specified as $e^{-t}$ itself, do not re-anchor the exponent; just gate the original formula with the interval window.

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}\big[u(t-2)-u(t-6)\big]
$$

The step difference is $1$ only on $2\le t<6$, so it preserves the exponential on that interval and kills it outside. If the waveform itself had to move to a new time origin, the exponent would shift too; here it does not.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<2\\ e^{-t}, & 2\le t<6\\ 0, & t\ge 6\end{cases}
$$

Quick test: step difference only means gate the existing waveform; shifted exponent plus step means re-time the waveform itself. This prompt is the first case, so the unshifted-gated form is the match.
