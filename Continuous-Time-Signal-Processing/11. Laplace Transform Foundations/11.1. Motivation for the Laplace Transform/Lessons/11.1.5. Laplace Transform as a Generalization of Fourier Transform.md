## Introduction to Laplace Transform as a Generalization of Fourier Transform

The goal of this lesson is to handle a signal class that does not always fit the pure Fourier-transform pattern. Some signals make $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$ diverge, so the target move is to build a generalized transform form that keeps the Fourier frequency kernel but introduces controlled damping.

Start with the generalized form $X(s)$ and one convergent behavior pattern. In the convergent base case, take a decaying signal such as $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, where the integral remains well defined. The Laplace definition is $X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$, and this single line is the object students must recognize.

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Replace $s$ with its real-imaginary split, $s=\sigma+j\omega$. This is the key recognition action: separate the damping term and the Fourier-frequency term in the exponent.

$$
s=\sigma+j\omega
$$

Next, apply that split directly to the transform expression. You should read $e^{-\sigma t}$ as a weighting that can force convergence and read $e^{-j\omega t}$ as the usual Fourier kernel on the weighted signal.

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} [x(t)e^{-\sigma t}]e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Now connect directly to ordinary Fourier language: when $\sigma=0$, the weighted term becomes $x(t)$, and the same expression reduces to the Fourier transform $X(j\omega)$. That is the bridge this lesson uses, with the action step being, "set $\sigma=0$ and read the special case."

Because this is a tutorial step, stop after the rule and its meaning. Do not add extra examples yet; the next section will hold the simplest worked comparison between weighted and unweighted forms while you keep notation stable across later questions.

## Rewriting $X(s)$ as a Fourier Transform of $x(t)e^{-\sigma t}$

**Example:** Rewrite $X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$ as a Fourier-style transform using $s=\sigma+j\omega$.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the Laplace variable form $X(s)$. To reuse the same core move, start by substituting the complex split $s=\sigma+j\omega$ so the expression is directly comparable to Fourier notation.

$$
s=\sigma+j\omega
$$

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-(\sigma+j\omega)t}\,dt
$$

Apply the exponent-splitting rule $e^{-(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}$, then group terms so one factor is the weighted signal and the other is the Fourier kernel.

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[x(t)e^{-\sigma t}]e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

This is exactly a Fourier transform in $\omega$ of the weighted signal $x(t)e^{-\sigma t}$. For fixed $\sigma$, write the result as $X(s)=\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}(\omega)$.

$$
X(s)=\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}(\omega)
$$

## Recovering the Fourier Transform by Setting $\sigma=0$

**Example:** Given $X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$, show the result when $\sigma=0$ and identify it as the Fourier transform.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the Laplace form $X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$. Use the same rule as before: split the complex variable as $s=\sigma+j\omega$ so you can compare with Fourier-kernel notation.

$$
s=\sigma+j\omega
$$

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} [x(t)e^{-\sigma t}]e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Set the damping part to its special value by substituting $\sigma=0$. This makes the exponential weight $e^{-\sigma t}$ equal to $1$, so the transform collapses to the pure Fourier-kernel form.

$$
s=\sigma+j\omega,\quad \sigma=0 \Rightarrow s=j\omega
$$

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[x(t)e^{-0\cdot t}]e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

So the exact checkpoint is $X(j\omega)=\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\}(\omega)$: the Fourier transform is the $\sigma=0$ slice of $X(s)$.

## Comparing Weighted and Unweighted Transform Integrals

**Example:** Compare $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$ for the same signal.

**Explanation**

The cue is that both expressions use the same $x(t)$, but one is unweighted Fourier form and one is Laplace-weighted form. Use a concrete test signal to decide when the unweighted form is valid.

Let $x(t)=u(t)$. Then the two candidates are

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt,\qquad X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Now use the step function support: $u(t)=0$ for $t<0$ and $u(t)=1$ for $t\ge 0$. The Fourier form becomes $\int_0^{\infty}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$, which does not produce a finite ordinary convergent value in this lesson setting.

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt \;\text{(not convergent in the ordinary sense)}
$$

For the weighted form, the same support gives a real-exponential damping term:

$$
X(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\sigma+j\omega}, \quad \sigma>0
$$

So the rule is: use the unweighted integral when it converges as $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$; otherwise use the weighted Laplace form with $\sigma>0$ to enforce convergence and recover $X(s)$.

## Interpreting $X(s)$ as a Family of Fourier Transforms Indexed by $\sigma$

**Example:** Using two parameter values $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, express $X(s)$ as two related Fourier transforms of weighted signals.

**Explanation**

The cue is again the Laplace definition and the split $s=\sigma+j\omega$. To show the family viewpoint, hold one signal fixed and evaluate the transform at two different damping parameters.

Choose $x(t)=u(t)$ and form the two candidates with $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$.

$$
X_{\sigma_1}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-\sigma_1 t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt,\qquad X_{\sigma_2}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-\sigma_2 t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Each expression is the Fourier transform of a different weighted signal, so identify both as a family of the same transform shape with different damping levels.

$$
X_{\sigma_1}(\omega)=\mathcal{F}\{u(t)e^{-\sigma_1 t}\}(\omega),\qquad X_{\sigma_2}(\omega)=\mathcal{F}\{u(t)e^{-\sigma_2 t}\}(\omega)
$$

$$
u(t)e^{-\sigma_i t}=e^{-\sigma_i t}\;\text{for }t\ge0,\;0\text{ for }t<0,\quad i\in\{1,2\}
$$

So the same formula yields two convergent one-sided integrals when $\sigma_i>0$, but the two integrands are not the same because each $\sigma_i$ changes damping strength in the same Fourier-kernel slot.

$$
X_{\sigma}(\omega)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=\frac{1}{\sigma+j\omega},\quad \sigma>0
$$

The family message is: $X(s)$ is not one isolated object here but the family $\{\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}(\omega)\}_{\sigma}$, indexed by damping parameter choices.
