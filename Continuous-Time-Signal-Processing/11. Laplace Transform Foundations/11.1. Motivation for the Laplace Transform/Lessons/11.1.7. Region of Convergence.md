## Introduction to Region of Convergence

The object to recognize is the **region of convergence**.
For a given signal $x(t)$, the ROC is the set of complex values $s$ where the Laplace integral exists and is finite.

$$
X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-st}\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Take one complex point $s=\sigma+j\omega$ and test directly whether the integral is finite. The action is: separate the kernel into a magnitude term and a phase term, then use $|e^{-j\omega t}|=1$, so convergence is controlled by the real exponent factor and the signal tail, not by oscillation alone.

Then define the ROC as all $s$ values that pass that convergence test. That means the same rule applies point-by-point in the $s$-plane: collect every $\sigma+j\omega$ that makes the integral finite into one explicit set.

$$
\mathrm{ROC}=\left\{s: \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt<\infty\right\}
$$

Result: the ROC is the full set of $s$ that satisfy the inequality. In the next sections, turn this test into a condition on $\sigma$ and then reinterpret that condition as a geometric $s$-plane region.

## Testing Convergence at One $s$-Plane Value

**Example:** Compute whether the Laplace transform converges for $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$ at $s=-1+j\,3$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the Laplace test form $X(s)=\int_0^{\infty}x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$. For a single-point check, substitute the candidate $s$ value directly and test whether the resulting integral value is finite.

$$
X(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-2t}e^{-st}\,dt
$$

$$
X(-1+j3)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-2t}e^{-(-1+j3)t}\,dt=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}e^{-j3t}\,dt
$$

Separate the exponential terms. Since $|e^{-j3t}|=1$, the oscillatory factor $e^{-j3t}$ does not change magnitude, so convergence is controlled by $\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}\,dt$.

$$
\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}\,dt=1
$$

Result: the integral is finite, so the Laplace transform converges at $s=-1+j3$. In the lesson rule form, $\sigma=-1$ and the condition $\sigma>-2$ is true.

## Converting a Convergence Test into a Condition on $\sigma$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$, rewrite the convergence result as a condition on $\sigma$ for all $s=\sigma+j\omega$.

**Explanation**

The cue now is to convert a single-point outcome into a reusable inequality. Start from the same weighted kernel and isolate the part of the exponent that drives magnitude.

$$
\int_0^{\infty} e^{-2t}e^{-st}\,dt=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-2t}e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Use $|e^{-j\omega t}|=1$ so only the real-exponential factor sets tail behavior. This means convergence is equivalent to the real exponent on $e^{-2t}e^{-\sigma t}=e^{-(2+\sigma)t}$ being negative.

$$
-(2+\sigma)<0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma>-2
$$

So for all $s=\sigma+j\omega$, the ROC is the half-plane defined by $\sigma>-2$. Boundary is the vertical line Re{$s$}$=-2$, and the valid side is the right side of that line.

## Marking the Region of Convergence in the $s$-Plane

**Example:** Given condition $\mathrm{Re}\{s\}> -2$, show the ROC on the $s$-plane and mark the boundary.

**Explanation**

Cue: the algebraic result is $\sigma>-2$ for the signal $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$. The action is to map this inequality into the $s$-plane and mark every admissible $s$ point.

$$
\mathrm{ROC}=\left\{s=\sigma+j\omega:\,\sigma>-2\right\}
$$

![](<../Source/11.1.7. Region of Convergence/Images/l007-s004-te-section-004-roc.png>)

$$
\sigma=-2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{vertical boundary line in }s\text{-plane}
$$

The shaded region is the right half-plane. It is the set of points with $\sigma>-2$, so the $j\omega$ axis is inside this ROC since $\sigma=0$ satisfies the inequality.

Interpretation for the next step: a ROC condition written as $\mathrm{Re}\{s\}>a$ always means shade away from the left boundary side and keep the boundary line labeled as excluded unless an equality condition is explicitly stated.

## Checking Whether the Fourier-Transform Slice Is Included in the Region of Convergence

**Example:** For ROC $\mathrm{Re}\{s\}> -2$, decide whether the Fourier-transform slice $s=j\omega$ is included.

**Explanation**

The cue is a direct membership test: use the derived ROC inequality and the slice identity $s=j\omega$ to test whether one point on the $s$-plane is inside.

Step 1 is to convert the slice to a real-part test: $s=j\omega \iff \sigma=0$. That gives a single scalar value for the condition.

$$
\sigma = 0
$$

Step 2 is to check the ROC inequality with this value: substitute into $\mathrm{Re}\{s\}>-2$ and evaluate $0>-2$, which is true.

$$
s=j\omega \Rightarrow X(j\omega)\text{ exists because }0 \in \{\sigma>-2\}
$$

Result: the Fourier-transform slice is included in the ROC, so the original signal has a valid $X(j\omega)$ interpretation for this ROC inequality.
