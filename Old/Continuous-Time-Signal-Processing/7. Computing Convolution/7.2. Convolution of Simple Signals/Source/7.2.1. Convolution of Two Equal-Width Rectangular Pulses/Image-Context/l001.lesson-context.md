Tutorial blocks:
- paragraph:
  text: For two equal-width rectangular pulses, the convolution is easiest to read in the convolution variable tau. Think of x(tau) as the fixed rectangle, and think of h(t - tau) as the rectangle that slides across it as t changes.
- display-math:
  latex: y(t) = (x * h)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t - \tau)\,d\tau
- paragraph:
  text: Use the base case where both pulses have width T. The fixed pulse x(tau) is nonzero on 0 <= tau <= T, while the shifted pulse h(t - tau) is nonzero on t - T <= tau <= t. The only part of the integral that contributes is the tau-interval where these two supports overlap.
- image:
  image_slot_id: section-001
- paragraph:
  text: Because rectangular pulses are constant on their supports, their product is also constant wherever they overlap. If the pulse heights are A and B, then the integrand equals AB on the overlap and equals 0 outside the overlap.
- display-math:
  latex: y(t) = AB\,L(t)
- display-math:
  latex: L(t) = \text{length of }(\operatorname{supp} x) \cap (\operatorname{supp} h(t - \tau))
- paragraph:
  text: So the main action is not to expand the integral right away. First track the overlap length L(t). For equal widths, there are three key events: first contact, peak alignment, and last contact. The overlap length starts at 0, grows until the rectangles fully align, and then shrinks back to 0.
- paragraph:
  text: That rising-then-falling overlap length is why the convolution output is triangular. Later sections will compute the contact times, the rising length, the falling length, and the height scaling one piece at a time.
