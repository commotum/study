## Introduction to Finding Output with $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$

When an LTI system is described in the frequency domain, the output is found by staying in $j\omega$ until the output spectrum is formed. If the input spectrum is $X(j\omega)$ and the system response is $H(j\omega)$, then the output spectrum is $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

$H(j\omega)$ comes from the impulse response $h(t)$, so the system is already represented by a transform pair. The only new work is to identify the two factors, multiply them, and simplify the product only as much as needed.

$$
y(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{Y(j\omega)\}
$$

The full procedure is always the same: read off $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$, form $Y(j\omega)$, simplify the product if that makes the inverse transform easier, and then apply the inverse Fourier transform to recover $y(t)$. Keep the first pass small so the theorem attaches to one repeatable algebraic move before the lesson becomes more abstract.

## Forming $Y(j\omega)$ from $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$

**Example:** Given $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{j\omega+1}$ and $H(j\omega)=\frac{2}{j\omega+3}$, form $Y(j\omega)$ in product form.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the input spectrum $X(j\omega)$ and the system response $H(j\omega)$ are already given, so the only job is to multiply them using the frequency-domain LTI rule $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$. Substitute the two expressions exactly as they are, with no cancellation yet.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{j\omega+1}\frac{2}{j\omega+3}
$$

This is the correct output spectrum in product form. Leave both factors visible so the next step can simplify the product before moving on to the inverse Fourier transform.

## Simplifying the Frequency-Domain Product

**Example:** Given $Y(j\omega)=\frac{2(j\omega+4)}{(j\omega+1)(j\omega+2)}\cdot\frac{j\omega+1}{j\omega+4}$, simplify the product before inversion.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the product already contains a factor that appears in both the numerator and denominator, so the job is to simplify $Y(j\omega)$ before inversion. Start from the product exactly as formed in the previous step and keep the notation unchanged.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{2(j\omega+4)}{(j\omega+1)(j\omega+2)}\cdot\frac{j\omega+1}{j\omega+4}
$$

Now cancel the shared factors $(j\omega+1)$ and $(j\omega+4)$. After the cancellation, the spectrum is much simpler and is ready to match a standard inverse-transform pair.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{j\omega+2}
$$

This reduced form is the correct answer for this step. Keep the output in this transform-ready shape so the next step can recover $y(t)$ without carrying the canceled factors any farther.

## Inverting $Y(j\omega)$ Using a Known Transform Pair

**Example:** Given $Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{j\omega+2}$, use the standard pair for $e^{-at}u(t)$ to find $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the single factor $j\omega+2$ in the denominator. That matches a standard causal exponential pair, so the job here is to recognize the form and read off the time-domain signal directly.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{j\omega+2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad y(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{Y(j\omega)\}=2e^{-2t}u(t)
$$

The constant $2$ stays outside the pair, and the value $2$ in the denominator becomes the decay rate. The inverse transform gives the output $y(t)=2e^{-2t}u(t)$ with no extra algebra.

## Finding $y(t)$ from a Decomposed Frequency-Domain Product

**Example:** Given $Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{(j\omega+1)(j\omega+3)}$, decompose it into standard terms and find $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the product of two first-order factors in the denominator. That means split $Y(j\omega)$ into a sum of standard single-pole terms, then invert each term separately.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{2}{(j\omega+1)(j\omega+3)}=\frac{A}{j\omega+1}+\frac{B}{j\omega+3}
$$

Clear the denominators to get $2=A(j\omega+3)+B(j\omega+1)$. Matching the poles gives $A=1$ and $B=-1$, so the spectrum becomes a difference of two standard causal-exponential pairs.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{j\omega+1}-\frac{1}{j\omega+3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad y(t)=e^{-t}u(t)-e^{-3t}u(t)
$$

This is the output signal. The decomposition lets linearity handle the inverse transform term by term, so the final answer is just the sum of the two time-domain pieces.
