## Introduction to Magnitude and Phase Effects of Time Shifting

Suppose $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and let $Y(j\omega)$ be the transform of the delayed signal $y(t)=x(t-t_0)$ with $t_0>0$. The cue in this step is the extra factor from the shift: separate what it does to magnitude and what it does to phase.

$$
x(t-t_0)\leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega t_0}X(j\omega)
$$

The factor $e^{-j\omega t_0}$ has unit magnitude for every $\omega$, so the magnitude spectrum does not change at all. The delay only rotates the spectrum.

$$
|Y(j\omega)|=|X(j\omega)|,\qquad \angle Y(j\omega)=\angle X(j\omega)-\omega t_0
$$

So the phase gains the linear term $-\omega t_0$, which means the phase slope changes by $-t_0$.

If the shift is an advance instead of a delay, just flip the sign of $t_0$; the magnitude still stays unchanged, and the phase term becomes $+\omega t_0$. Later examples will reuse exactly this pattern by keeping the magnitude fixed and reading the sign from the phase slope.

## Magnitude Spectrum of a Time-Shifted Signal

**Example:** If $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and $y(t)=x(t-2)$, what is $|Y(j\omega)|$?

**Explanation**

The cue is the delay by $2$ in $y(t)=x(t-2)$. Use the time-shift property with the known pair $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, so the shifted spectrum is the original spectrum multiplied by $e^{-j2\omega}$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=e^{-j2\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

Now take the magnitude of both sides. The factor $e^{-j2\omega}$ has unit magnitude for every $\omega$, so it does not change the size of the spectrum.

$$
|Y(j\omega)|=|e^{-j2\omega}|\,|X(j\omega)|=|X(j\omega)|
$$

So the magnitude spectrum is unchanged by the delay, and the answer is $|Y(j\omega)|=|X(j\omega)|$.

## Phase Change from a Time Delay

**Example:** If $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and $\angle X(j\omega)=\omega/2$, what is $\angle Y(j\omega)$ for $y(t)=x(t-3)$?

**Explanation**

The cue is the delay by $3$ in $y(t)=x(t-3)$. Keep the original phase $\angle X(j\omega)=\omega/2$ and use the time-shift rule to add only the delay-induced linear term.

$$
Y(j\omega)=e^{-j3\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

The factor $e^{-j3\omega}$ contributes phase $-3\omega$, and it has unit magnitude, so it does not change the magnitude spectrum.

$$
\angle Y(j\omega)=\angle X(j\omega)-3\omega=\omega/2-3\omega=-5\omega/2
$$

So the new phase spectrum is $-5\omega/2$, and the magnitude stays unchanged.

## Phase Change from a Time Advance

**Example:** If $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and $\angle X(j\omega)=\omega/2$, what is $\angle Y(j\omega)$ for $y(t)=x(t+3)$?

**Explanation**

A time advance is the mirror image of the delay case. Keep the original phase $\omega/2$ and add the advance term that comes from shifting $x(t)$ to $x(t+3)$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=e^{j3\omega}X(j\omega)
$$

The factor $e^{j3\omega}$ has unit magnitude, so the magnitude spectrum stays $|Y(j\omega)|=|X(j\omega)|$. Its phase contribution is $+3\omega$.

$$
\angle Y(j\omega)=\angle X(j\omega)+3\omega=\omega/2+3\omega=7\omega/2
$$

So the advance leaves the magnitude unchanged and shifts the phase line upward by $3\omega$, giving $\angle Y(j\omega)=7\omega/2$.

<a id="identifying-a-time-shift-from-linear-phase"></a>
## Identifying a Time Shift from Linear Phase

**Example:** If $Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)e^{-j4\omega}$, what time shift does $y(t)$ represent relative to $x(t)$?

**Explanation**

Read the linear phase term first. The factor multiplying $X(j\omega)$ is $e^{-j4\omega}$, so compare its exponent to the standard shift form $e^{-j\omega t_0}$.

$$
-4\omega=-\omega t_0\;\Rightarrow\;t_0=4
$$

Because $t_0$ is positive, the signal is delayed by 4 units: $y(t)=x(t-4)$. The factor $e^{-j4\omega}$ has unit magnitude, so the magnitude spectrum does not change.

$$
y(t)=x(t-4),\qquad |Y(j\omega)|=|X(j\omega)|
$$

So the negative linear phase slope identifies a delay of 4, and the magnitude remains unchanged.
