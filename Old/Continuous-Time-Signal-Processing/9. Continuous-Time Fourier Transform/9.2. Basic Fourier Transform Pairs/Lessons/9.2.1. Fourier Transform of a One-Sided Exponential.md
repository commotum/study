## Introduction to Fourier Transform of a One-Sided Exponential

The signal to recognize here is a decaying one-sided exponential, $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$ with $a>0$. The unit step $u(t)$ is the cue that the signal is zero for $t<0$ and equals the exponential only on the nonnegative-time side.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Use that cue to rewrite the transform over the step-limited support, so the integral runs from $0$ to $\infty$. At this point you are setting up the transform, not evaluating it yet.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_0^\infty e^{-at}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Combine the two exponentials into the single kernel $e^{-(a+j\omega)t}$. Because $a>0$, the kernel decays as $t$ grows, which is the convergence condition that makes this one-sided exponential a standard Fourier-transform pair.

$$
e^{-at}u(t)\;\leftrightarrow\;\frac{1}{a+j\omega},\qquad a>0
$$

That pair is the result to keep in view for the rest of this lesson: one-sided support gives the lower limit, and the exponential decay gives the denominator $a+j\omega$.

## Setting Up the Transform Integral for a One-Sided Exponential

**Example:** Set up the Fourier transform integral for $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit step $u(t)$: it turns $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$ into a one-sided signal, so the Fourier-transform setup only needs the nonnegative-time support.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Start from the transform definition and use the support limit from $u(t)$ to replace the lower bound with $0$ and the upper bound with $\infty$.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Keep the decay factor and the kernel visible long enough to show the product. Then combine them into a single exponential so the setup is ready for the next step.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(2+j\omega)t}\,dt
$$

That is the complete setup for this example: one-sided support sets the limits, and the decay constant becomes part of the complex exponent. The next step can evaluate the integral from this starting point.

## Evaluating the Exponential Integral

**Example:** Evaluate the Fourier transform integral for $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$ and simplify the result.

**Explanation**

Start from the setup integral for $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$ and treat it as an improper integral, since the upper limit extends to infinity.

$$
X(\omega)=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_0^b e^{-(2+j\omega)t}\,dt
$$

Use the antiderivative of the exponential kernel, keeping the denominator $2+j\omega$ intact.

$$
X(\omega)=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac{e^{-(2+j\omega)t}}{2+j\omega}\right]_0^b
$$

Because the real part of $2+j\omega$ is $2>0$, the exponential term vanishes as $b\to\infty$. At $t=0$, the exponential equals $1$.

$$
X(\omega)=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1-e^{-(2+j\omega)b}}{2+j\omega}
$$

So the closed form is $X(\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$. That is the evaluated transform for the one-sided exponential with decay constant $2$.

## Writing the One-Sided Exponential Transform Pair

**Example:** Write the transform pair for $e^{-at}u(t)$ and use it to identify the transform of $e^{-4t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The reusable cue is the one-sided exponential $e^{-at}u(t)$ with $a>0$: the unit step limits the signal to $t\ge 0$, so the Fourier transform can be stored as a lookup pair.

$$
e^{-at}u(t)\;\leftrightarrow\;\frac{1}{a+j\omega},\qquad a>0
$$

The earlier calculation with $a=2$ produced exactly the same denominator pattern, so it is the concrete check that the lookup rule matches the integral evaluation.

$$
e^{-2t}u(t)\;\leftrightarrow\;\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

Now use the same template with $a=4$ to read off the transform of $e^{-4t}u(t)$.

$$
e^{-4t}u(t)\;\leftrightarrow\;\frac{1}{4+j\omega}
$$

So the final lookup form is $e^{-at}u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{a+j\omega}$ for $a>0$, and any specific positive decay constant drops straight into the denominator as $a+j\omega$.
