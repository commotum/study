## Introduction to Adding Same-Frequency Cosines

When two cosine terms share the same angular frequency, do not add them directly in the time domain. Treat the shared $\omega$ as the cue that the terms belong to one phasor-addition problem, then convert each cosine to a phasor and add the phasors as complex quantities.

Start with the base case $A\cos(\omega t)+B\cos(\omega t)$. The shared $\omega$ tells you that both terms use the same frequency tag, so each cosine becomes a zero-phase phasor before anything is added.

$$
A\cos(\omega t)+B\cos(\omega t)\rightarrow A\angle 0 + B\angle 0
$$

The same rule works for phase-shifted cosines such as $A_1\cos(\omega t+\phi_1)+A_2\cos(\omega t+\phi_2)$. Keep the common $\omega$ outside the phasor value, and convert the amplitudes and phases into $A_1\angle\phi_1$ and $A_2\angle\phi_2$.

$$
A_1\cos(\omega t+\phi_1)+A_2\cos(\omega t+\phi_2)\rightarrow A_1\angle\phi_1 + A_2\angle\phi_2
$$

After conversion, add the phasors as ordinary complex quantities. The frequency tag $\omega$ does not get added; it is only the shared label that tells you the cosines are comparable at the same frequency.

This step is just the setup: recognize the shared $\omega$, rewrite each cosine as $A\angle\phi$, and write the complex sum cleanly. Later steps will make the phasor sum more explicit, but this lesson starts with the recognition-and-setup move.

<a id="recognizing-a-common-angular-frequency"></a>
## Recognizing a Common Angular Frequency

**Example:** In $4\cos(5t+\pi/6)+7\cos(5t-\pi/3)$, identify the shared angular frequency and decide whether the terms can be combined as same-frequency phasors.

**Explanation**

Read the coefficient of $t$ in each cosine argument. The first term has $5t+\pi/6$ and the second term has $5t-\pi/3$, so both cosines carry the same angular frequency.

Because both terms use the same $\omega$, the pair is eligible for phasor addition. The amplitudes $4$ and $7$ and the phases $\pi/6$ and $-\pi/3$ do not change that decision.

State the result explicitly: the shared angular frequency is $\omega=5$, so these two cosines can be treated as one phasor-addition problem.

This recognition step does not convert anything yet; it only confirms that the later phasor conversion is valid because the frequency is common.

## Converting Each Cosine to a Phasor

**Example:** Convert $4\cos(5t+\pi/6)+7\cos(5t-\pi/3)$ into a phasor-sum expression.

**Explanation**

Because the two cosines already share $\omega=5$, this step is just a notation conversion. Read the first term's amplitude and phase as $4$ and $\pi/6$, then read the second term's amplitude and phase as $7$ and $-\pi/3$.

Write the first cosine as $4\angle\pi/6$. The amplitude stays with the phasor, and the phase becomes the angle.

Write the second cosine as $7\angle-\pi/3$ and keep the shared frequency tag $5$ outside the phasors. That keeps the notation frequency-preserving.

The phasor-sum form is $4\angle\pi/6 + 7\angle-\pi/3$. Nothing has been added yet; this step only converts each cosine cleanly so the next step can combine the phasors.

## Forming the Resultant Phasor Sum

**Example:** Form the resultant phasor sum for $5\cos(3t)+5\cos(3t-\pi/2)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shared angular frequency $\omega=3$, so the two terms can be handled as one phasor-addition problem. The converted phasors are already visible from the cosine data: the first term has amplitude $5$ and phase $0$, and the second term has amplitude $5$ and phase $-\pi/2$.

$$
5\angle 0 + 5\angle(-\pi/2)
$$

Write the result as the phasor sum $5\angle 0 + 5\angle(-\pi/2)$. The plus sign is ordinary complex addition, so the two phasors are combined directly instead of being turned back into time-domain cosines.

The resultant expression stays in phasor form for now. That keeps the shared frequency $3$ attached to the sum and leaves rectangular or polar simplification for the next step.

## Adding More Than Two Same-Frequency Cosine Phasors

**Example:** Convert $2\cos(7t)+3\cos(7t+\pi/4)+4\cos(7t-\pi/3)$ into one phasor-sum expression.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shared angular frequency $\omega=7$ in all three cosine terms. That means the whole expression is one phasor-addition problem, and each cosine should be carried into phasor form without dropping any term.

Convert the zero-phase term to $2\angle 0$, the positive-phase term to $3\angle\pi/4$, and the negative-phase term to $4\angle-\pi/3$. Each amplitude stays attached to its own phasor, and the shared frequency label remains $7$.

$$
2\angle 0 + 3\angle(\pi/4) + 4\angle(-\pi/3)
$$

Write the full phasor-sum expression as $2\angle 0 + 3\angle(\pi/4) + 4\angle(-\pi/3)$. The plus signs mean ordinary complex addition, and the expression stays in phasor form rather than being collapsed into rectangular coordinates yet.

The result shows the rule scales unchanged from two terms to three: keep the common $\omega$, convert every cosine to its phasor, and add all of them together as a single complex sum.
