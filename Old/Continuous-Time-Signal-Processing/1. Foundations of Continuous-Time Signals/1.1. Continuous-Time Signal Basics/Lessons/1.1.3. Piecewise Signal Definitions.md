## Introduction to Piecewise Signal Definitions

A piecewise signal definition is a way to describe one continuous-time signal using different formulas on different time intervals. The object to recognize is the brace form: each row gives an expression for $x(t)$ and a condition on $t$ that says when that expression applies.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}\text{expression 1}, & \text{condition 1}\\\text{expression 2}, & \text{condition 2}\end{cases}
$$

To read a piecewise definition, choose the row whose condition contains the time value you care about, then use the expression from that row. To write a piecewise definition, assign one expression to each time interval and write the interval condition beside it.

For a simple base case, suppose a signal has value $1$ from $t=0$ up to, but not including, $t=2$, and has value $3$ from $t=2$ through $t=4$. The piecewise definition records both the values and the time intervals where those values apply.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}1, & 0\le t<2\\3, & 2\le t\le 4\end{cases}
$$

The expressions $1$ and $3$ are the signal values. The conditions $0\le t<2$ and $2\le t\le4$ are the time intervals. The endpoint $t=2$ belongs to the second row because the second condition includes $2$, while the first condition stops just before $2$.

Those endpoint details matter. The interval conditions should make it clear which expression controls each boundary time, without accidentally assigning the same time to two different rows or leaving a boundary time uncovered. In the examples that follow, the same piecewise format will be built from flat graph segments, sloped graph segments, endpoint markings, interval-based rules, and otherwise cases.

## Writing a Piecewise Definition from Constant Graph Segments

**Example:** Write a piecewise definition for the graphed signal shown.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.1.3. Piecewise Signal Definitions/Images/l003-s002-te-section-002.png>)

The cue is that the graph is made of horizontal segments. A horizontal segment gives a constant expression for $x(t)$ on the time interval covered by that segment.

The first segment has height $2$ and runs from $t=0$ up to, but not including, $t=3$. So the first piece is $2$ with condition $0\le t<3$.

The second segment has height $-1$ and runs from $t=3$ through $t=5$. So the second piece is $-1$ with condition $3\le t\le5$.

$$
2 \text{ for } 0\le t<3, \qquad -1 \text{ for } 3\le t\le5
$$

Putting those value-interval pairs into brace form gives the piecewise definition.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}2, & 0\le t<3\\-1, & 3\le t\le5\end{cases}
$$

Each row now matches one horizontal graph segment: the expression gives the segment's height, and the condition gives the time interval where that height applies.

## Writing a Piecewise Definition from Linear Graph Segments

**Example:** Write a piecewise definition for the graphed signal whose first segment runs from $(-2,0)$ to $(0,2)$ and whose second segment runs from $(0,2)$ to $(3,-1)$.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.1.3. Piecewise Signal Definitions/Images/l003-s003-te-section-003.png>)

The cue is that the graph is made of straight-line segments. Each segment needs its own line expression, then that expression gets paired with the time interval covered by the segment.

For the first segment, use the points $(-2,0)$ and $(0,2)$. The slope is $1$, so the line rises one unit in $x(t)$ for each one-unit increase in $t$. Since the segment passes through $(0,2)$, its expression is $t+2$.

$$
m_1=\frac{2-0}{0-(-2)}=1, \qquad x(t)=t+2 \text{ on } -2\le t<0
$$

For the second segment, use the points $(0,2)$ and $(3,-1)$. The slope is $-1$, so the line falls one unit in $x(t)$ for each one-unit increase in $t$. Since this segment also passes through $(0,2)$, its expression is $2-t$.

$$
m_2=\frac{-1-2}{3-0}=-1, \qquad x(t)=2-t \text{ on } 0\le t\le3
$$

Now put the two line expressions into one piecewise definition, with each expression attached to its time interval.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}t+2, & -2\le t<0\\2-t, & 0\le t\le3\end{cases}
$$

The structure is the same as with constant graph segments. The only added step is finding the line formula for each straight segment before writing the piecewise rows.

## Assigning Endpoint Conditions in a Piecewise Definition

**Example:** Choose the piecewise definition that matches the graphed signal, paying close attention to the open and closed endpoint markings at $t=1$.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.1.3. Piecewise Signal Definitions/Images/l003-s004-te-section-004.png>)

The new decision is not the value on each flat segment; it is which expression owns the boundary time. At $t=1$, the open circle is excluded and the closed dot is included.

The left segment has value $2$, but its endpoint at $t=1$ is open. That means the left condition should include times up to, but not including, $1$:

$$
2,\quad 0\le t<1
$$

The right segment has value $-1$, and its endpoint at $t=1$ is closed. That means the right condition includes $t=1$ and continues through the closed endpoint at $t=3$:

$$
-1,\quad 1\le t\le 3
$$

Putting the two expression-and-condition pairs into one piecewise definition gives

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}2, & 0\le t<1\\-1, & 1\le t\le 3.\end{cases}
$$

This definition assigns $t=1$ to exactly one row: the second row. It does not overlap the two pieces at the breakpoint, and it does not omit the boundary time.

## Writing a Piecewise Definition from Interval-Based Function Rules

**Example:** Write a piecewise definition for a signal that equals $2t+1$ for $-1\le t<1$ and equals $4-t$ for $1\le t\le3$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the signal is described by interval-based rules instead of by a graph. Each phrase of the form "equals this expression for this interval" becomes one row of the piecewise definition.

The first stated rule says the signal equals $2t+1$ for $-1\le t<1$. Copy both parts: the expression is $2t+1$, and the condition is $-1\le t<1$.

$$
2t+1,\quad -1\le t<1
$$

The second stated rule says the signal equals $4-t$ for $1\le t\le3$. Again, copy the expression and keep its interval condition attached to it.

$$
4-t,\quad 1\le t\le3
$$

Now place the two expression-and-condition pairs into brace form. No new formula needs to be inferred; the work is preserving the given rules in standard piecewise notation.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}2t+1, & -1\le t<1\\4-t, & 1\le t\le3\end{cases}
$$

The endpoint conditions have also been preserved: the first row stops before $t=1$, and the second row includes $t=1$. That means the boundary time is assigned to exactly the row specified by the original interval-based description.

<a id="adding-an-otherwise-case-to-a-piecewise-definition"></a>
## Adding an Otherwise Case to a Piecewise Definition

**Example:** Write a piecewise definition for a signal that equals $t+1$ for $-1\le t<0$, equals $2$ for $0\le t\le2$, and equals $0$ for all other times.

**Explanation**

The cue is the phrase "for all other times." That phrase means the piecewise definition needs a final row for every time not covered by the explicitly stated intervals.

First copy the explicitly stated pieces. The signal equals $t+1$ on $-1\le t<0$, and it equals $2$ on $0\le t\le2$.

$$
t+1,\quad -1\le t<0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad 2,\quad 0\le t\le2
$$

The description also says the signal equals $0$ for all other times. Instead of listing every outside interval separately, use an otherwise condition for the final row.

$$
0,\quad \text{otherwise}
$$

Now place the two stated interval rules and the otherwise rule into one complete piecewise definition.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}t+1, & -1\le t<0\\2, & 0\le t\le2\\0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

This keeps the original endpoint conditions unchanged: $t=0$ belongs to the second row, not the first. Every time outside the two stated intervals falls under the otherwise row, so the definition covers all time values.
