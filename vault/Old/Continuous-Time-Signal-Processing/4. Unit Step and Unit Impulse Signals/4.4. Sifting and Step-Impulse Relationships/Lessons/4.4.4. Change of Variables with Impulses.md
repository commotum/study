## Introduction to Change of Variables with Impulses

Start with the unit-slope sifting pattern: if the impulse is already written as $\delta(t-t_0)$, then $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=x(t_0)$. That is the standard form the later algebra must recover.

For a linear impulse argument $\delta(a t-b)$, first find the zero of the argument by solving $a t-b=0$. The sampled time is $t_0=b/a$, and the change of variables must preserve that time while turning the impulse into standard form.

Use the simplest nonunit case, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\delta(2t-4)\,dt$. Set $u=2t-4$, so $dt=du/2$, and rewrite the integral so the impulse becomes $\delta(u)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\delta(2t-4)\,dt &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\!\left(\frac{u+4}{2}\right)\delta(u)\,\frac{du}{2} \\ &= \frac{1}{2}x(2)\end{aligned}
$$

That same change-of-variables step gives the general positive-slope rule: the root $t_0=b/a$ is the sample point, and the slope contributes the inverse factor $1/|a|$.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\delta(a t-b)\,dt &= \frac{1}{|a|}x\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) \\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,\delta(-a t+b)\,dt &= \frac{1}{|a|}x\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)\end{aligned}
$$

Negative slope does not change the sampled time; it only changes the algebra of the substitution, which is why the absolute value appears. The rule to carry forward is simple: find the zero, convert to unit-impulse form, divide by the slope magnitude, and evaluate the signal at that sampled time.

## Converting a Positive Linear Impulse Argument

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y(t)\,\delta(4t-8)\,dt$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the linear impulse argument $4t-8$. Find its zero first: $4t-8=0$ gives $t=2$. That is the sampled time the change of variables must preserve.

Use the substitution $u=4t-8$, so $dt=du/4$. Then rewrite the integral so the impulse is in standard form $\delta(u)$ and the signal is evaluated at the transformed time.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y(t)\,\delta(4t-8)\,dt &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y\!\left(\frac{u+8}{4}\right)\delta(u)\,\frac{du}{4} \\ &= \frac{1}{4}y(2)\end{aligned}
$$

So the positive-slope linear impulse argument contributes the scale factor $1/4$, and the signal is sampled at the root $t=2$. The worked result is $\frac{1}{4}y(2)$.

## Evaluating the Signal Factor After the Variable Change

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(t^2+1)\,\delta(5t-10)\,dt$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the linear impulse argument $5t-10$. Solve $5t-10=0$ to get the sampled time $t=2$; that is the time the signal factor must be evaluated after the change of variables.

Use $u=5t-10$, so $dt=du/5$ and $t=(u+10)/5$. Because the signal factor is $t^2+1$, replace $t$ inside that factor before sifting.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(t^2+1)\,\delta(5t-10)\,dt &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{u+10}{5}\right)^2+1\right)\delta(u)\,\frac{du}{5} \\ &= \frac{1}{5}(2^2+1)=1\end{aligned}
$$

So the slope contributes the factor $1/5$, and the transformed signal is sampled at the root $t=2$. The worked result is $1$.

## Converting a Negative Linear Impulse Argument

**Example:** Find $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\delta(-3t+9)\,dt$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the negative-slope impulse argument $-3t+9$. Solve $-3t+9=0$ to get the sampled time $t=3$; the sign of the slope does not change the sample point.

Use $u=-3t+9$, so $dt=-du/3$ and $t=(9-u)/3$. The minus sign in $dt$ is the sign bookkeeping that will turn into the absolute scale factor.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\delta(-3t+9)\,dt &= \int_{\infty}^{-\infty}x\!\left(\frac{9-u}{3}\right)\delta(u)\left(-\frac{du}{3}\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{3}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\!\left(\frac{9-u}{3}\right)\delta(u)\,du \\ &= \frac{1}{3}x(3)\end{aligned}
$$

So the negative slope still gives a positive factor $1/3$, and the signal is sampled at the same root $t=3$. The worked result is $\frac{1}{3}x(3)$.

## Checking Definite Integral Bounds After the Variable Change

**Example:** Compute $\int_{0}^{2}x(t)\,\delta(4t-2)\,dt$ and explain why the bounds matter.

**Explanation**

The cue is the sampled time from $4t-2=0$. Solve first: $t_0=1/2$. Because $1/2$ lies inside the interval $[0,2]$, the impulse contributes; if the sampled time were outside the interval, the definite integral would be $0$.

Use $u=4t-2$, so $dt=du/4$ and $t=(u+2)/4$. The finite bounds move with the substitution, but the $\delta$ still samples only when the transformed interval contains $u=0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}\int_{0}^{2}x(t)\,\delta(4t-2)\,dt &= \int_{-2}^{6}x\!\left(\frac{u+2}{4}\right)\delta(u)\,\frac{du}{4} \\ &= \frac{1}{4}x\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\end{aligned}
$$

So the bounds act as an inclusion check: here $t_0=1/2$ is inside $[0,2]$, so the scaled result is $\frac{1}{4}x(1/2)$. If the sampled time missed the interval, the answer would be $0$.
