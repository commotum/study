## Introduction to Evaluating Integrals with Shifted Impulses

A shifted impulse $\delta(\tau-t_0)$ tells you where to sample the signal inside the integral. The key cue is the impulse location $t_0$, because that is the only time value the impulse preserves.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-t_0)\,d\tau = x(t_0)
$$

For a finite interval, first check whether the impulse time lies inside the limits $[a,b]$. If $t_0\in[a,b]$, the integral returns the sample $x(t_0)$; if $t_0$ is outside the interval, the impulse contributes $0$.

$$
\int_a^b x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-t_0)\,d\tau = \begin{cases}x(t_0), & a\le t_0\le b \\ 0, & t_0<a \text{ or } t_0>b\end{cases}
$$

So the procedure is simple: read the impulse center first, test it against the integration limits, and then substitute that time into $x(\tau)$ only when the impulse sits inside the interval. If more than one shifted impulse appears later, apply the same rule term by term.

## Evaluating an Integral When the Impulse Time Is Inside the Limits

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-2}^{5} 3\tau\,\delta(\tau-1)\,d\tau$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shifted impulse at $\tau=1$, and $1$ lies inside the integration limits $[-2,5]$. That means the inside-case rule applies: sample the signal at the impulse time instead of integrating the signal normally.

$$
\int_{-2}^{5} x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-t_0)\,d\tau = x(t_0)\quad\text{when }a\le t_0\le b
$$

Here the signal factor is $x(\tau)=3\tau$, so the rule gives $x(1)$. The impulse does not change the signal shape; it only selects the value at the impulse location.

$$
x(1)=3\cdot 1=3
$$

So the integral evaluates to $3$. The same one-line substitution is the whole move when the impulse time is inside the limits.

## Evaluating an Integral When the Impulse Time Is Outside the Limits

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-2}^{1} \cos(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-3)d\tau$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shifted impulse at $\tau=3$, with $x(\tau)=\cos(\tau)$. The interval test is the only decision here: $3$ is not in $[-2,1]$, so the impulse sits outside the integration window.

$$
3\notin[-2,1]
$$

When the impulse time is outside the limits, there is no sampled value to return, so the integral is zero.

$$
\int_{-2}^{1} \cos(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-3)d\tau = 0
$$

That is the entire outside-case branch: check the limits first, then return $0$ if the impulse location is not included.

## Evaluating Integrals with Multiple Shifted Impulses

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-2}^{3} (\tau+2)\,\delta(\tau-1)d\tau + 2\int_{-2}^{3} \delta(\tau+4)d\tau - \int_{-2}^{3} 5\,\delta(\tau-\tau_0)d\tau$ with $\tau_0=4$.

**Explanation**

The cue is linearity: split the expression into separate shifted-impulse terms and test each impulse time against the same limits $[-2,3]$. The impulse times are $1$, $-4$, and $4$, so only the first term lies inside the interval.

$$
1\in[-2,3],\quad -4\notin[-2,3],\quad 4\notin[-2,3]
$$

The inside term samples its paired signal factor at $\tau=1$, so $\int_{-2}^{3} (\tau+2)\,\delta(\tau-1)d\tau = 1+2 = 3$. The other two terms contribute $0$ because their impulses are outside the interval.

$$
\int_{-2}^{3} (\tau+2)\,\delta(\tau-1)d\tau = 3,\quad 2\int_{-2}^{3} \delta(\tau+4)d\tau = 0,\quad \int_{-2}^{3} 5\,\delta(\tau-4)d\tau = 0
$$

So the whole expression evaluates to $3$. The rule is still the same one impulse at a time: check the bounds, sample the inside terms, and drop the outside terms.
