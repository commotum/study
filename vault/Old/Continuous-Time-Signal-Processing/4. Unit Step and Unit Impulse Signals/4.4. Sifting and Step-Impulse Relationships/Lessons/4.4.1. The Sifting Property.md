## Introduction to The Sifting Property

When you see $x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-t_0)$ inside an integral, read the shifted impulse as a sampling cue. It marks the one time that survives, $\tau=t_0$, and tells you to keep the value of $x(\tau)$ there.

The clean base case is the impulse at the origin. If the center is $\tau=0$, the integral returns the signal value at $0$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau)\,d\tau = x(0)
$$

The shifted version keeps the same rule. Moving the impulse center from $0$ to $t_0$ moves the sampled value with it.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\,\delta(\tau-t_0)\,d\tau = x(t_0)
$$

So the sifting property is a substitution pattern, not a rule for integrating $x(\tau)$ term by term. Identify the impulse location, substitute that time into $x(\tau)$, and write the sampled result directly as $x(t_0)$.

<a id="sifting-a-signal-with-an-impulse-at-the-origin"></a>
## Sifting a Signal with an Impulse at the Origin

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\tau^2+4\tau+1)\,\delta(\tau)\,d\tau$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the origin-centered impulse $\delta(\tau)$, so this is a sampling problem at $\tau=0$. By the sifting property, the impulse keeps only the signal value at the origin.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\tau^2+4\tau+1)\,\delta(\tau)\,d\tau = x(0)
$$

Here $x(\tau)=\tau^2+4\tau+1$, so the sample at the origin is found by substituting $\tau=0$ into the signal expression.

$$
x(0)=0^2+4(0)+1=1
$$

So the integral evaluates to $1$. The impulse does not make you integrate the polynomial term by term; it makes you read off the sampled value at the impulse location.

## Sifting a Signal with a Shifted Impulse

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\cos\tau+2)\,\delta(\tau-1.5)\,d\tau$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shifted impulse $\delta(\tau-1.5)$, so the sample point is $t_0=1.5$. By the sifting property, the integral keeps only the value of the signal at that time.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\cos\tau+2)\,\delta(\tau-1.5)\,d\tau = x(1.5)
$$

Here $x(\tau)=\cos\tau+2$, so substitute $\tau=1.5$ into the signal expression.

$$
x(1.5)=\cos(1.5)+2
$$

So the integral evaluates to $\cos(1.5)+2$. The shift in the impulse simply moves the sampling location from $0$ to $1.5$.

## Sifting a Time-Domain Formula at the Impulse Location

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\tau^3+\tau-\frac{1}{\tau})\,\delta(\tau+1)\,d\tau$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the shifted impulse $\delta(\tau+1)$, which is the same as $\delta(\tau-(-1))$. So the sample point is $t_0=-1$.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\tau^3+\tau-\frac{1}{\tau})\,\delta(\tau+1)\,d\tau = x(-1)
$$

Here $x(\tau)=\tau^3+\tau-\frac{1}{\tau}$, so substitute $\tau=-1$ into the signal expression.

$$
\begin{aligned}x(-1) &= (-1)^3+(-1)-\frac{1}{-1} \\ &= -1-1+1 \\ &= -1\end{aligned}
$$

So the integral evaluates to $-1$. The shifted impulse simply moves the sampling location from $0$ to $-1$.
