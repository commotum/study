## Introduction to Unit Impulse as the Derivative of a Unit Step

A derivative is the right tool when a signal has an abrupt jump. For a unit step $u(t)$, that jump happens at the switching time, so the derivative is represented by a unit impulse at the same location.

Start with the base case. Differentiate the unshifted step $u(t)$ and read the result as the origin impulse $\delta(t)$. This is the identity to remember when a step transition becomes an impulse form.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=\delta(t)
$$

The same reading rule applies after a shift. If the step is $u(t-t_0)$, the jump occurs at $t=t_0$, so the impulse moves to the same time. In practice, you locate the impulse by reading the step argument.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u(t-t_0)=\delta(t-t_0)
$$

A constant scale factor passes straight through by linearity, so the weighted shifted step $A\,u(t-t_0)$ differentiates to $A\,\delta(t-t_0)$. That is the compact form you will reuse in later examples: keep the same shift, keep the same scale, and replace the jump with an impulse.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}[A\,u(t-t_0)]=A\,\delta(t-t_0)
$$

## Differentiating the Basic Unit Step

**Example:** Differentiate $u(t)$.

**Explanation**

Differentiate the basic unit step $u(t)$. The cue is the jump at $t=0$, so we apply the tutorial rule that a step transition differentiates to an impulse at the same location.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=\delta(t)
$$

Because the step is flat everywhere except at the jump, the derivative is zero away from $t=0$ and concentrated entirely at the origin. The result is the unit impulse $\delta(t)$ with unit weight.

## Differentiating a Shifted Unit Step

**Example:** Differentiate $u(t-3)$.

**Explanation**

Differentiate the shifted step $u(t-3)$. The cue is the jump at $t=3$, so we use the same rule as before: a step transition differentiates to an impulse at its switch time.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u(t-3)=\delta(t-3)
$$

The shift stays in the impulse location, so the result is centered at $t=3$ instead of at the origin. The final answer is the unit impulse $\delta(t-3)$ with unit weight.

## Differentiating a Scaled Shifted Unit Step

**Example:** Differentiate $5u(t-2)$.

**Explanation**

Differentiate the scaled shifted step $5u(t-2)$. The cue is the jump at $t=2$, and the factor $5$ is a constant gain, so we pull it outside the derivative before applying the step rule.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}[5u(t-2)] = 5\frac{d}{dt}u(t-2) = 5\delta(t-2)
$$

The shift stays with the impulse, so the result is centered at $t=2$. Reattaching the constant gain gives the final answer $5\delta(t-2)$, with the factor multiplying the impulse and not the argument.

## Differentiating Sums of Shifted Unit Steps

**Example:** Differentiate $u(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}u(t+2)+3u(t-4)$.

**Explanation**

Differentiate the sum of shifted steps term by term. The cue is the three separate jump times at $t=1$, $t=-2$, and $t=4$, so each step becomes an impulse at its own switch location.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\Big[u(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}u(t+2)+3u(t-4)\Big] = \frac{d}{dt}u(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}u(t+2)+3\frac{d}{dt}u(t-4)
$$

Now apply the shifted-step rule to each term and keep the coefficients attached to their own impulses. The linearity of differentiation lets you preserve the order of the terms while converting each jump into the matching impulse.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\Big[u(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}u(t+2)+3u(t-4)\Big] = \delta(t-1)-\frac{1}{2}\delta(t+2)+3\delta(t-4)
$$

The result is a sum of shifted impulses, one for each step in the original expression. Each impulse stays at the same switch time as its step, and each coefficient stays with the term it came from.
