## Introduction to Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution

The convolution setup already tells us where the two factors are nonzero at a fixed time t. The geometric area method starts with that overlap interval on the $\tau$-axis and turns it directly into the value of the convolution.

$$
y(t)=(x*h)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

In the fixed-time picture, $x(\tau)$ stays fixed while $h(t - \tau)$ is the shifted pulse. Only the $\tau$-values where both rectangles overlap contribute to the integral.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s001-te-section-001.png>)

Use the simplest case first: suppose the two positive rectangular pulses overlap on one interval of length $L(t)$. If $x(\tau)$ has height A and $h(t - \tau)$ has height B on that overlap, then the product $x(\tau)$$h(t - \tau)$ is constant there with height AB.

So the integral over the nonzero overlap is just the area of a rectangle. Its width is the overlap length, and its height is the product of the pulse amplitudes.

$$
y(t)=AB\,L(t)
$$

As t changes, the same area rule is reused. The overlap length can grow, stay constant during full overlap, or shrink, and those three behaviors become the pieces of the triangular or trapezoidal convolution output.

<a id="converting-an-overlap-interval-into-rectangular-area"></a>
## Converting an Overlap Interval into Rectangular Area

**Example:** At a fixed time, $x(\tau)$ has height 2, $h(t - \tau)$ has height 3, and the two pulses overlap only on $1 \le \tau \le 4$. Use the geometric area method to find the convolution value $y(t)$ at that time.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the overlap interval is already known. Since the factors are rectangular pulses, the convolution value at this fixed time is the area of one rectangle: overlap width times the product of the pulse heights.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s002-te-section-002.png>)

First find the width of the shaded overlap. The overlap runs from $\tau = 1$ to $\tau = 4$, so its width is right endpoint minus left endpoint.

$$
L=4-1=3
$$

Next find the height of the rectangle in the integrand. On the overlap, $x(\tau)$ has height 2 and $h(t - \tau)$ has height 3, so their product is constant with height 6.

$$
x(\tau)h(t-\tau)=2\cdot 3=6 \quad \text{for } 1\le \tau\le 4
$$

Now multiply the rectangle height by the overlap width. That area is the value of the convolution at this fixed time.

$$
y(t)=\int_1^4 6\,d\tau=6(4-1)=18
$$

So the convolution value at this fixed time is 18.

<a id="finding-growing-and-shrinking-areas-for-a-triangular-output"></a>
## Finding Growing and Shrinking Areas for a Triangular Output

**Example:** Two unit-height rectangular pulses each have width 2. In the $\tau$-axis picture, $x(\tau)$ is supported on $0 \le \tau \le 2$ and $h(t - \tau)$ is supported on $t - 2 \le \tau \le t$. Use geometric area to find the triangular convolution output over the times when the pulses overlap.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the overlap interval is no longer fixed. The same area rule still applies, but the overlap width now changes with t. Since both pulse heights are 1, the convolution value equals the overlap length.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s003-te-section-003.png>)

Start by locating the three breakpoints. The shifted support first touches the fixed support at $t = 0$, fully aligns at $t = 2$, and separates at $t = 4$.

$$
\operatorname{supp}x(\tau)=[0,2],\qquad \operatorname{supp}h(t-\tau)=[t-2,t]
$$

On the growing interval, the overlap runs from $\tau = 0$ to $\tau = t$. Its width is t, and the unit height product leaves the area equal to that width.

$$
y(t)=1\cdot t=t \quad \text{for } 0\le t\le 2
$$

On the shrinking interval, the overlap runs from $\tau = t - 2$ to $\tau = 2$. Its width is the fixed right endpoint minus the moving left endpoint.

$$
L(t)=2-(t-2)=4-t
$$

Again the unit height product does not change the area, so the falling branch is the shrinking overlap length.

$$
y(t)=1\cdot(4-t)=4-t \quad \text{for } 2\le t\le 4
$$

Outside the contact interval, the overlap length is zero. Putting the growing and shrinking areas together gives the triangular convolution output.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0,\\ t, & 0\le t\le 2,\\ 4-t, & 2\le t\le 4,\\ 0, & t>4.\end{cases}
$$

<a id="finding-constant-full-overlap-area-for-a-trapezoidal-output"></a>
## Finding Constant Full-Overlap Area for a Trapezoidal Output

**Example:** A unit-height rectangular pulse of width 4 is convolved with a unit-height rectangular pulse of width 2. In the $\tau$-axis picture, let $x(\tau)$ be supported on $0 \le \tau \le 4$ and $h(t - \tau)$ be supported on $t - 2 \le \tau \le t$. Use geometric area to find the trapezoidal output over the nonzero time interval.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the pulse widths are unequal. The overlap can grow, then hold constant while the shorter pulse is fully inside the longer pulse, then shrink. Since both heights are 1, the convolution value is still just the overlap length.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s004-te-section-004.png>)

Use the supports to mark the case changes. First contact occurs at $t = 0$. The shorter pulse becomes fully overlapped at $t = 2$, stops being fully overlapped at $t = 4$, and separates at $t = 6$.

$$
\operatorname{supp}x(\tau)=[0,4],\qquad \operatorname{supp}h(t-\tau)=[t-2,t]
$$

On the growing interval, the overlap runs from $\tau = 0$ to $\tau = t$. Its width is t, so the area grows linearly.

$$
y(t)=t \quad \text{for } 0\le t\le 2
$$

On the full-overlap interval, the entire shorter pulse is inside the longer pulse. The overlap length is not changing; it is the shorter width, 2.

$$
y(t)=2 \quad \text{for } 2\le t\le 4
$$

On the shrinking interval, the overlap runs from $\tau = t - 2$ to $\tau = 4$. Its width is the fixed right edge minus the moving left edge.

$$
L(t)=4-(t-2)=6-t
$$

Because the heights are unit heights, that shrinking length is the falling branch of the output. Outside $0 \le t \le 6$, there is no overlap, so the output is zero.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}t, & 0\le t\le 2,\\ 2, & 2\le t\le 4,\\ 6-t, & 4\le t\le 6.\end{cases}
$$

<a id="applying-pulse-amplitudes-to-geometric-area"></a>
## Applying Pulse Amplitudes to Geometric Area

**Example:** Two rectangular pulses have heights 2 and 3. Their overlap length is t for $0 \le t \le 2$ and 4 - t for $2 \le t \le 4$. Use geometric area to write the convolution output on the nonzero intervals.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the overlap-length pieces are already known. This section does not change the time intervals or recompute the geometry; it only scales each overlap length by the product of the pulse heights.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s005-te-section-005.png>)

Start with the two overlap-length pieces from the geometry. These are the widths of the shaded overlap region on the two nonzero intervals.

$$
L(t)=t \quad \text{for } 0\le t\le 2,\qquad L(t)=4-t \quad \text{for } 2\le t\le 4
$$

Now compute the height of the rectangle in the integrand. Wherever the pulses overlap, $x(\tau)$ has height 2 and $h(t - \tau)$ has height 3, so their product has height 6.

$$
2\cdot 3=6
$$

Use the area rule from the earlier sections: convolution value equals product height times overlap length. The amplitude product multiplies both pieces and leaves the intervals unchanged.

$$
y(t)=6L(t)
$$

Substitute each overlap-length expression into that rule to get the scaled output on the nonzero intervals.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}6t, & 0\le t\le 2,\\ 6(4-t), & 2\le t\le 4.\end{cases}
$$

The breakpoints stay at $t = 0$, $t = 2$, and $t = 4$. Only the vertical scale changes, because the heights multiply the overlap area by 6.

<a id="assembling-the-piecewise-convolution-from-area-cases"></a>
## Assembling the Piecewise Convolution from Area Cases

**Example:** Let $x(\tau)$ have height 2 on $0 \le \tau \le 3$, and let $h(t - \tau)$ have height 1 on $0 \le t - \tau \le 1$. Use geometric area cases to assemble the complete piecewise formula for $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that this is an assembly problem. Use the support endpoints to find the time cases, use the overlap length in each case, and multiply every nonzero area by the amplitude product.

![](<../Source/7.2.5. Geometric Area Methods for Rectangular Pulse Convolution/Images/l005-s006-te-section-006.png>)

First write the two supports in the $\tau$-axis picture. The fixed pulse is on [0, 3], and the shifted pulse has width 1 with support [t - 1, t].

$$
\operatorname{supp}x(\tau)=[0,3],\qquad \operatorname{supp}h(t-\tau)=[t-1,t]
$$

The case changes happen at first contact, start of full overlap, end of full overlap, and final separation. Those breakpoints are $t = 0$, $t = 1$, $t = 3$, and $t = 4$.

$$
0,\;1,\;3,\;4
$$

Next compute the amplitude product. Wherever the pulses overlap, the integrand height is the product of the two rectangular pulse heights.

$$
2\cdot 1=2
$$

On the growing interval, the overlap is [0, t], so the overlap length is t. Multiplying by the amplitude product gives the first nonzero piece.

$$
y(t)=2t \quad \text{for } 0\le t\le 1
$$

On the full-overlap interval, the entire width-1 pulse is inside the width-3 pulse. The overlap length is constant at 1, so the area is constant.

$$
y(t)=2\cdot 1=2 \quad \text{for } 1\le t\le 3
$$

On the shrinking interval, the overlap is [t - 1, 3]. Its length is the fixed right endpoint minus the moving left endpoint.

$$
L(t)=3-(t-1)=4-t
$$

Multiplying that shrinking length by the amplitude product gives the final nonzero piece. Before first contact and after final separation, the overlap length is zero.

$$
y(t)=2(4-t) \quad \text{for } 3\le t\le 4
$$

Now place all pieces in increasing order of t to get the complete piecewise convolution output.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0,\\ 2t, & 0\le t\le 1,\\ 2, & 1\le t\le 3,\\ 2(4-t), & 3\le t\le 4,\\ 0, & t>4.\end{cases}
$$
