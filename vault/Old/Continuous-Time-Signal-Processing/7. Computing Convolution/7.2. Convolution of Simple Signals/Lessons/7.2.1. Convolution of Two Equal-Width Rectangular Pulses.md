## Introduction to Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses

For two equal-width rectangular pulses, the convolution is easiest to read in the convolution variable $\tau$. Think of $x(\tau)$ as the fixed rectangle, and think of $h(t - \tau)$ as the rectangle that slides across it as t changes.

$$
y(t) = (x * h)(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t - \tau)\,d\tau
$$

Use the base case where both pulses have width T. The fixed pulse $x(\tau)$ is nonzero on $0 \le \tau \le T$, while the shifted pulse $h(t - \tau)$ is nonzero on $t - T \le \tau \le t$. The only part of the integral that contributes is the $\tau$-interval where these two supports overlap.

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s001-te-section-001.png>)

Because rectangular pulses are constant on their supports, their product is also constant wherever they overlap. If the pulse heights are A and B, then the integrand equals AB on the overlap and equals 0 outside the overlap.

$$
y(t) = AB\,L(t)
$$

$$
L(t) = \text{length of }(\operatorname{supp} x) \cap (\operatorname{supp} h(t - \tau))
$$

So the main action is not to expand the integral right away. First track the overlap length $L(t)$. For equal widths, there are three key events: first contact, peak alignment, and last contact. The overlap length starts at 0, grows until the rectangles fully align, and then shrinks back to 0.

That rising-then-falling overlap length is why the convolution output is triangular. Later sections will compute the contact times, the rising length, the falling length, and the height scaling one piece at a time.

## Locating First Contact, Peak Alignment, and Last Contact

**Example:** For unit-height rectangular pulses $x(\tau) = 1$ on 0 $\le$ $\tau$ $\le$ T and $h(\tau) = 1$ on 0 $\le$ $\tau$ $\le$ T, locate first contact, peak alignment, and last contact for $h(t - \tau)$ as t varies.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the two pulses have equal width. Before computing any overlap length, track only the two support intervals on the $\tau$-axis and find the times when their edge relationship changes.

$$
x(\tau) \ne 0 \text{ on } [0,T]
$$

Since h is nonzero on [0, T], the shifted factor $h(t - \tau)$ is nonzero when t - $\tau$ lies between 0 and T. Solving that inequality gives the moving support in the $\tau$ variable.

$$
0 \le t-\tau \le T \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad t-T \le \tau \le t
$$

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s002-te-section-002.png>)

First contact occurs when the right edge of the moving support, $\tau = t$, just reaches the left edge of the fixed support at $\tau = 0$. That edge equation gives the first breakpoint.

$$
t = 0
$$

Peak alignment occurs when the two equal-width supports match exactly. For [t - T, t] to equal [0, T], the left edges and right edges must line up at the same time.

$$
[t-T,t] = [0,T] \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = T
$$

Last contact occurs when the left edge of the moving support, $\tau = t - T$, just reaches the right edge of the fixed support at $\tau = T$. After this time, the supports no longer overlap.

$$
t - T = T \quad \Rightarrow \quad t = 2T
$$

So the ordered breakpoints are first contact at $t = 0$, peak alignment at $t = T$, and last contact at $t = 2T$. These breakpoints divide the time axis into no overlap, growing overlap, peak alignment, shrinking overlap, and no overlap; the actual overlap-length formulas come after this edge map is in place.

<a id="computing-the-rising-overlap-length"></a>
## Computing the Rising Overlap Length

**Example:** For two unit-height rectangular pulses supported on [0, T], compute the overlap length for 0 $\le$ t $\le$ T.

**Explanation**

The cue is the rising interval: t is between first contact and peak alignment. On this interval, the shifted rectangle has started entering the fixed rectangle, but it has not fully aligned yet.

$$
\operatorname{supp} x(\tau) = [0,T], \qquad \operatorname{supp} h(t-\tau) = [t-T,t]
$$

Restrict to $0 \le t \le T$. In this range, the moving left edge t - T is at or to the left of 0, while the moving right edge t is inside the fixed support [0, T]. So the common part starts at 0 and ends at t.

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s003-te-section-003.png>)

$$
[0,T] \cap [t-T,t] = [0,t], \qquad 0 \le t \le T
$$

The overlap length is the right endpoint minus the left endpoint. Here that means subtracting 0 from t.

$$
L(t) = t - 0 = t, \qquad 0 \le t \le T
$$

Thus the rising overlap length is $L(t) = t$. It starts at 0 when $t = 0$ and reaches the full width T when $t = T$, matching the entering edge sweeping across the fixed pulse.

## Computing the Falling Overlap Length

**Example:** For two unit-height rectangular pulses supported on [0, T], compute the overlap length for T $\le$ t $\le$ 2T.

**Explanation**

The cue is the falling interval: t is between peak alignment and last contact. The shifted rectangle is now leaving the fixed rectangle, so the overlap should shrink as t increases.

$$
\operatorname{supp} x(\tau) = [0,T], \qquad \operatorname{supp} h(t-\tau) = [t-T,t]
$$

Restrict to $T \le t \le 2T$. In this range, the moving left edge $t - T$ lies inside the fixed support $[0, T]$, while the moving right edge $t$ is at or to the right of $T$. So the common part starts at $t - T$ and ends at $T$.

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s004-te-section-004.png>)

$$
[0,T] \cap [t-T,t] = [t-T,T], \qquad T \le t \le 2T
$$

The overlap length is still right endpoint minus left endpoint. In the falling region, the right endpoint is T and the left endpoint is t - T.

$$
L(t) = T - (t - T) = 2T - t, \qquad T \le t \le 2T
$$

Thus the falling overlap length is $L(t) = 2T - t$. At $t = T$, it equals $T$, and at $t = 2T$, it equals $0$, so the formula matches the rectangle leaving the fixed support.

<a id="scaling-the-overlap-length-by-rectangular-pulse-heights"></a>
## Scaling the Overlap Length by Rectangular Pulse Heights

**Example:** If $x(t)$ has height A and $h(t)$ has height B, and both pulses have width T, compute the convolution value from the overlap length on the rising and falling regions.

**Explanation**

The new cue is nonunit pulse height. The overlap lengths from the previous sections do not change; only the height of the product inside the overlap changes.

$$
x(\tau)h(t-\tau)=AB \quad \text{where the supports overlap}
$$

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s005-te-section-005.png>)

Because the integrand is constant over the overlap interval, the convolution value is the product height AB times the overlap length $L(t)$.

$$
y(t)=AB\,L(t)
$$

On the rising region, the overlap length is $L(t) = t$. Substitute that length into the scaling rule.

$$
y(t)=ABt, \qquad 0 \le t \le T
$$

On the falling region, the overlap length is $L(t) = 2T - t$. Use the same height product; only the length expression changes.

$$
y(t)=AB(2T-t), \qquad T \le t \le 2T
$$

At peak alignment, both formulas give $y(T)$ = ABT. The heights scale the vertical value of the convolution, but they do not move the contact times or change the overlap-length formulas.

## Writing the Piecewise Triangular Convolution Output

**Example:** Write the complete convolution $y(t)$ for two equal-width rectangular pulses of heights A and B supported on intervals of width T.

**Explanation**

The cue is final assembly. Use the contact times, the overlap-length formulas, and the height scaling rule from the earlier sections, then place each formula on its own time region.

$$
t<0, \qquad 0\le t\le T, \qquad T\le t\le 2T, \qquad t>2T
$$

Before first contact and after last contact, the supports do not overlap, so the convolution value is $0$. On the rising region, use $y(t) = ABt$. On the falling region, use $y(t) = AB(2T - t)$.

![](<../Source/7.2.1. Convolution of Two Equal-Width Rectangular Pulses/Images/l001-s006-te-section-006.png>)

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0 \\ ABt, & 0\le t\le T \\ AB(2T-t), & T\le t\le 2T \\ 0, & t>2T\end{cases}
$$

The two active branches meet at $t = T$ because $ABt$ and $AB(2T - t)$ both give $ABT$ there. So the output rises linearly to the peak $ABT$, then falls linearly back to $0$, forming the triangular convolution output.
