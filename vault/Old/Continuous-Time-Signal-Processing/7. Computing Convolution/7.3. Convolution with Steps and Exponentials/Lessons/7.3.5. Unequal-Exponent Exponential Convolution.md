## Introduction to Unequal-Exponent Exponential Convolution

Use the unequal-exponent case when both signals are one-sided exponentials, but their decay rates are different. The standard form is

$$
x(t)=e^{-at}u(t),\qquad h(t)=e^{-bt}u(t),\qquad a>0,\ b>0,\ a\ne b.
$$

Both unit steps make the signals causal. Inside the convolution integral, $x(\tau)$ can contribute only when $\tau \ge 0$, and $h(t - \tau)$ can contribute only when $t - \tau \ge 0$. For $t \ge 0$, those two conditions give the overlap interval $0 \le \tau \le t$.

$$
(x*h)(t)=\int_0^t e^{-a\tau}e^{-b(t-\tau)}\,d\tau,\qquad t\ge 0.
$$

The new feature is the unequal exponent. In the equal-exponent case, the $\tau$ terms cancel and the integral produces a ramp-weighted exponential. Here the product keeps a $\tau$-dependent exponential, so the evaluated result becomes a difference of two decays.

$$
(x*h)(t)=\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-bt}}{b-a}\,u(t),\qquad a\ne b.
$$

When you use this pattern, keep the order consistent: the numerator $e^{-at}$-$e^{-bt}$ goes with the denominator b-a. The final $u(t)$ records the no-overlap region, so the output is zero for $t < 0$.

<a id="setting-causal-overlap-bounds-for-unequal-exponentials"></a>
## Setting Causal Overlap Bounds for Unequal Exponentials

**Example:** For $x(t)=e$^{-2t}$u(t)$ and $h(t)=e$^{-5t}$u(t)$, set the causal overlap bounds for (x*h)(t).

**Explanation**

The cue is that both signals are one-sided: each formula contains a unit step. In a convolution, rewrite one signal using $\tau$ and rewrite the shifted signal using t-$\tau$.

$$
x(\tau)=e^{-2\tau}u(\tau),\qquad h(t-\tau)=e^{-5(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)
$$

Use only the unit-step factors to set the bounds. The exponent values 2 and 5 change the integrand, but they do not create integration limits.

$$
u(\tau)\ne 0 \text{ only when } \tau\ge 0,\qquad u(t-\tau)\ne 0 \text{ only when } t-\tau\ge 0
$$

The second condition is $\tau$$\le$ t. Combining it with $\tau$$\ge$ 0 gives a nonempty overlap exactly when t$\ge$ 0.

$$
t-\tau\ge 0 \Longleftrightarrow \tau\le t,\qquad 0\le \tau\le t\quad \text{for } t\ge 0
$$

So the output has no causal overlap for $t<0$. For t$\ge$ 0, the convolution integral should run from $\tau$=0 to $\tau$=t.

<a id="multiplying-unequal-exponential-terms-inside-the-integral"></a>
## Multiplying Unequal Exponential Terms Inside the Integral

**Example:** For $x(t)=e$^{-2t}$u(t)$ and $h(t)=e$^{-5t}$u(t)$, simplify the product $x(\tau)$$h(t-\tau)$ on the causal overlap interval.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same overlap interval from the previous step: for t$\ge$ 0, the product is active only on 0$\le$ $\tau$$\le$ t. On that interval, use the exponential parts of $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$.

$$
x(\tau)h(t-\tau)=e^{-2\tau}e^{-5(t-\tau)},\qquad 0\le \tau\le t
$$

Now distribute the shifted exponent carefully. The negative sign applies to both terms inside t-$\tau$.

$$
-5(t-\tau)=-5t+5\tau
$$

Substitute that back into the product and combine the $\tau$ exponents. Keep the t-only factor separate from the $\tau$-dependent factor.

$$
e^{-2\tau}e^{-5(t-\tau)}=e^{-2\tau}e^{-5t+5\tau}=e^{-5t}e^{(-2+5)\tau}=e^{-5t}e^{3\tau}
$$

So the simplified product is $e^{-5t}$$e^{3\tau}$. Unlike the equal-exponent case, the integrand still contains a $\tau$-dependent exponential factor.

<a id="evaluating-the-unequal-exponent-overlap-integral"></a>
## Evaluating the Unequal-Exponent Overlap Integral

**Example:** Evaluate int_0^t $e^{-5t}$$e^{3 \tau}$ d $\tau$ for $t \ge 0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the variable of integration. The integral is with respect to $\tau$, so t is fixed during this step. That makes $e^{-5t}$ a constant factor.

$$
\int_0^t e^{-5t}e^{3\tau}\,d\tau=e^{-5t}\int_0^t e^{3\tau}\,d\tau
$$

Now antidifferentiate the remaining $\tau$-dependent exponential. Because the exponent is $3\tau$, divide by $3$.

$$
e^{-5t}\int_0^t e^{3\tau}\,d\tau=e^{-5t}\left[\frac{e^{3\tau}}{3}\right]_{\tau=0}^{\tau=t}
$$

Evaluate both limits. The upper limit replaces $\tau$ with $t$, and the lower limit gives $e^0=1$.

$$
e^{-5t}\left(\frac{e^{3t}}{3}-\frac{e^0}{3}\right)=\frac{e^{-5t}(e^{3t}-1)}{3}
$$

So the evaluated overlap integral is $e^{-5t}(e^{3t}-1)/3$. Leave it in this factored form for now; rewriting it as a scaled difference of exponentials is the next algebra step.

<a id="simplifying-to-a-scaled-difference-of-exponentials"></a>
## Simplifying to a Scaled Difference of Exponentials

**Example:** Simplify $e^{-5t}$($e^{3t}$-1)/3 as a scaled difference of exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is the factored result from the evaluated integral. To get the scaled difference form, distribute $e^{-5t}$ across the parentheses while keeping the outside factor 1/3.

$$
\frac{e^{-5t}(e^{3t}-1)}{3}=\frac{e^{-5t}e^{3t}-e^{-5t}}{3}
$$

Now combine the exponents in the first term. Multiplying exponentials with the same base means adding their exponents.

$$
e^{-5t}e^{3t}=e^{(-5+3)t}=e^{-2t}
$$

Substitute that simplification back into the expression. The lower-limit term stays negative, and the scale factor stays in the denominator.

$$
\frac{e^{-5t}e^{3t}-e^{-5t}}{3}=\frac{e^{-2t}-e^{-5t}}{3}
$$

So the scaled difference of exponentials is ($e^{-2t}$-$e^{-5t}$)/3. Do not attach the causal unit step in this step; that belongs when writing the final output.

<a id="writing-the-final-result-as-a-causal-output"></a>
## Writing the Final Result as a Causal Output

**Example:** Find (x*h)(t) for $x(t)=e$^{-2t}$u(t)$ and $h(t)=e$^{-5t}$u(t)$, including the causal unit step.

**Explanation**

The cue is causal support. Since both signals are one-sided, there is no overlap before $t=0$.

$$
(x*h)(t)=0,\qquad t<0
$$

For t$\ge$ 0, use the overlap interval 0$\le$ $\tau$$\le$ t and the scaled difference already obtained from the unequal-exponent integral.

$$
(x*h)(t)=\frac{e^{-2t}-e^{-5t}}{3},\qquad t\ge 0
$$

To write one causal formula instead of a piecewise result, attach $u(t)$. The unit step keeps the expression active for t$\ge$ 0 and zero for $t<0$.

$$
(x*h)(t)=\frac{e^{-2t}-e^{-5t}}{3}\,u(t)
$$

This has the expected unequal-exponent form: a scaled difference of the two decays, with the causal unit step included in the final answer.
