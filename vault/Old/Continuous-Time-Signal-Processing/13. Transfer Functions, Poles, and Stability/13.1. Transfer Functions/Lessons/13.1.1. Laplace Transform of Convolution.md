## Introduction to Laplace Transform of Convolution

When an LTI output is written as $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$, the cue is convolution in time and the next move is to switch to the one-sided Laplace domain. In this section, the rule to recognize is that convolution becomes multiplication of the input transform and the system transform, provided the causal and ROC conditions are valid.

Start with the simplest causal case: let $x(t)=\delta(t)$. Then $x(t)*h(t)=h(t)$, and because $\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t)\}=1$, the output transform is just $Y(s)=H(s)$ in this base case.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau\;\Longrightarrow\;Y(s)=\mathcal{L}\{x(t)*h(t)\}
$$

Now name the pieces: $X(s)=\mathcal{L}\{x(t)\}$, $H(s)=\mathcal{L}\{h(t)\}$, and $Y(s)=\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}$. The Laplace convolution theorem packages those symbols into one compact relation that later sections can use without redoing the time-domain integral.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x(t)*h(t)\}=X(s)H(s)
$$

Operationally, this is what makes transfer-function analysis manageable: the time-domain convolution turns into algebra in $s$, so the system can be carried forward as $H(s)$ and combined with the input through multiplication. The next step will use this same notation unchanged.

## Recognizing an LTI Output as a Convolution

**Example:** Recognize the form of $y(t)=\int_0^t e^{-\tau}\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ as a convolution and identify $x(t)$ and $h(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the integral kernel $h(t-\tau)$ together with the one-sided limits $0\le \tau\le t$. That matches the standard convolution pattern, so the output should be read as an LTI system response rather than as an ordinary product or a cross-correlation.

Match the integrand to the canonical form $\int x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ by taking the input signal to be $x(\tau)=e^{-\tau}u(\tau)$. The unit step is what makes this the causal one-sided case, because it keeps the signal active only for nonnegative time.

$$
y(t)=\int_0^t e^{-\tau}\,h(t-\tau)\,d\tau \;\equiv\; x(t)*h(t), \qquad x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)
$$

With that identification in place, the time-domain output is already in convolution form, so the one-sided Laplace transform will turn it into the product $Y(s)=X(s)H(s)$. For this base case, the input transform is $X(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}$ for $\Re\{s\}>-1$, and the system factor remains $H(s)$ as the transfer-function multiplier.

## Transforming $x(t)*h(t)$ into $X(s)H(s)$

**Example:** Given $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=t\,u(t)$, write the Laplace-domain product for $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$ and simplify the result form.

**Explanation**

The cue is the convolution structure from the previous section, so the move is to transform each factor separately and then multiply the results. Because both signals are causal, the one-sided Laplace transform applies directly.

First identify the transform of the input factor $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$. By the standard one-sided Laplace table, this gives $X(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}$ with ROC $\Re\{s\}>-1$.

$$
X(s)=\mathcal{L}\{e^{-t}u(t)\}=\frac{1}{s+1}
$$

Next identify the system factor $h(t)=t\,u(t)$. Its one-sided Laplace transform is $H(s)=\frac{1}{s^2}$ with ROC $\Re\{s\}>0$, so the product must use the intersection of the two one-sided regions.

$$
H(s)=\mathcal{L}\{t\,u(t)\}=\frac{1}{s^2}
$$

Now apply the convolution theorem: the Laplace transform of $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$ is the product of the individual transforms. The result is a transfer-function-style expression with the causal ROC inherited from both factors, so the final form is $Y(s)=\frac{1}{(s+1)s^2}$ with $\Re\{s\}>0$.

$$
Y(s)=X(s)H(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2}=\frac{1}{(s+1)s^2}, \qquad \Re\{s\}>0
$$

<a id="applying-the-convolution-transform-to-ytxtht"></a>
## Applying the Convolution Transform to $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$

**Example:** If $Y(s)=\frac{1}{s(s+1)}$ and $X(s)=\frac{1}{s}$, apply the convolution transform rule to find $H(s)$ and state the corresponding relationship in time domain.

**Explanation**

The cue is the product identity $Y(s)=X(s)H(s)$. Since $Y(s)$ and one factor are already known, isolate the missing factor with algebra instead of recomputing anything from the time domain.

$$
Y(s)=X(s)H(s) \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}
$$

Substitute the given transforms: $H(s)=\dfrac{\frac{1}{s(s+1)}}{\frac{1}{s}}$. The $s$ factor cancels, so the missing system factor is $\dfrac{1}{s+1}$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{\frac{1}{s(s+1)}}{\frac{1}{s}}=\frac{1}{s+1}
$$

So the recovered transfer multiplier is $H(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}$, with one-sided ROC $\Re\{s\}>-1$. The time-domain relationship remains $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$; the Laplace-domain step is the algebraic way to identify the missing system factor.

## Interpreting Convolution Multiplication in the $s$-Domain

**Example:** Given $X(s)=\frac{1}{s}$ and $H(s)=\frac{1}{s+1}$, interpret $Y(s)=X(s)H(s)$ for the causal LTI system and state the corresponding time-domain operation.

**Explanation**

The cue is the product identity $Y(s)=X(s)H(s)$. Here $X(s)$ plays the role of the input, $H(s)$ plays the role of the system transfer factor, and $Y(s)$ is the output. Read the product as a system relation, not as two separate inputs being combined.

$$
Y(s)=X(s)H(s)
$$

Translate that identity back to the time domain: the output is obtained by convolving the input with the impulse response, so $y(t)=x(t)*h(t)$. That is the meaning of multiplication in the $s$-domain for an LTI system.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

The practical point is that the system can be analyzed by multiplying transfer factors instead of evaluating convolution directly. In later transfer-function work, this is the rule that turns a signal-flow description into algebra.
