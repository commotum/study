## Introduction to Determining Stability from RLC Poles

For an RLC transfer function, the stability cue is the denominator of $H(s)$. Write the transfer function in the rational form $H(s)=N(s)/D(s)$, then treat the poles as the roots of $D(s)=0$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

The test is a left-half-plane test. The circuit is BIBO stable only when every pole $s_k$ has negative real part, written as $\Re\{s_k\}<0$. A right-half-plane pole grows, and an imaginary-axis pole does not decay, so either case fails the BIBO stability test.

$$
\Re\{s_k\}<0 \text{ for all poles } s_k \Rightarrow \text{ stable; } \exists\,s_k:\Re\{s_k\}\ge 0 \Rightarrow \text{ unstable}.
$$

In the simplest base case $H(s)=1/(s+3)$, the denominator is $D(s)=s+3$. Solving $s+3=0$ gives the single pole $s=-3$, and $\Re\{-3\}=-3<0$, so this transfer function is stable.

For higher-order RLC denominators, repeat the same check for each root of $D(s)$. A single pole with $\Re\{s_k\}>0$ or $\Re\{s_k\}=0$ determines the classification, because one growing or nondecaying circuit mode is enough to prevent BIBO stability.

## Classifying Stability from Negative Real RLC Poles

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{6}{(s+4)(s+1)}$, classify the system as BIBO stable, unstable, or nondecaying.

**Explanation**

Start with the denominator, because BIBO stability for this RLC transfer function is decided by the pole locations. The denominator is already factored, so set $D(s)=0$ and use each factor to find a pole.

$$
D(s)=(s+4)(s+1)
$$

$$
D(s)=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (s+4)(s+1)=0
$$

Solving each factor gives the two real poles $s=-4$ and $s=-1$. Both have strictly negative real part, so both poles lie in the left half of the $s$-plane.

$$
s=-4 \quad \text{or} \quad s=-1
$$

Because every pole satisfies $\operatorname{Re}(s)<0$, the modes decay instead of growing or persisting forever. The transfer function is therefore BIBO stable.

## Classifying Stability from Left-Half-Plane Complex RLC Poles

**Example:** Given $H(s)=\frac{40}{s^2+2s+10}$, find the poles and decide if the RLC system is stable.

**Explanation**

The cue is that stability is decided by the pole locations of $H(s)$. The poles come from the denominator, so set the characteristic polynomial equal to zero.

$$
s^2+2s+10=0
$$

Use the quadratic formula. The negative discriminant means the poles form a complex-conjugate pair.

$$
\begin{aligned} s &= \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{2^2-4(1)(10)}}{2} \\ &= \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-36}}{2} \\ &= -1\pm 3j \end{aligned}
$$

The poles are $s=-1+3j$ and $s=-1-3j$. Both have real part $-1$, so both poles lie strictly in the left half-plane.

$$
\operatorname{Re}(-1+3j)=\operatorname{Re}(-1-3j)=-1<0
$$

Therefore the RLC transfer function is BIBO stable. The complex pair means the natural response oscillates, but the negative real part makes that oscillation decay; there are no right-half-plane or imaginary-axis poles to create a nondecaying mode.

## Classifying Instability from a Right-Half-Plane RLC Pole

**Example:** Classify $H(s)=\frac{5}{(s-2)(s+1)(s+4)}$: is this RLC transfer function BIBO stable or unstable?

**Explanation**

The stability cue is the pole locations, not the numerator value. For a rational RLC transfer function, BIBO stability requires every pole to satisfy $\Re\{s\}<0$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{5}{(s-2)(s+1)(s+4)}
$$

Set each denominator factor equal to zero to list the poles.

$$
s-2=0\Rightarrow s=2,\qquad s+1=0\Rightarrow s=-1,\qquad s+4=0\Rightarrow s=-4
$$

The poles $s=-1$ and $s=-4$ are in the left half-plane, but $s=2$ has positive real part because $\Re\{2\}=2>0$.

$$
\Re\{2\}=2>0
$$

Therefore the transfer function is BIBO unstable. One right-half-plane pole is enough to fail the all-poles test, even though the other two poles are stable left-half-plane poles.

## Classifying Instability from Imaginary-Axis RLC Poles

**Example:** Classify stability for $H(s)=\frac{2}{s^2+9}$ and interpret the time-domain implication of poles on the imaginary axis.

**Explanation**

The cue is the denominator $s^2+9$. For this lesson's BIBO rule, the transfer function is stable only when every pole has strictly negative real part.

$$
s^2+9=0
$$

$$
s^2=-9 \quad \Rightarrow \quad s=\pm 3j
$$

Both poles are purely imaginary. Their real parts are exactly zero, so they sit on the boundary of the left half-plane rather than strictly inside it.

$$
\Re\{3j\}=0,\qquad \Re\{-3j\}=0
$$

Because the poles do not satisfy $\Re\{s\}<0$, the circuit transfer function is classified as unstable for BIBO purposes in this topic context.

The time-domain meaning is a nondecaying sinusoidal mode. These imaginary-axis poles do not create exponential growth like a right-half-plane pole, but they also do not decay away like left-half-plane poles.
