## Introduction to RC Transfer Functions in the $s$-Domain

In this lesson sequence we use transfer functions to replace circuit differential-equation setup with one compact algebraic workflow. The goal is to build a repeatable pattern: identify the circuit impedance model, form one output-to-input ratio, then use it as the basis for all low-pass and high-pass examples in this module.

Start each RC section by recognizing the nodes as Laplace signals. Map the input node to $V_i(s)$, the chosen output node to $V_o(s)$, and label the capacitor impedance with the substitution $Z_C=\frac{1}{sC}$. Then define the transfer function explicitly as $H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}
$$

![](<../Source/13.5.2. Analyzing RC Filters with Transfer Functions/Images/l002-s001-te-section-image-01.png>)

For the base case, use a series RC with the output across the capacitor. Apply the divider rule in the $s$-domain to get $V_o(s)=V_i(s)\,\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}=V_i(s)\,\frac{\frac{1}{sC}}{R+\frac{1}{sC}}=\frac{V_i(s)}{1+sRC}$. Therefore the base transfer function is $H(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC}$, which is the canonical first-order low-pass form.

$$
Z_C=\frac{1}{sC}
$$

If you keep the same steps and only change the output node to be across the resistor, the divider form becomes complementary: $H(s)=\frac{R}{R+\frac{1}{sC}}=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}$. The only meaningful structural change is the node-choice slotting, while the same one-parameter workflow remains intact.

The remaining example sections will reuse this exact move with one new wrinkle at a time: set up the $s$-domain objects, apply ratio logic, simplify to first-order $H(s)$, and then read poles, zeros, and the time constant from the same result.

## Writing the $s$-Domain RC Voltage Divider

**Example:** Write the transfer function for a series RC circuit where $V_o$ is the capacitor voltage and reduce it to a standard first-order form.

**Explanation**

The cue is a series RC branch with output taken across the capacitor. Treat the input as $V_i(s)$ and the target output as $V_o(s)$, then build $H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}$ from that diagram.

![](<../Source/13.5.2. Analyzing RC Filters with Transfer Functions/Images/l002-s002-te-section-image-02.png>)

Use the capacitor model first. Replace the capacitor impedance with its $s$-domain equivalent, then substitute that value into the divider structure that connects $R$ and the capacitor in series.

$$
Z_C = \frac{1}{sC}
$$

The divider expression is

$$
V_o(s)=V_i(s)\,\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}
$$

Substitute $Z_C=\frac{1}{sC}$ and simplify the fraction by multiplying numerator and denominator by $sC$. This keeps one controlled algebraic wrinkle from the previous section while making the final ratio explicit.

$$
V_o(s)=V_i(s)\,\frac{\frac{1}{sC}}{R+\frac{1}{sC}}=V_i(s)\,\frac{1}{1+sRC}
$$

The resulting transfer function is

$$
H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}=\frac{1}{1+sRC}
$$

This form is the standard first-order low-pass pattern with time constant $\tau=RC$ and a single pole at $s=-1/(RC)$. The circuit is now represented by one reusable $H(s)$ expression ready for pole-zero interpretation in the next section.

## Deriving a Low-Pass RC Transfer Function

**Example:** Derive the low-pass expression and compute $\tau$, $\omega_c$, and $H(0)$ for a capacitor-output RC divider.

**Explanation**

Use the same recognition cue as the previous example: a series RC branch where the output is across the capacitor. Map the input to $V_i(s)$, the output to $V_o(s)$, and the target ratio is still $H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}$.

![](<../Source/13.5.2. Analyzing RC Filters with Transfer Functions/Images/l002-s003-te-section-image-03.png>)

The divider form is the same as before, but now we also extract endpoint interpretation from the final transfer function.

$$
H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}=\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}
$$

Replace the capacitor by its $s$-domain impedance and simplify.

$$
Z_C=\frac{1}{sC},\qquad H(s)=\frac{1}{R+\frac{1}{sC}}=\frac{1}{1+sRC}
$$

Now write the result in standard first-order time-constant form.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC}=\frac{1}{1+s\tau}, \quad \tau=RC
$$

Read endpoint behavior directly from this form.

$$
\lim_{s\to 0}H(s)=1,\qquad \lim_{s\to \infty}H(s)=0
$$

The DC value is $H(0)=1$, and the cutoff frequency is $\omega_c=1/\tau=1/(RC)$. Because low frequency passes with unity gain and high frequency decays to zero, this is a low-pass transfer function.

## Deriving a High-Pass RC Transfer Function

**Example:** Derive $H(s)$ for the same RC network but with $V_o$ across $R$, then interpret numerator and limits.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same series RC branch as before, but the output node flips from the capacitor to the resistor. Keep the same mapping: input is $V_i(s)$, output is $V_o(s)$ across $R$, and the ratio target is $H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}$.

![](<../Source/13.5.2. Analyzing RC Filters with Transfer Functions/Images/l002-s004-te-section-image-04.png>)

Use the divider rule again so the only new variation is the tap point.

$$
H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}=\frac{R}{R+Z_C}
$$

Substitute $Z_C=\frac{1}{sC}$ and simplify.

$$
H(s)=\frac{R}{R+\frac{1}{sC}}=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}
$$

Write it in standard first-order form and read the pole-zero structure from it.

$$
H(s)=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}=\frac{s\tau}{1+s\tau},\quad \tau=RC
$$

The zero is at $s=0$ and the pole is at $s=-1/(RC)$ from the first-order denominator.

$$
\lim_{s\to 0}H(s)=0,\qquad \lim_{s\to \infty}H(s)=1
$$

These endpoint checks and the nonzero numerator show it is high-pass: DC gain is zero, while high-frequency gain approaches unity.

## Reading Poles, Zeros, and Time Constant from an RC Transfer Function

**Example:** Identify poles and zeros for both low-pass and high-pass RC transfer functions, then map each to $\tau=RC$.

**Explanation**

The cue is now pure recognition: you no longer build a new divider; you read structure from a finished transfer-function form. Use $H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}$ and extract features directly.

Start with the two canonical forms from previous steps: $H_{LP}(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC}$ and $H_{HP}(s)=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}$. Keep numerator and denominator explicit for a stable comparison.

$$
H_{LP}(s)=\frac{N_{LP}(s)}{D_{LP}(s)}=\frac{1}{1+sRC},\qquad H_{HP}(s)=\frac{N_{HP}(s)}{D_{HP}(s)}=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}
$$

Use the same rule for both functions: zeros satisfy $N(s)=0$, poles satisfy $D(s)=0$.

$$
N_{LP}(s)=1\;\Rightarrow\; \text{No finite zeros},\qquad D_{LP}(s)=1+sRC\;\Rightarrow\; s=-\frac{1}{RC}
$$

$$
N_{HP}(s)=sRC\;\Rightarrow\; s=0,\qquad D_{HP}(s)=1+sRC\;\Rightarrow\; s=-\frac{1}{RC}
$$

Extract the shared time constant from the first-order denominator factor the same way in both cases.

$$
\tau = RC,\qquad s=-\frac{1}{\tau}
$$

For $R>0$ and $C>0$, $\tau=RC>0$, so the pole is in the left-half plane and both filters are stable; low-pass has a DC-zero numerator and high-pass has a zero at the origin.

## Classifying RC Filter Behavior from an $s$-Domain Transfer Function

**Example:** Given two candidate RC transfer functions, classify each as low-pass or high-pass and justify with $H(0)$ and $H(\infty)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is no longer deriving a circuit; it is classification from completed rational forms. Use the same workflow: write the DC and high-frequency limits, then use zero location to confirm behavior.

Use these two examples: $H_1(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC}$ and $H_2(s)=\frac{4sRC}{1+sRC}$, with $R,C>0$.

$$
H_1(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC},\qquad H_2(s)=\frac{4sRC}{1+sRC}
$$

For $H_1(s)$, evaluate endpoints:

$$
\lim_{s\to 0}H_1(s)=1,\qquad \lim_{s\to \infty}H_1(s)=0
$$

The numerator has no finite zero, so this form passes low frequencies and attenuates high frequencies. Also, $s=-1/(RC)<0$ puts the pole in the stable left-half plane.

For $H_2(s)$, use the same endpoint rule.

$$
\lim_{s\to 0}H_2(s)=0,\qquad \lim_{s\to \infty}H_2(s)=4
$$

The zero at $s=0$ in the numerator forces DC suppression, and the nonzero high-frequency gain indicates high-pass behavior (with gain scaling $4$ here due to the chosen numerator constant).

Final classification: $H_1(s)$ is low-pass and $H_2(s)$ is high-pass; in both cases the classification comes directly from numerator zeros and endpoint gains.
