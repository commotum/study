## Introduction to Computing Poles of an RLC Circuit

In this step, the transfer function is already in rational form, so the first thing to recognize is the denominator. Write the RLC transfer function as $H(s)=\dfrac{N(s)}{D(s)}$ and treat $D(s)$ as the characteristic polynomial for pole analysis.

$$
H(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

The poles are the values of $s$ that make the denominator zero, so the next action is to solve $D(s)=0$. In the simplest base case, $H(s)=\dfrac{1}{s+1}$ has one pole at $s=-1$, which is a left-half-plane root and signals a decaying mode.

$$
D(s)=0
$$

That sign test is the stability cue to keep in mind: left-half-plane poles decay, while right-half-plane poles grow. In the next steps, you will use this same denominator-to-root move on second-order RLC polynomials, including repeated and complex-conjugate cases.

## Identifying the RLC Characteristic Polynomial

**Example:** Given $H(s)=\dfrac{12}{2s^2+6s+3}$, state the characteristic polynomial and write the equation to solve for poles.

**Explanation**

The cue is the denominator of the transfer function. Write $H(s)$ in the form $H(s)=\dfrac{N(s)}{D(s)}$, so here the characteristic polynomial is $D(s)=2s^2+6s+3$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{12}{2s^2+6s+3}=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}
$$

To prepare for pole solving, set the denominator equal to zero: $D(s)=0$. If you want the polynomial in normalized form, divide by the leading coefficient to get $s^2+3s+\frac{3}{2}=0$.

$$
2s^2+6s+3=0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad s^2+3s+\frac{3}{2}=0
$$

This is the form you will keep using: identify the denominator, name it as the characteristic polynomial, and isolate the equation whose roots are the poles. The next step will solve that polynomial and interpret the roots in the $s$-plane.

## Computing Distinct Real RLC Poles

**Example:** Find poles of $H(s)=\dfrac{10}{s^2+4s+3}$ and classify them as distinct real poles.

**Explanation**

The cue is the denominator polynomial, so start by setting $s^2+4s+3=0$. Because this is a second-order characteristic polynomial, the roots of this equation are the poles of the circuit.

$$
s^2+4s+3=0
$$

Compute the discriminant to check whether the roots are real and distinct: $\Delta=b^2-4ac=4^2-4(1)(3)=16-12=4$. Since $\Delta>0$, there are two different real roots.

$$
\Delta=16-12=4
$$

Now solve the quadratic to get the poles $s=-1$ and $s=-3$. Both poles lie in the left half of the $s$-plane, so this example is a stable two-pole case with distinct real decay rates.

$$
s=-1,\,-3
$$

## Computing Repeated RLC Poles

**Example:** Compute poles of $H(s)=\dfrac{7}{s^2+2s+1}$ and write them as a repeated-pole result.

**Explanation**

The cue is still the denominator polynomial, so start from $s^2+2s+1=0$. This is the same characteristic-polynomial step as before, but now the coefficients produce a zero discriminant.

$$
s^2+2s+1=0
$$

Compute the discriminant: $\Delta=b^2-4ac=2^2-4(1)(1)=4-4=0$. A zero discriminant means the quadratic has one repeated real root.

$$
\Delta=4-4=0
$$

Apply the quadratic formula once and keep the multiplicity: $s=-1$ with multiplicity $2$. That repeated pole is a left-half-plane pole, so the mode is stable but has a repeated-root response shape rather than two distinct decay rates.

$$
s=-1 \text{ with multiplicity } 2
$$

## Computing Complex-Conjugate RLC Poles

**Example:** Find poles of $H(s)=\dfrac{4}{s^2+2s+5}$ and classify stability from the pair in the $s$-plane.

**Explanation**

The cue is the denominator polynomial, so set $s^2+2s+5=0$ and read off $a=1$, $b=2$, and $c=5$. Because this is the characteristic equation, the roots of that quadratic are the poles.

$$
s^2+2s+5=0
$$

Now check the discriminant. Here $\Delta=b^2-4ac=2^2-4(1)(5)=4-20=-16$, so the roots are complex conjugates rather than two real poles.

$$
\Delta=4-20=-16
$$

Use the quadratic formula in complex form: $s=-\dfrac{b}{2a}\pm j\dfrac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}$. Substituting the coefficients gives $s=-1\pm 2j$, so the pole pair lies in the left half-plane and produces a decaying oscillatory response.

$$
s=-1\pm 2j
$$

## Locating RLC Poles in the $s$-Plane

**Example:** Create a worked example that computes poles from $s^2+2s+1=0$ and $s^2+4s+10=0$, plots each, and classifies each set for stability in the same $s$-plane diagram.

**Explanation**

The cue is each denominator polynomial, and the rule is to solve the characteristic equation first, then place the resulting poles on the $s$-plane. For $s^2+2s+1=0$, the polynomial factors immediately; for $s^2+4s+10=0$, the discriminant is negative, so the poles must be a complex-conjugate pair.

$$
\begin{aligned}s^2+2s+1&=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad (s+1)^2=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad s=-1 \text{ (double pole)}\\s^2+4s+10&=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Delta=4^2-4(1)(10)=16-40=-24<0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad s=-2\pm j\sqrt{6}\end{aligned}
$$

![](<../Source/13.5.5. Computing Poles of an RLC Circuit/Images/l005-s006-te-section-006.png>)

On the plot, the double pole at $s=-1$ sits in the left-half-plane, so it is stable. The conjugate pair $s=-2\pm j\sqrt{6}$ also lies in the left-half-plane, so it is stable as well. The location rule is the same in every case: left-half-plane poles are stable, right-half-plane poles are unstable, and poles on the imaginary axis require special attention.
