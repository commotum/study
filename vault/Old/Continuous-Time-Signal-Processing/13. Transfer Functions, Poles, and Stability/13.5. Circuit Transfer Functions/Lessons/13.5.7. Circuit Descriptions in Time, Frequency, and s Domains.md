## Introduction to Circuit Descriptions in Time, Frequency, and $s$ Domains

A linear circuit can be described in three matching ways: a time-domain differential equation, an $s$-domain transfer function, and a frequency response. The differential equation relates input and output signals such as $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ directly in time. The transfer function $H(s)$ gives the same input-output behavior as an algebraic ratio after a zero-initial-condition Laplace transform. The frequency response $H(j\omega)$ is the same function evaluated on the imaginary axis for sinusoidal steady-state analysis.

The conversion workflow has three actions. First, convert each time derivative to a power of $s$ under zero initial conditions. Next, collect the transformed output terms with $Y(s)$ and the transformed input terms with $X(s)$. Finally, isolate the ratio $H(s)=Y(s)/X(s)$, then substitute $s=j\omega$ when the question asks for frequency response.

$$
Y(s)=H(s)X(s)
$$

For a first-order circuit model, the time-domain description usually has one output derivative term and one output term, driven by an input term. After the Laplace transform, that derivative contributes a factor of $sY(s)$, so the denominator of $H(s)$ comes from the output-side coefficients. The root of that denominator is the circuit pole.

Once $H(s)$ is known, the frequency-response form does not require a new circuit derivation. Replace $s$ with $j\omega$ and interpret the resulting complex value as sinusoidal gain and phase at angular frequency $\omega$. The poles and zeros belong to $H(s)$ itself, so this substitution changes the evaluation path, not the underlying circuit description.

$$
H(j\omega)=H(s)|_{s=j\omega}
$$

Stability is read from the pole locations in the $s$ domain. For continuous-time circuits, poles in the left half-plane correspond to decaying natural responses, while poles in the right half-plane correspond to growing natural responses. The rest of this lesson uses the same sequence each time: derive $H(s)$ from the circuit equation, evaluate $H(j\omega)$ for sinusoidal response, read the poles, and reconcile the time, frequency, and $s$-domain descriptions as one model.

## Converting a Circuit Differential Equation to a Transfer Function

**Example:** For zero initial conditions, convert $L y''(t)+R C y'(t)+y(t)=R C x'(t)$ into $H(s)=Y(s)/X(s)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the differential equation is already linear with constant coefficients, and the initial conditions are zero. That means each time derivative transforms by multiplying the corresponding signal transform by $s$.

$$
y'(t)\to sY(s),\qquad y''(t)\to s^2Y(s),\qquad x'(t)\to sX(s)
$$

Substitute those zero-initial-condition transforms into the circuit equation. Keep the output terms on the left and the input term on the right.

$$
Ls^2Y(s)+RCsY(s)+Y(s)=RCsX(s)
$$

Now factor out $Y(s)$ from the left side. This isolates the polynomial that will become the transfer-function denominator.

$$
\left(Ls^2+RCs+1\right)Y(s)=RCsX(s)
$$

Divide by $X(s)$ and by the collected output factor to isolate the ratio $Y(s)/X(s)$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{RCs}{Ls^2+RCs+1}
$$

In numerator-denominator form, the input derivative contributes the factor $s$ in the numerator, while the pole locations are governed by the denominator $D(s)=Ls^2+RCs+1$.

$$
N(s)=RCs,\qquad D(s)=Ls^2+RCs+1
$$

## Finding Frequency Response from a Circuit Transfer Function

**Example:** Take $H(s)=\frac{s+1}{s^2+3s+2}$ and rewrite it as $H(jw)$ in a form suitable for extracting magnitude and phase behavior.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the circuit description is already an $s$-domain transfer function. To find the frequency response, evaluate the same transfer function on the imaginary axis by substituting $s=jw$.

$$
H(jw)=\frac{jw+1}{(jw)^2+3jw+2}
$$

Now use $(jw)^2=-w^2$ and collect the real and imaginary parts in the denominator. The numerator is already in real-plus-imaginary form.

$$
H(jw)=\frac{1+jw}{2-w^2+j3w}
$$

This is the useful frequency-domain form: the numerator is $1+jw$ and the denominator is $(2-w^2)+j3w$. From here, magnitude and phase would come from the complex numerator divided by the complex denominator at each frequency $w$, while the poles remain the same poles encoded by the original denominator in $H(s)$.

## Interpreting Pole Locations Across Circuit Descriptions

**Example:** Find the poles of $H(s)=1/(s^2+4s+20)$, classify their locations, and state the implied time-domain behavior and BIBO stability.

**Explanation**

The poles of $H(s)$ are the roots of the denominator. For this transfer function, the denominator polynomial is $s^2+4s+20$, so the pole calculation starts by setting that expression equal to zero.

$$
s^2+4s+20=0
$$

$$
s=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{4^2-4(1)(20)}}{2}=\frac{-4\pm\sqrt{-64}}{2}=-2\pm j4
$$

The two poles are $s=-2+j4$ and $s=-2-j4$. They form a complex-conjugate pair, and both have real part $-2$, so both poles lie in the open left half-plane. The nonzero imaginary parts indicate oscillation, while the negative real part indicates exponential decay.

$$
e^{(-2+j4)t},\ e^{(-2-j4)t}\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad e^{-2t}\bigl(C_1\cos 4t+C_2\sin 4t\bigr)
$$

Thus the homogeneous time-domain response is an underdamped decaying oscillation. The same conclusion is visible from the circuit differential equation because these poles are also the roots of its characteristic polynomial.

For a continuous-time rational LTI circuit transfer function, BIBO stability requires every pole to be in the open left half-plane. Since both poles here have negative real part, the circuit is BIBO stable. In the frequency-response view, substituting $s=j\omega$ does not move the poles onto the axis; it samples the response along the imaginary axis while the nearby left-half-plane poles shape the resonance and phase.

## Matching Time, Frequency, and $s$-Domain Descriptions of One Circuit

**Example:** For zero initial conditions, reconcile the circuit model $y''(t)+3y'(t)+2y(t)=x'(t)$ across time, $s$-, and frequency-domain descriptions. Find $H(s)$, write $H(jw)$, and state the pole-based stability conclusion.

**Explanation**

The time-domain cue is the input derivative on the right side and the output derivatives on the left side. With zero initial conditions, each derivative becomes multiplication by $s$ in the transform domain.

$$
s^2Y(s)+3sY(s)+2Y(s)=sX(s)
$$

Collect the output transform $Y(s)$, then divide by $X(s)$ and by the output polynomial. The input derivative contributes the numerator factor $s$.

$$
\left(s^2+3s+2\right)Y(s)=sX(s)
$$

$$
H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{s}{s^2+3s+2}=\frac{s}{(s+1)(s+2)}
$$

The matching frequency response is not a new system; it is the same transfer function evaluated on the imaginary axis by substituting $s=jw$.

$$
H(jw)=\frac{jw}{(jw)^2+3jw+2}=\frac{jw}{2-w^2+j3w}
$$

The pole check comes from the same denominator that appears in both $H(s)$ and $H(jw)$. The roots are $s=-1$ and $s=-2$, so both poles are in the open left half-plane.

$$
p_1=-1,\qquad p_2=-2
$$

Thus the three descriptions match: the ODE $y''(t)+3y'(t)+2y(t)=x'(t)$ corresponds to $H(s)=s/(s^2+3s+2)$, to $H(jw)=jw/(2-w^2+j3w)$, and to a BIBO-stable circuit because every pole has negative real part.
