## Introduction to Growing Exponential Responses

The object to recognize first is the causal pole-mode mapping: a transfer-function or impulse-response pole at $s$ contributes a causal term $h(t)=A e^{st}u(t)$. In this lesson, the target is to read this term and identify whether the exponent causes growth, decay, or steady behavior.

$$
h(t)=A e^{st}u(t)\;\text{for a simple causal pole at } s.
$$

Use the same response form as previous exponential lessons: $s=-\alpha$ with $\alpha>0$ gives $e^{-\alpha t}$, which decays for $t\ge 0$. Now use the contrast case $s=a$ with $a>0$ and keep the causal multiplier: $h(t)=A e^{at}u(t)$. Because $a$ is positive, this term becomes unbounded in time.

$$
\Re\{s\}>0\Rightarrow |e^{st}|=e^{\Re\{s\}t}=e^{at}\to\infty\quad (t\to\infty),
$$

Action rule for the step: convert each causal pole to its time-domain mode and scan its real part. If any causal mode has $\Re\{s\}>0$, one growing exponential is enough to make the impulse response unbounded, so the causal LTI system is BIBO unstable.

Next in this section sequence, you will identify the positive pole first, write the corresponding growing exponential mode, then classify the full system by the same one-dangerous-mode rule when moving to mixed cases.

## Identifying Positive Real Pole Contributions

**Example:** For $H(s)=\frac{6}{(s-4)(s+1)(s+2)}$, identify the pole that creates a growing exponential contribution.

**Explanation**

The cue is a factorized transfer function. Treat each linear denominator factor as one pole location, then keep only the pole with positive real part because only that causal pole produces a growing exponential mode.

$$
H(s)=\frac{6}{(s-4)(s+1)(s+2)}.
$$

Find the pole set by solving the denominator factors: $(s-4)=0$, $(s+1)=0$, and $(s+2)=0$. This gives poles at $s=4$, $s=-1$, and $s=-2$.

$$
\{4,-1,-2\}.
$$

Filter by sign: the positive real root is only $s=4$, so this is the only candidate that generates growth in the impulse response.

$$
s=4 \Rightarrow h_{\text{candidate}}(t)=A e^{4t}u(t),
$$

The remaining poles at $s=-1$ and $s=-2$ correspond to decaying causal terms and therefore do not override the instability signal. Record the growing contributor now; in the next section you will convert it directly into the full time-domain mode.

## Converting a Positive Real Pole to a Growing Exponential Response

**Example:** Find the unstable growing term for $H(s)=\\frac{5}{s-3}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a simple transfer block with one real pole. Convert it using the same causal inverse-Laplace pattern used for each isolated term in this topic, then keep the term with positive exponential growth.

$$
H(s)=\\frac{5}{s-3}.
$$

Step 1 is matching to $\\frac{A}{s-a}$. Read $A=5$ and $a=3$ directly from the numerator and pole location.

$$
\\frac{A}{s-a} \\to A e^{at}u(t)
$$

Step 2 is applying that causal rule, and Step 3 is scaling by $A$. The time-domain contribution is therefore

$$
h_{\\text{unstable}}(t)=5e^{3t}u(t).
$$

This mode is growing because the exponent coefficient is positive: $3>0$. As $t\\to\\infty$, $5e^{3t}u(t)$ is unbounded, so it is the unstable growing exponential term used for BIBO instability classification in this lesson.

<a id="classifying-instability-from-an-unbounded-exponential-term"></a>
## Classifying Instability from an Unbounded Exponential Term

**Example:** Determine BIBO stability for $h(t)=2e^{2t}u(t)-5e^{-3t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is an explicit causal time-domain response with separate exponential modes. Use the stability rule: scan each additive term and classify whether it grows, decays, or stays bounded for large positive time.

$$
h(t)=2e^{2t}u(t)-5e^{-3t}u(t).
$$

Step 1: list the terms as $2e^{2t}u(t)$ and $-5e^{-3t}u(t)$. Both are causal because each has $u(t)$.

Step 2: classify each exponent: $e^{2t}$ has positive exponent $2$, so it is growing; $e^{-3t}$ has negative exponent $-3$, so it is decaying.

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} 2e^{2t}u(t)=\infty, \quad \lim_{t\to\infty}-5e^{-3t}u(t)=0.
$$

Step 3: because one causal term is unbounded and grows without limit, the impulse response is not absolutely bounded for any bounded input, so BIBO stability fails regardless of the decaying companion term.

Final result: the system is **BIBO unstable** and the unstable contribution to cite is $2e^{2t}u(t)$.

## Classifying Mixed Pole Sets with One Growing Exponential Contribution

**Example:** Classify BIBO stability for $H(s)=\\frac{10(s-3)(s+5)}{(s-3)(s+1)(s+2)(s^2+4s+8)}$ and identify the unstable time-domain contribution.

**Explanation**

The cue is a mixed pole set in a rational transfer function. Use the same rule from earlier sections: any term with positive real part in the pole contributes a growing causal mode, so scan all poles before deciding stability.

Step 1 is to list the pole roots from the denominator factors: the first three are $s=-1$, $s=-2$, and the quadratic $s^2+4s+8$.

$$
s^2+4s+8=0\\Rightarrow s=-2\\pm j2.
$$

Do not forget the factor $(s-3)$ as well, so the full pole set is $(3,-1,-2,-2\\pm j2).$

Step 2 is checking real parts: $3>0$, $-1<0$, $-2<0$, and the pair $-2\\pm j2$ also has real part $-2<0$. Only the pole at $s=3$ is positive.

Step 3 is to convert only this growing source into the time domain: $\\frac{A}{s-3}\\to A e^{3t}u(t)$, which is the decisive unstable contribution in the response.

Step 4 is the conclusion: because at least one causal term is exponentially growing, the system is **BIBO unstable**, regardless of the additional stable or oscillatory stable terms with negative real parts.
