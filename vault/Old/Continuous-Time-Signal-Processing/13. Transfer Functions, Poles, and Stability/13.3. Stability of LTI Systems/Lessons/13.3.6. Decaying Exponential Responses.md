## Introduction to Decaying Exponential Responses

This section introduces the core rule for this topic: a negative real pole in a causal LTI transfer function creates a decaying exponential mode in the impulse response. If the pole is at $s=-\sigma$ with $\sigma>0$, the mode is stable and causal, and it is identified as $e^{-\sigma t}u(t)$ multiplied by an amplitude constant.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+\sigma}\right\}=e^{-\sigma t}u(t)
$$

Use the base case directly: for $H(s)=1/(s+1)$, this gives $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, so the pole at $s=-1$ produces decay rate $\sigma=1$. The mapping is one-to-one in this symbolic form: denominator parameter $\sigma$ becomes the exponential decay rate.

For a general term $\frac{K}{s+\sigma}$, apply the same mapping and append the same causal multiplier: the impulse-response contribution is $K e^{-\sigma t}u(t)$. The coefficient $K$ scales amplitude, while $\sigma$ controls how quickly the response magnitude decreases, and $u(t)$ enforces right-sided, causal support.

$$
s=-\sigma,\; \sigma>0 \implies 1/(s+\sigma) \leftrightarrow e^{-\sigma t}u(t)
$$

Interpretation rule: $\sigma>0$ (pole at $s=-\sigma$) means decaying behavior and supports causal LTI stability reasoning; this is the reusable template we will use when later steps add coefficient factors, compare decay rates, and classify stability.

## Finding the Exponential Response from a Negative Real Pole

**Example:** Find the causal time-domain term for $H(s)=\frac{6}{s+3}$.

**Explanation**

The rule is to match the transfer-function term to $\frac{K}{s+\alpha}$. For a causal LTI mode, this converts to $K e^{-\alpha t}u(t)$, and stable decay requires $\alpha>0$.

$$
\frac{K}{s+\alpha} \leftrightarrow K e^{-\alpha t}u(t)
$$

From the cue $H(s)=\frac{6}{s+3}$, identify $K=6$ and $\alpha=3$. This is a single negative real pole at $s=-3$, so the contribution is one exponential term with decay rate $3$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{6}{s+3} \leftrightarrow h(t)=6e^{-3t}u(t)
$$

Final result: $h(t)=6e^{-3t}u(t)$. The negative pole gives decay, and the positive $3$ is the decay constant used later when comparing stability rates across modes.

## Comparing Decay Rates from Negative Real Pole Locations

**Example:** Compare the decay in $H(s)=\\frac{2}{s+1}$ and $H(s)=\\frac{2}{s+4}$ and state which term decays faster.

**Explanation**

Use the same cue used before: whenever a term has the form $\\frac{K}{s+\\alpha}$ in a causal transfer function, the time response is a decaying exponential $K e^{-\\alpha t}u(t)$. The action is to extract $K$ and $\\alpha$, then compare the $\\alpha$ values.

$$
\\frac{K}{s+\\alpha} \leftrightarrow K e^{-\\alpha t}u(t)
$$

Apply the rule to each transfer-function term. For $H(s)=\\frac{2}{s+1}$, identify $K=2$ and $\\alpha_1=1$, giving $2e^{-1\\cdot t}u(t)=2e^{-t}u(t)$. For $H(s)=\\frac{2}{s+4}$, identify $K=2$ and $\\alpha_2=4$, giving $2e^{-4t}u(t)$.

$$
h_1(t)=2e^{-t}u(t),\\qquad h_2(t)=2e^{-4t}u(t)
$$

Compare the decay rates by the $\\alpha$ values: $\\alpha_2=4>\\alpha_1=1$, so $e^{-4t}$ decays faster than $e^{-t}$. Therefore $2e^{-4t}u(t)$ is the faster-decaying response term and $2e^{-t}u(t)$ is slower. Both poles stay in the stable-left-half-plane, so each term remains stable; they just decay at different speeds.

## Interpreting Multiple Negative Real Poles as Decaying Terms

**Example:** Convert $H(s)=\\frac{3}{s+1}+\\frac{2}{s+5}$ to time-domain response terms and state the two decay rates.

**Explanation**

The cue is still the same single-term map: a causal transfer term of the form $\\frac{K}{s+\\alpha}$ maps to $K e^{-\\alpha t}u(t)$. Keep the sum written as separate summands first, then convert each one.

Write the transfer function as two terms, $\\frac{3}{s+1}+\\frac{2}{s+5}$, so the first term is $K=3$, $\\alpha_1=1$, and the second is $K=2$, $\\alpha_2=5$.

$$
\\frac{3}{s+1} \leftrightarrow 3e^{-t}u(t),\qquad \frac{2}{s+5} \leftrightarrow 2e^{-5t}u(t)
$$

Apply the one-to-one map independently to each summand because of superposition: $\\frac{3}{s+1} \to 3e^{-t}u(t)$ and $\\frac{2}{s+5} \to 2e^{-5t}u(t)$. Then combine the responses by summing terms.

$$
h(t)=3e^{-t}u(t)+2e^{-5t}u(t)
$$

The result has two decaying exponentials with decay rates $1$ and $5$. Because both poles are in the left half-plane, both modes are stable and causal; only their decay speeds differ, with $e^{-5t}$ decaying fastest.

## Connecting Decaying Exponential Responses to BIBO Stability

**Example:** Classify BIBO stability for $H(s)=\frac{5}{s+2}+\frac{1}{s+7}$ and explain why the time-domain response is stable.

**Explanation**

Use the same pole-to-mode cue from earlier: each term $\frac{K}{s+\alpha}$ in a causal transfer function maps to $K e^{-\alpha t}u(t)$, so for a sum, convert each term and combine by superposition.

List the poles from the denominator: $s=-2$ and $s=-7$, both negative real. That means each associated exponential will decay.

$$
\frac{5}{s+2} \leftrightarrow 5e^{-2t}u(t),\qquad \frac{1}{s+7} \leftrightarrow e^{-7t}u(t)
$$

Apply the map independently to each summand and then add responses: the two terms are separate decaying modes in a causal system.

$$
h(t)=5e^{-2t}u(t)+e^{-7t}u(t)
$$

Because both poles are in the left half-plane, both exponentials decay and are absolutely integrable over $t\ge 0$. A finite weighted sum of decaying terms cannot grow without bound for bounded input, so $H(s)=\frac{5}{s+2}+\frac{1}{s+7}$ is BIBO stable.
