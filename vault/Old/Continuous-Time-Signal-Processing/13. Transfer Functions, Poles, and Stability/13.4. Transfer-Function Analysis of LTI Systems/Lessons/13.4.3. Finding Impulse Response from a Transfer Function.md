## Introduction to Finding Impulse Response from a Transfer Function

A transfer function $H(s)$ describes the LTI system in the Laplace domain. To find the causal impulse response, transform $H(s)$ back to the time domain and attach the causality factor $u(t)$ through the inverse-Laplace pair.

$$
h(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{H(s)\}
$$

The base case is a single first-order factor. When $H(s)=\frac{1}{s+a}$, the matching inverse pair gives an exponentially decaying impulse response.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{1/(s+a)\}=e^{-at}u(t)
$$

More complicated rational transfer functions are prepared for the same entrywise inversion step by rewriting them into recognizable terms: linear factors, repeated factors, or irreducible quadratic forms.

## Finding an Impulse Response from a First-Order Transfer Function

**Example:** Find the causal impulse response $h(t)$ for $H(s)=\frac{1}{s+2}$.

**Explanation**

Use the same impulse-response rule from the tutorial: keep the expression in a first-order inverse-Laplace shape and immediately map it to a time-domain exponential with $u(t)$.

Cue: the transfer function already has one shifted pole and no repeated factors, so it is a direct-match case.

Step 1: write $H(s)$ in canonical form $\frac{K}{s+a}$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{s+2}=\frac{K}{s+a}\text{ with }K=1,\;a=2
$$

Step 2: identify the inverse pair for a first-order denominator term.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+a}\right\}=e^{-at}u(t)
$$

Step 3: apply the gain from the canonical coefficient and carry through causality.

Here $K=1$, so the gain does not change the magnitude. The final result is $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$.

Quick result check: the exponent sign is negative because the pole is at $s=-2$, so the response is stable and causal. The next step is the first variation with another wrinkle in $H(s)$.

## Finding an Impulse Response from Distinct Real Poles

**Example:** Find the causal impulse response $h(t)$ for $H(s)=\frac{6}{(s+1)(s+3)}$.

**Explanation**

This is the first distinct-real-pole case, so this section adds one extra denominator factor and uses partial fractions before inversion.

Step 1: set up partial fractions in the canonical first-order form for each pole.

$$
\frac{6}{(s+1)(s+3)}=\frac{A}{s+1}+\frac{B}{s+3}
$$

Step 2: multiply through by $(s+1)(s+3)$ and match coefficients.

$$
6=A(s+3)+B(s+1)
$$

Comparing coefficients gives $A+B=0$ and $3A+B=6$, so $A=3$ and $B=-3$.

$$
\frac{6}{(s+1)(s+3)}=\frac{3}{s+1}-\frac{3}{s+3}
$$

Step 3: apply the known inverse pairs for each first-order term and include $u(t)$ for causality.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+1}\right\}=e^{-t}u(t),\quad \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+3}\right\}=e^{-3t}u(t)
$$

Step 4: use linearity and combine results to state the final causal impulse response:

$$
h(t)=3e^{-t}u(t)-3e^{-3t}u(t)
$$

Check against the pole locations: both poles are in the left-half-plane, so the signal is causal with decaying modes.

## Finding an Impulse Response from a Transfer Function with a Zero

**Example:** Find the causal impulse response $h(t)$ for $H(s)=\frac{s+1}{(s+2)(s+4)}$.

**Explanation**

This follows the same inverse-Laplace flow as the first two examples, but the zero in the numerator adds one algebraic cue: split the rational function first so each pole stays in a first-order denominator.

Step 1: choose partial fractions for the distinct poles. Since the poles are at $s=-2$ and $s=-4$, set up the decomposition with one residue per pole.

$$
\frac{s+1}{(s+2)(s+4)}=\frac{A}{s+2}+\frac{B}{s+4}
$$

Step 2: multiply both sides by $(s+2)(s+4)$ and match coefficients on the numerator.

$$
s+1=A(s+4)+B(s+2)= (A+B)s + (4A+2B)
$$

Comparing coefficients gives $A+B=1$ and $4A+2B=1$, so $A=-\frac12$ and $B=\frac32$.

$$
\frac{s+1}{(s+2)(s+4)}=-\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{s+2}+\frac32\cdot\frac{1}{s+4}
$$

Step 3: invert each first-order term with the same pair rule used earlier and carry causality as $u(t)$.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+2}\right\}=e^{-2t}u(t),\quad \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{s+4}\right\}=e^{-4t}u(t)
$$

Step 4: apply linearity, then combine terms and simplify the final causal response.

$$
h(t)=-\frac12 e^{-2t}u(t)+\frac32 e^{-4t}u(t)
$$

Quick check: at $t=0^+$, $h(0^+)= -\frac12 + \frac32 =1$, matching the high-frequency coefficient implied by the transfer function numerator behavior.

The practical pattern is stable: the poles determine the exponentials, while the zero changes only residue values via algebraic expansion.

## Finding an Impulse Response from a Repeated Pole

**Example:** Find the causal impulse response $h(t)$ for $H(s)=\frac{2}{(s+3)^2}$.

**Explanation**

The same core inversion pattern now appears with one repeated pole: the numerator is a pure gain and the denominator is a repeated shifted first-order factor.

Step 1: match the transfer function directly to repeated-pole form $\frac{K}{(s+a)^2}$ and read off $K=2$ and $a=3$.

Step 2: confirm there are no distinct linear factors left to split; repeated-pole terms are handled by the special identity rather than partial fractions.

Step 3: apply the inverse-Laplace identity for a repeated pole and include causality.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s+a)^2}\right\}=t e^{-at}u(t)
$$

With $a=3$, $\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s+3)^2}\right\}=te^{-3t}u(t)$, so multiplying by $K=2$ gives the final causal impulse response.

$$
h(t)=2te^{-3t}u(t)
$$

This is the key repeated-pole signature to reuse: repeated real poles produce a polynomial-in-time term times a causal exponential, while non-repeated cases do not.

## Finding an Impulse Response from Complex-Conjugate Poles

**Example:** Find the causal impulse response for $H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+2)^2+9}$.

**Explanation**

The cue here is a shifted irreducible quadratic denominator, so this is the complex-conjugate case with a damped sinusoid inverse rather than pure exponentials.

Step 1: normalize the denominator as a standard form $(s+a)^2+\omega^2$. Here it is already in that form with $a=2$ and $\omega=3$.

$$
H(s)=\frac{1}{(s+2)^2+3^2}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{(s+2)^2+3^2}
$$

Step 2: match the numerator to the sine form constant, then scale by the missing factor. The identity is

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\omega}{(s+a)^2+\omega^2}\right\}=e^{-at}\sin(\omega t)u(t)
$$

Step 3: apply the identity to the normalized form and include causality.

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{(s+2)^2+3^2}\right\}=\frac{1}{3}e^{-2t}\sin(3t)u(t)
$$

Step 4: the final causal impulse response is therefore $h(t)=\frac{1}{3}e^{-2t}\sin(3t)u(t)$.
