## Introduction to Pure Real Exponentials

Set $s=\sigma$ in the generalized exponential $e^{st}$ and you get the pure real exponential $x(t)=e^{\sigma t}$. Here $t$ is the variable and $\sigma$ is a fixed real number, so the only thing the signal can do is change in magnitude over time.

$$
x(t)=e^{\sigma t}
$$

Use $\sigma=0$ as the base case. Then $e^{0t}=1$, which is the constant unit signal and the cleanest reference point for the sign rule.

$$
e^{0t}=1
$$

When $\sigma<0$, the signal decays toward $0$ as $t$ increases. When $\sigma>0$, it grows. In both cases the behavior is non-oscillatory because the exponent is real.

$$
\sigma<0 \Rightarrow x(t)\to 0,\qquad \sigma=0 \Rightarrow x(t)=1,\qquad \sigma>0 \Rightarrow x(t)\to \infty
$$

The size of $|\sigma|$ controls how fast the change happens: larger $|\sigma|$ means faster decay or faster growth. That sign-and-magnitude rule is the core way to read pure real exponentials.

## Recognizing $e^{\sigma t}$ from $s=\sigma$

**Example:** Let $s=-4$ in $e^{st}$. Rewrite the signal and identify whether it grows, decays, or stays constant.

**Explanation**

Because $s$ is real, write $s=\sigma$ with $\sigma=-4$. Substituting that value into $e^{st}$ gives the pure real exponential form in $t$.

$$
x(t)=e^{-4t}
$$

Since $\sigma=-4<0$, the exponent becomes more negative as $t$ increases, so the signal decays toward $0$. There is no oscillation here because the exponent is real.

$$
\sigma=-4<0,\qquad x(t)\to 0
$$

The magnitude $|\sigma|=4$ controls how fast the decay happens, but the sign is what tells you that this signal decays rather than grows or stays constant.

## Interpreting Decay When $\sigma<0$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-2t}$, describe the behavior as $t$ increases and explain why the exponent sign forces decay.

**Explanation**

The cue is a real coefficient, so write $s=\sigma$. Here $\sigma=-2<0$, which puts the signal in the decay case right away.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2t}
$$

Substituting gives $x(t)=e^{-2t}$. As $t$ increases, the exponent becomes more negative, so the signal shrinks toward $0$.

$$
\sigma=-2<0,\qquad x(t)\to 0
$$

Because the exponent is real, there is no oscillation. The sign tells you it decays, and the magnitude $|\sigma|=2$ sets how fast it decays.

## Interpreting Growth When $\sigma>0$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{3t}$, describe the behavior as $t$ increases and explain why the positive exponent forces growth.

**Explanation**

The cue is a real coefficient, so write $s=\sigma$. Here $\sigma=3>0$, which puts the signal in the growth case right away.

$$
x(t)=e^{3t}
$$

Substituting gives $x(t)=e^{3t}$. As $t$ increases, the exponent becomes more positive, so the signal increases without bound and never oscillates.

$$
\sigma=3>0,\qquad x(t)\to\infty
$$

Because the exponent is real, the rise is monotone rather than oscillatory. The sign tells you it grows, and the magnitude $|\sigma|=3$ sets how fast it grows.

## Interpreting Constancy When $\sigma=0$

**Example:** Set $\sigma=0$ in $e^{\sigma t}$ and determine the resulting signal.

**Explanation**

The cue is a real coefficient, so write $s=\sigma$. Here $\sigma=0$, which is the boundary case between growth and decay.

$$
x(t)=e^{0t}=e^0=1
$$

Substituting gives $x(t)=e^{0t}=1$. The value no longer depends on $t$, so the signal stays constant at the unit level.

$$
\sigma=0,\qquad x(t)=1
$$

Because the exponent is real and equal to zero, there is no oscillation and no growth or decay. The zero exponent produces the constant unit signal.

## Comparing Real Exponential Rates Using $|\sigma|$

**Example:** Compare $x(t)=e^{-2t}$ and $y(t)=e^{-5t}$ and decide which one decays faster.

**Explanation**

The cue is that both exponents are real and negative, so both signals are in the decay case. To compare their rates, look at the magnitudes $|\sigma|$.

$$
x(t)=e^{-2t},\qquad y(t)=e^{-5t}
$$

Here $|{-5}|>|{-2}|$, so $y(t)$ has the larger magnitude exponent. That means $y(t)$ changes faster in magnitude, so it decays faster than $x(t)$ as $t$ increases.

$$
|{-5}|>|{-2}|\;\Rightarrow\; e^{-5t}\text{ decays faster than }e^{-2t}
$$

The general rule is that the sign tells you whether the signal grows or decays, while the larger $|\sigma|$ tells you which one changes faster. In this case, both signals decay, and the one with the larger magnitude decays more quickly.
