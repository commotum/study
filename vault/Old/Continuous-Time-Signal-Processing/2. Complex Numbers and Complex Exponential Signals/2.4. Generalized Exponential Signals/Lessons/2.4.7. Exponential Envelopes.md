## Introduction to Exponential Envelopes

An exponential envelope is the real exponential factor $e^{\sigma t}$ inside $e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}$. It controls the size of the oscillation, while $e^{j\omega t}$ controls the oscillation itself.

$$
e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}=e^{\sigma t}e^{j\omega t}
$$

Write the signal as $e^{\sigma t}e^{j\omega t}$. That split separates the amplitude envelope from the oscillatory factor you already know.

$$
\left|e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}\right|=e^{\sigma t}
$$

The envelope is the magnitude scale: $\left|e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}\right|=e^{\sigma t}$. If you look at the real part, the oscillation stays between $-e^{\sigma t}$ and $e^{\sigma t}$.

$$
\Re\{e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}\}=e^{\sigma t}\cos(\omega t)
$$

The boundary case is $\sigma=0$, so $e^{\sigma t}=1$. In that case the envelope is flat and the signal keeps constant amplitude.

$$
\sigma=0\;\Rightarrow\;e^{\sigma t}=1
$$

From the sign of $\sigma$, you will later read decay when $\sigma<0$ and growth when $\sigma>0$, using the same factorization each time.

## Identifying the Envelope in $e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t}$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{(-3+j4)t}$, identify the exponential envelope and the oscillatory factor.

**Explanation**

The cue is the mixed exponent $(-3+j4)t$. The real part $-3$ sets the amplitude envelope, and the imaginary part $j4$ gives the oscillation you already know.

$$
e^{(-3+j4)t}=e^{-3t}e^{j4t}
$$

Rewrite the signal as $e^{-3t}e^{j4t}$. That split isolates $e^{-3t}$ as the exponential envelope and leaves $e^{j4t}$ as the oscillatory factor.

So the envelope is $e^{-3t}$. That is the factor that later tells you whether the signal decays or grows from the sign of $\sigma$.

## Interpreting a Decaying Envelope with $\sigma<0$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{(-3+j4)t}$, explain how the envelope behaves as $t$ increases.

**Explanation**

The cue is the negative real part $\sigma=-3$. That means the same factorization from the previous example will produce an envelope that shrinks as time increases.

$$
x(t)=e^{-3t}e^{j4t}
$$

Write $x(t)=e^{-3t}e^{j4t}$. The factor $e^{-3t}$ is the exponential envelope, and the negative exponent means that envelope decays toward zero as $t$ increases.

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-3t}=0
$$

So the oscillation keeps going, but it is bounded by a shrinking envelope. That is the damping behavior you read from $\sigma<0$.

## Interpreting a Growing Envelope with $\sigma>0$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{(3+j4)t}$, explain how the envelope behaves as $t$ increases.

**Explanation**

The cue is the positive real part $\sigma=3$. That means the same factorization from the previous example will produce an envelope that expands as time increases.

$$
x(t)=e^{3t}e^{j4t}
$$

Write $x(t)=e^{3t}e^{j4t}$. The factor $e^{3t}$ is the exponential envelope, and the positive exponent means that envelope grows without bound as $t$ increases.

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{3t}=\infty
$$

So the oscillation keeps going, but it is bounded by an expanding envelope. That is the growth behavior you read from $\sigma>0$.

## Recognizing a Constant-Amplitude Envelope with $\sigma=0$

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j4t}$, identify the envelope and explain why the amplitude stays constant.

**Explanation**

The cue is the zero real part: this is the boundary case between decay and growth, so the envelope should be flat.

$$
x(t)=e^{(0+j4)t}=e^{0t}e^{j4t}
$$

Rewrite the signal as $e^{0t}e^{j4t}$. The factor $e^{0t}$ is the envelope, and because $e^{0t}=1$, the amplitude stays constant.

$$
e^{0t}=1
$$

So the signal only oscillates; it neither grows nor decays. That is the constant-amplitude behavior you read from $\sigma=0$.
