## Introduction to Magnitude and Phase of a Complex Exponential

When you see $x(t)=C e^{j\omega t}$, read the expression as a constant complex coefficient multiplying a rotating unit-magnitude exponential. The coefficient $C$ sets the constant magnitude, and the exponential supplies the time-varying angle.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

That base case is the anchor: $e^{j\omega t}$ already has magnitude $1$ and phase $\omega t$. So multiplying by $C$ can only scale the radius and add a fixed angle to the rotation.

Write the coefficient in polar form as $C=|C|e^{j\angle C}$ so its magnitude and angle are explicit. Then the coefficient and the rotating exponential combine in one read-off rule.

$$
C=|C|e^{j\angle C}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C e^{j\omega t}=|C|e^{j(\angle C+\omega t)}
$$

So the magnitude of $C e^{j\omega t}$ is $|C|$, and the phase is $\angle C+\omega t$. That is the rule this topic will reuse: separate the constant amplitude from the time-varying angle, and the same complex exponential becomes a rotating phasor with a clear sinusoidal interpretation.

## Finding Magnitude and Phase of a Unit Complex Exponential

**Example:** Find the magnitude and phase of $x(t)=e^{j7t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit-coefficient signal $x(t)=e^{j7t}$, so read it as $C e^{j\omega t}$ with $C=1$ and $\omega=7$.

$$
C=1,\qquad |C|=1,\qquad \angle C=0
$$

Since the coefficient is $1$, it contributes no scaling beyond a unit magnitude and no extra angle. The only time-varying part is the rotation $7t$.

$$
|x(t)|=1,\qquad \angle x(t)=7t
$$

So the magnitude is $1$ and the phase is $7t$. This is the base case that later examples will extend when the coefficient is no longer $1$.

## Finding Magnitude and Phase with a Positive Real Coefficient

**Example:** Find the magnitude and phase of $x(t)=4e^{j5t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the positive real coefficient in $x(t)=4e^{j5t}$, so read it as $C e^{j\omega t}$ with $C=4$ and $\omega=5$.

$$
C=4,\qquad |4|=4,\qquad \angle 4=0
$$

Since $4$ is positive and real, it contributes only magnitude scaling. The phase comes only from the rotating term $e^{j5t}$, so there is no extra angle from the coefficient.

$$
|x(t)|=4,\qquad \angle x(t)=5t
$$

So the magnitude is $4$ and the phase is $5t$. This is the same read-off rule as the unit case, with the coefficient changing only the radius.

## Finding Magnitude and Phase with a Polar Coefficient

**Example:** Find the magnitude and phase of $x(t)=4e^{j\pi/6}e^{j2t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the polar coefficient in $x(t)=4e^{j\pi/6}e^{j2t}$. Read it as $C e^{j\omega t}$ with $C=4e^{j\pi/6}$ and $\omega=2$.

$$
C=4e^{j\pi/6},\qquad |C|=4,\qquad \angle C=\pi/6
$$

Because the coefficient is already in polar form, its magnitude is the radius $4$ and its angle is $\pi/6$. The rotating exponential contributes the additional angle $2t$.

$$
4e^{j\pi/6}e^{j2t}=4e^{j(\pi/6+2t)}
$$

So the magnitude is $4$ and the phase is $\pi/6+2t$. This is the same read-off rule as before, with the coefficient angle carried straight into the total phase.

## Finding Magnitude and Phase with a Rectangular Coefficient

**Example:** Find the magnitude and phase of $x(t)=(1-j\sqrt{3})e^{j2t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the rectangular coefficient in $x(t)=(1-j\sqrt{3})e^{j2t}$. Treat the signal as $C e^{j\omega t}$ with $C=1-j\sqrt{3}$ and $\omega=2$.

$$
|1-j\sqrt{3}|=\sqrt{1^2+(\sqrt{3})^2}=2,\qquad \angle(1-j\sqrt{3})=-\pi/3
$$

The coefficient lies in quadrant IV, so its principal angle is $-\pi/3$. Rewrite it in polar form as $2e^{-j\pi/3}$ before combining it with the rotating term.

$$
x(t)=2e^{-j\pi/3}e^{j2t}=2e^{j(2t-\pi/3)}
$$

So the magnitude is $2$ and the phase is $2t-\pi/3$. The rectangular coefficient has been converted to polar form, and its angle is carried directly into the total phase.

## Evaluating the Phase at a Specific Time

**Example:** Evaluate the phase of $x(t)=2e^{j(\pi/4+3t)}$ at $t=\pi/6$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the phase expression inside the exponential: $\pi/4+3t$. The magnitude stays fixed at $2$, so the only job here is to substitute the given time into the phase.

$$
\angle x(t)=\pi/4+3t
$$

Substitute $t=\pi/6$ into the phase formula.

$$
\angle x\left(\pi/6\right)=\pi/4+3\left(\pi/6\right)=\pi/4+\pi/2
$$

Now simplify the angle to a single exact value.

$$
\angle x\left(\pi/6\right)=3\pi/4
$$

So the phase at $t=\pi/6$ is $3\pi/4$. The magnitude remains $2$, but the phase evaluation is the new step.
