## Introduction to Complex Exponentials

A complex exponential like $e^{j\theta}$ is a point on the unit circle written in polar form. Euler's formula tells you exactly how to read it: the real part is $\cos\theta$ and the imaginary part is $\sin\theta$.

$$
e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta
$$

That means the point never leaves the unit circle, because its magnitude is $\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}=1$. So the complex exponential has unit length even while its angle changes.

$$
|e^{j\omega t}|=\sqrt{\cos^2(\omega t)+\sin^2(\omega t)}=1
$$

Now set $\theta=\omega t$. At $t=0$, $e^{j\omega 0}=1$, so the phasor starts on the positive real axis. As $t$ increases, the angle advances to $\omega t$, which means the point rotates continuously at angular frequency $\omega$.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

That rotating-point view is the one you will reuse later for phasors, complex amplitudes, and Fourier representations.

## Recognizing $e^{j\omega t}$ as Unit Magnitude

**Example:** Show that $x(t)=e^{j4t}$ has magnitude 1 for every $t$ and identify what that means geometrically.

**Explanation**

Start by rewriting $x(t)=e^{j4t}$ with Euler's formula. That gives the real part $\cos(4t)$ and the imaginary part $\sin(4t)$.

$$
x(t)=e^{j4t}=\cos(4t)+j\sin(4t)
$$

Now compute the magnitude from those components: $|x(t)|=\sqrt{\cos^2(4t)+\sin^2(4t)}$.

$$
|x(t)|=\sqrt{\cos^2(4t)+\sin^2(4t)}=1
$$

Because the magnitude is always $1$, the point traced by $e^{j4t}$ stays on the unit circle. The factor $4$ changes the rotation rate, not the radius.

<a id="finding-the-angle-of-ejomega-t-at-a-given-time"></a>
## Finding the Angle of $e^{j\omega t}$ at a Given Time

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j2t}$, find the angle at $t=\pi/4$ and state the corresponding position on the unit circle.

**Explanation**

The cue is the exponent $j2t$: in $e^{j\omega t}$, the angle is $\theta=\omega t$. So for this signal, set $\theta=2t$ and substitute the given time.

$$
\theta=\omega t=2\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}
$$

No reduction is needed here because $\pi/2$ is already a standard angle. That means the phasor turns one quarter of a circle counterclockwise from the positive real axis.

$$
x\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=e^{j\pi/2}
$$

So the angle at $t=\pi/4$ is $\pi/2$, and the corresponding point on the unit circle is $j$ at the top of the circle.

<a id="locating-ejomega-t-on-the-unit-circle"></a>
## Locating $e^{j\omega t}$ on the Unit Circle

**Example:** Locate $x(t)=e^{j\pi/3}$ on the unit circle by writing its rectangular coordinates and naming the quadrant.

**Explanation**

The cue is the exponential form $e^{j\theta}$. By Euler's formula, that point on the unit circle has rectangular coordinates $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$.

$$
e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta
$$

Here the angle is $\theta=\pi/3$, so substitute that value into the cosine and sine. That gives the coordinates of the point directly.

$$
\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3},\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
$$

Both coordinates are positive, so the point is in the first quadrant. In rectangular form, $x(t)=e^{j\pi/3}=\frac{1}{2}+j\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

<a id="interpreting-angular-frequency-as-rotation-rate"></a>
## Interpreting Angular Frequency as Rotation Rate

**Example:** Compare $x_1(t)=e^{j2t}$ and $x_2(t)=e^{j5t}$ and determine which rotates faster and how much phase each accumulates by $t=\pi/2$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the coefficient of $t$ in $e^{j\omega t}$. That coefficient is the angular rotation rate, so read off $\omega_1=2$ and $\omega_2=5$.

$$
\theta_1(t)=2t,\qquad \theta_2(t)=5t
$$

Because phase grows as $\theta(t)=\omega t$, the larger $|\omega|$ turns faster. Here both rates are positive, so both signals rotate counterclockwise, but $x_2(t)$ advances more quickly.

$$
\theta_1\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\pi,\qquad \theta_2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=5\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{5\pi}{2}
$$

So by $t=\pi/2$, $x_1(t)$ has accumulated $\pi$ radians and $x_2(t)$ has accumulated $5\pi/2$ radians. The larger angular frequency gives the faster rotation, and a negative $\omega$ would reverse direction without changing the speed rule.
