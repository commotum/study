Tutorial blocks:
- paragraph:
  text: A phasor is a vector in the complex plane whose tip moves as it rotates. Its real-axis projection is the horizontal shadow on the real axis, and that shadow is the cosine-valued signal we want to read from the motion.
- image:
  image_slot_id: section-001
- paragraph:
  text: Start with the simplest base case: a unit phasor on the positive real axis projects to $1$. If you scale that same picture to magnitude $A$ and tilt it to angle $\theta$, the real-axis shadow is the adjacent side, so its length is $A\cos\theta$.
- display-math:
  latex: \operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j\theta}\}=A\cos\theta
- paragraph:
  text: The same rule is written compactly by taking the real part of $A e^{j\theta}$. That notation says to keep the horizontal component of the phasor and ignore the vertical one.
- paragraph:
  text: Now let the angle vary with time. For counterclockwise rotation at angular frequency $\omega$ with initial phase $\phi$, the angle is $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$.
- display-math:
  latex: \theta(t)=\omega t+\phi,\qquad x(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)
- paragraph:
  text: So the real-axis projection of the rotating phasor is exactly the cosine signal $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$. Later sections keep the same projection rule and vary only the rotation direction or the time at which you evaluate it.
