## Introduction to Counterclockwise and Clockwise Rotation

When a phasor is written as $e^{j\theta(t)}$ with $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$, read the rotation direction from how the phase angle changes over time. The starting angle comes from $\phi$; the motion comes from the sign of $\omega$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi
$$

Take a small positive time step $\Delta t>0$. Then the phase change is $\theta(t+\Delta t)-\theta(t)=\omega\Delta t$, so the sign of the phase change matches the sign of $\omega$.

$$
\theta(t+\Delta t)-\theta(t)=\omega\Delta t
$$

If $\omega>0$, the angle increases as $t$ increases. Increasing angle means counterclockwise rotation.

If $\omega<0$, the angle decreases as $t$ increases. Decreasing angle means clockwise rotation.

Use the unit phasor $e^{j\omega t}$ with $\phi=0$ as the clean base case: it keeps the focus on the sign of $\omega$ alone. The initial phase only sets the starting angle, and it does not change the direction of rotation.

## Positive Angular Frequency as Counterclockwise Rotation

**Example:** A phasor is given by $e^{j(4t+\pi/6)}$. Does it rotate clockwise or counterclockwise?

**Explanation**

Read the exponent as the phase angle $\theta(t)=4t+\pi/6$. The sign of the coefficient of $t$ is what tells you the direction of rotation.

Here the angular frequency is $\omega=4$, so it is positive. A positive $\omega$ means the phase angle increases as $t$ increases.

An increasing phase angle means the phasor moves counterclockwise around the complex plane.

The phase offset $\pi/6$ only sets the starting angle. It does not change the direction, so $e^{j(4t+\pi/6)}$ rotates counterclockwise.

## Classifying Rotation Direction from the Sign of Angular Frequency

**Example:** A phasor is written as $e^{-j(3t-\pi/8)}$. Does it rotate clockwise or counterclockwise after rewriting the exponent?

**Explanation**

Rewrite the exponent so the coefficient of $t$ is visible: $e^{-j(3t-\pi/8)}=e^{j(-3t+\pi/8)}$. The direction comes from the sign of the $t$-term, not from the phase offset.

After rewriting, read $\omega=-3$. Since $\omega$ is negative, the phase angle decreases as $t$ increases.

A decreasing phase angle means the phasor rotates clockwise.

The phase offset $\pi/8$ only sets the starting angle. It does not change the direction, so the phasor rotates clockwise.

<a id="recognizing-that-initial-phase-does-not-change-rotation-direction"></a>
## Recognizing That Initial Phase Does Not Change Rotation Direction

**Example:** Two phasors are given by $e^{j(4t+\pi/6)}$ and $e^{j(4t-\pi/3)}$. Do they rotate in the same direction or different directions?

**Explanation**

Read both exponents as phase angles: $4t+\pi/6$ and $4t-\pi/3$. The coefficient of $t$ is the same in both, so the shared angular frequency is $\omega=4$.

The phase offsets $\pi/6$ and $-\pi/3$ only change the starting angle. They move each phasor to a different initial position, but they do not change how the angle progresses with time.

Because the time-varying part is still $+4t$, the phase angle increases as $t$ increases. An increasing phase angle means counterclockwise rotation.

So they rotate in the same direction. Because $\omega=4>0$ in both cases, both phasors rotate counterclockwise; changing $\phi$ changes only the starting angle.
