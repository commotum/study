## Introduction to Positive and Negative Frequency Components

From the phasor view, $e^{j\omega t}$ is the positive-frequency component and $e^{-j\omega t}$ is the negative-frequency component. For a real sinusoid, read the signal as a matched $+\omega$/$-\omega$ pair whose imaginary parts cancel.

$$
\cos(\omega t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j\omega t}+e^{-j\omega t}\right)
$$

The cleanest base case is $\cos(\omega t)$. Its split is a sum of equal coefficients at $+\omega$ and $-\omega$, so the two complex exponentials form a conjugate pair and the result stays real.

$$
\sin(\omega t)=\frac{1}{2j}\left(e^{j\omega t}-e^{-j\omega t}\right)
$$

$\sin(\omega t)$ uses the same two-frequency split. The factor $1/j$ only changes the coefficients, not the frequency labels, so the $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ terms still appear as a conjugate pair.

$$
A\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}+\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega t}
$$

When amplitude and phase are added, keep the time factors $e^{j\omega t}$ and $e^{-j\omega t}$ fixed and move the phase into the coefficients. That gives a positive-frequency component with coefficient $\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}$ and a negative-frequency component with coefficient $\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}$.

$$
A\sin(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega t}
$$

The same rule works for $A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$. The coefficients still come in matched $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ terms, and the pair remains conjugate even though the common scale includes $1/j$.

The reusable rule is simple: a real sinusoid must have matched $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ components with conjugate coefficients, and those two terms are the counter-rotating phasor components of the signal.

## Decomposing a Cosine into Positive and Negative Frequency Components

**Example:** Rewrite $3\cos(4t-\pi/6)$ as a sum of positive- and negative-frequency complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is a cosine with both amplitude and phase, so start from the cosine conjugate-pair identity from the previous section. Keep the $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ terms separate and move the phase into the coefficients.

$$
\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}\right)
$$

Here $\omega=4$, $A=3$, and $\phi=-\pi/6$. Substituting those values gives one positive-frequency term at $+4$ and one negative-frequency term at $-4$, with the phase carried by the coefficients.

$$
3\cos(4t-\pi/6)=\frac{3}{2}e^{-j\pi/6}e^{j4t}+\frac{3}{2}e^{j\pi/6}e^{-j4t}
$$

The $+4$ component has coefficient $\frac{3}{2}e^{-j\pi/6}$, and the $-4$ component has the conjugate coefficient $\frac{3}{2}e^{j\pi/6}$. Because the coefficients are conjugates, the two counter-rotating components sum to the real cosine.

<a id="decomposing-a-sine-into-positive-and-negative-frequency-components"></a>
## Decomposing a Sine into Positive and Negative Frequency Components

**Example:** Rewrite $4\sin(2t+\pi/4)$ as a sum of positive- and negative-frequency complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

The cue is a sine with amplitude and phase, so switch from the cosine identity to the sine identity and keep the $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ terms explicit. The only new wrinkle relative to the cosine example is the $1/j$ factor and the subtraction between the two frequency components.

$$
\sin(\omega t+\phi)=\frac{1}{2j}\left(e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}-e^{-j(\omega t+\phi)}\right)
$$

Here $\omega=2$, $A=4$, and $\phi=\pi/4$. Substitute those values, then split each exponential into a coefficient and a frequency-sign term without changing the notation.

$$
4\sin(2t+\pi/4)=\frac{4}{2j}e^{j\pi/4}e^{j2t}-\frac{4}{2j}e^{-j\pi/4}e^{-j2t}
$$

The $+2$ component has coefficient $\frac{4}{2j}e^{j\pi/4}$, and the $-2$ component has the conjugate coefficient $-\frac{4}{2j}e^{-j\pi/4}$. The subtraction is exactly what makes the two counter-rotating components combine to the real sine.

<a id="identifying-conjugate-coefficients-in-a-real-sinusoid"></a>
## Identifying Conjugate Coefficients in a Real Sinusoid

**Example:** Given $x(t)=\frac{5}{2}(1-j)e^{j3t}+\frac{5}{2}(1+j)e^{-j3t}$, identify the positive-frequency coefficient, the negative-frequency coefficient, and whether the signal is real.

**Explanation**

The cue is a pair of complex exponentials at $+3$ and $-3$, so read the coefficients directly from the terms rather than expanding anything. This is exactly the conjugate-pair test from the previous sections.

$$
x(t)=\frac{5}{2}(1-j)e^{j3t}+\frac{5}{2}(1+j)e^{-j3t}
$$

The coefficient of $e^{j3t}$ is the positive-frequency coefficient, so the $+3$ component is $\frac{5}{2}(1-j)$. The coefficient of $e^{-j3t}$ is the negative-frequency coefficient, so the $-3$ component is $\frac{5}{2}(1+j)$.

$$
\frac{5}{2}(1+j)=\left(\frac{5}{2}(1-j)\right)^{*}
$$

The two coefficients are complex conjugates, so the pair is conjugate-symmetric. That means this expression can represent a real sinusoid, with the two terms acting as counter-rotating phasor components.

<a id="interpreting-counter-rotating-phasor-components"></a>
## Interpreting Counter-Rotating Phasor Components

**Example:** Interpret the components of $x(t)=\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}+\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega t}$ as two phasors rotating in opposite directions.

**Explanation**

The cue is the sign in the exponential. The $e^{j\omega t}$ term is the positive-frequency phasor, so it rotates counterclockwise at angular speed $\omega$. The $e^{-j\omega t}$ term is the negative-frequency phasor, so it rotates clockwise at the same speed.

$$
x(t)=\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}+\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega t}
$$

The coefficients are conjugates, $\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}$ and $\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}$. That symmetry means the two rotating vectors are mirror images about the real axis, which is the signature of a real-valued sinusoid.

$$
\left(\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}\right)^{*}=\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}
$$

When you add the two components, their imaginary parts cancel at every time. What remains is a real signal built from the real-axis projections of the counter-rotating phasors.
