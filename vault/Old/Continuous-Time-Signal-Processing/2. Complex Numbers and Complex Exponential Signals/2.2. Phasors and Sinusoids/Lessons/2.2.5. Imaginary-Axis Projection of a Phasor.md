## Introduction to Imaginary-Axis Projection of a Phasor

In the phasor picture, the tip moves in the complex plane. The imaginary-axis shadow is the sine-valued signal we want, because that shadow is the phasor's imaginary component.

![](<../Source/2.2.5. Imaginary-Axis Projection of a Phasor/Images/l005-s001-te-section-001.png>)

Start with a unit phasor on the positive real axis, then rotate it to angle $\theta$. Its imaginary-axis projection is the vertical component, so a phasor of magnitude $A$ at angle $\theta$ projects to $A\sin\theta$.

$$
\operatorname{Im}\{A e^{j\theta}\}=A\sin\theta
$$

In complex-number language, that rule is just taking the imaginary part of $A e^{j\theta}$. Keep this notation fixed; it is the same quantity the picture is showing.

Now let the angle depend on time as $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$ for counterclockwise rotation. The same projection becomes the time-domain signal we read from the imaginary axis.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi,\qquad x(t)=\operatorname{Im}\{A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=A\sin(\omega t+\phi)
$$

So the bridge is simple: imaginary-axis projection equals imaginary part, and a rotating phasor produces $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$. Later sections only vary magnitude, phase, and rotation direction.

<a id="projecting-a-unit-counterclockwise-phasor-onto-the-imaginary-axis"></a>
## Projecting a Unit Counterclockwise Phasor onto the Imaginary Axis

**Example:** A unit phasor starts on the positive real axis and rotates counterclockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write its imaginary-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the simplest case: the phasor has unit magnitude, no initial phase offset, and counterclockwise rotation. That means the angle grows as $\theta(t)=\omega t$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t
$$

For an imaginary-axis projection, take the vertical component of the rotating phasor. In complex form, that is the imaginary part of $e^{j\theta(t)}$.

$$
x(t)=\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\theta(t)}\}=\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\omega t}\}
$$

Use Euler's form to separate the real and imaginary parts: $e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)$. The imaginary part is the sine term, so the projected signal is $x(t)=\sin(\omega t)$.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

So the unit counterclockwise phasor maps directly to the sine-valued time signal $x(t)=\sin(\omega t)$.

## Projecting a Scaled Phasor onto the Imaginary Axis

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts on the positive real axis and rotates counterclockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write its imaginary-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the same counterclockwise motion as the previous example, but the phasor now has magnitude $A$. The angle still grows as $\theta(t)=\omega t$, so the only new factor is the scale.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t
$$

For the imaginary-axis projection, take the imaginary part of the rotating phasor and keep the magnitude $A$ outside the projection rule.

$$
x(t)=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\theta(t)}\}=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\omega t}\}
$$

Use Euler's form, $e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)$, to separate the imaginary part. The sine term gets scaled by $A$, so the projection signal is $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t)$.

$$
e^{j\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+j\sin(\omega t)
$$

So the scaled counterclockwise phasor produces the sine-valued signal $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t)$.

## Projecting a Phase-Shifted Phasor onto the Imaginary Axis

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts at phase $\phi$ and rotates counterclockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write its imaginary-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the same counterclockwise motion as before, but the phasor starts with an initial phase $\phi$. That means the angle is $\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi$ and the magnitude is still $A$.

$$
\theta(t)=\omega t+\phi
$$

For the imaginary-axis projection, take the imaginary part of the rotating phasor and keep the magnitude $A$ outside the rule.

$$
x(t)=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\theta(t)}\}=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}
$$

Use Euler's form, $e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}=\cos(\omega t+\phi)+j\sin(\omega t+\phi)$, to separate the imaginary part. The phase shift stays inside the sine, so the projection signal is $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$.

$$
e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}=\cos(\omega t+\phi)+j\sin(\omega t+\phi)
$$

So the phase-shifted counterclockwise phasor produces the sine-valued signal $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$.

## Projecting a Clockwise Phasor onto the Imaginary Axis

**Example:** A phasor of magnitude $A$ starts at phase $\phi$ and rotates clockwise with angular frequency $\omega$. Write its imaginary-axis projection signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the same magnitude and starting phase as before, but the phasor now rotates clockwise. That means the angle decreases as $\theta(t)=\phi-\omega t$.

$$
\theta(t)=\phi-\omega t
$$

For the imaginary-axis projection, take the imaginary part of the rotating phasor and keep the magnitude $A$ outside the rule.

$$
x(t)=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j\theta(t)}\}=A\operatorname{Im}\{e^{j(\phi-\omega t)}\}
$$

Use Euler's form, $e^{j(\phi-\omega t)}=\cos(\phi-\omega t)+j\sin(\phi-\omega t)$, to separate the imaginary part. The clockwise sign is carried by the angle, so the projection signal is $x(t)=A\sin(\phi-\omega t)$.

$$
e^{j(\phi-\omega t)}=\cos(\phi-\omega t)+j\sin(\phi-\omega t)
$$

So the clockwise phasor produces the sine-valued signal $x(t)=A\sin(\phi-\omega t)$.
