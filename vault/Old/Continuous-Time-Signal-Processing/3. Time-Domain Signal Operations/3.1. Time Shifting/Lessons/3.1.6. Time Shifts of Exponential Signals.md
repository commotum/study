## Introduction to Time Shifts of Exponential Signals

For an exponential signal like $e^{at}$ or $e^{st}$, a time shift changes only the constant multiplier. The time-dependent exponential shape stays the same, so the first thing to look for is whether the input has become $t-T$ or $t+T$ inside the exponent.

$$
e^{a(t-T)} = e^{at-aT} = e^{-aT}e^{at}
$$

In the delay case, distribute the exponent first, then separate the part that still depends on $t$ from the part that does not. The delayed exponential is just the original exponential multiplied by the constant factor $e^{-aT}$.

$$
e^{a(t+T)} = e^{at+aT} = e^{aT}e^{at}
$$

The advance case works the same way, except the constant factor has the opposite sign in the exponent. So a delay gives $e^{-aT}$ and an advance gives $e^{aT}$, while the exponential part in $t$ remains unchanged.

$$
e^{s(t-T)} = e^{-sT}e^{st}
$$

The same algebra works for $e^{st}$ with complex $s$ as well. This is the reusable rule for the rest of the lesson: substitute $t-T$ or $t+T$ into the exponential, distribute the exponent, and pull the shift out as a constant multiplier.

## Delaying a Real Exponential Signal

**Example:** If $x(t)=e^{3t}$, find the delayed signal $x(t-2)$ in simplified form.

**Explanation**

Use the delay rule from the introduction and substitute $t-2$ into the original exponential $x(t)=e^{3t}$. The cue is the shift inside the input, and the goal is to rewrite the result as a constant times the same exponential shape.

$$
x(t-2)=e^{3(t-2)}=e^{3t-6}
$$

Now separate the time-dependent part from the constant part. The $3t$ term stays inside the exponential shape, and the $-6$ term becomes a constant multiplier outside.

$$
x(t-2)=e^{-6}e^{3t}
$$

So the delayed signal is $e^{-6}e^{3t}$. The delay changes only the constant factor in front; it does not change the exponential dependence on $t$.

## Factoring a Shift in $e^{st}$

**Example:** Rewrite $e^{s(t-T)}$ in the form $C e^{st}$ and identify $C$.

**Explanation**

Start from the shifted exponential $e^{s(t-T)}$ and treat the shift as part of the exponent. The cue is the form $t-T$, and the goal is to separate the $t$-dependent factor from the constant factor.

$$
e^{s(t-T)}=e^{st-sT}
$$

Now split the exponent into a time term and a shift term. The $st$ part stays with $e^{st}$, while the $-sT$ part becomes the constant multiplier.

$$
e^{s(t-T)}=e^{-sT}e^{st}
$$

So the expression is $C e^{st}$ with $C=e^{-sT}$. This is the general delay identity, and the matching advance form is $e^{s(t+T)}=e^{sT}e^{st}$.

## Shifting Damped or Growing Sinusoids

**Example:** If $x(t)=e^{(-1+j4)t}$, write the delayed signal $x(t-2)$ in factored form.

**Explanation**

Use the same shift rule with the complex exponential $x(t)=e^{(-1+j4)t}$. The cue is the delay $t-2$, and the goal is to factor the result into a constant multiplier times the original time dependence.

$$
x(t-2)=e^{(-1+j4)(t-2)}=e^{(-1+j4)t+2-j8}
$$

Now separate the constant part from the $t$-dependent part. The $(-1+j4)t$ term stays with the original complex exponential, and the $2-j8$ term becomes a multiplier outside.

$$
x(t-2)=e^{2-j8}e^{(-1+j4)t}=e^{2}e^{-j8}e^{(-1+j4)t}
$$

So the delayed signal is $e^{2}e^{-j8}e^{(-1+j4)t}$. The shift changes only the constant amplitude and phase factors; it does not change the exponential's time dependence.
