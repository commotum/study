## Introduction to Comparing Time Shifts and Time Scaling

Track one labeled landmark in a reference signal. If $x(t)$ has a peak at $t=3$, that peak is the cue we will follow through both kinds of time transformation.

$$
y(t)=x(t-t_s)
$$

For a shift, the same landmark keeps its shape and moves by a constant offset. If the original peak is at $t=3$, then $x(t-t_s)$ places it at $t=3+t_s$, so every key time changes by the same additive amount.

$$
t_{new}=t_{old}+t_s
$$

$$
y(t)=x(at)
$$

For a scale with positive $a$, the landmark moves by multiplication instead of addition. If the original peak is at $t=3$, then $x(at)$ places it at $t=3/a$, so all landmark times are compressed or expanded relative to $t=0$.

$$
t_{new}=t_{old}/a
$$

Use this comparison test: the same additive offset means a time shift, while uniform multiplication or division of landmark times means time scaling. Shifts preserve spacing between features, but scaling changes spacing relative to $t=0$.

## Comparing One Key Time Under a Shift and a Scale

**Example:** A signal $x(t)$ has a peak at $t=3$. Compare the peak location in $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ and in $y_2(t)=x(4t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the peak at $t=3$ in the reference signal $x(t)$. For each transformed signal, match the inside argument to that original peak time and solve for $t$.

$$
t-2=3
$$

$$
t=5
$$

So the peak in $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ lands at $t=5$. A shift adds the same constant offset to the landmark time.

$$
4t=3
$$

$$
t=\frac{3}{4}
$$

So the peak in $y_2(t)=x(4t)$ lands at $t=\frac{3}{4}$. A scale divides the landmark time by the scale factor and pulls it toward $t=0$.

The contrast is the rule to remember: shifts add a constant offset to every key time, while scaling multiplies the argument and changes the key time by a factor.

## Comparing Multiple Key Times Under a Shift and a Scale

**Example:** The signal $x(t)$ has key times at $t=2$, $t=4$, and $t=6$. Compare the key times in $y_1(t)=x(t-3)$ and in $y_2(t)=x(2t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the set of key times $t=2$, $t=4$, and $t=6$ in the reference signal $x(t)$. Use the same landmark rule as before: a shift adds the same offset to every key time, while a scale divides every key time by the scale factor.

$$
2+3=5,\quad 4+3=7,\quad 6+3=9
$$

So $y_1(t)=x(t-3)$ places the key times at $5$, $7$, and $9$. The gaps stay the same, so the whole pattern has shifted rigidly by a constant amount.

$$
2/2=1,\quad 4/2=2,\quad 6/2=3
$$

So $y_2(t)=x(2t)$ places the key times at $1$, $2$, and $3$. The gaps shrink from $2$ to $1$, so the pattern is compressed toward $t=0$.

The comparison rule is now visible in the spacing: equal additive offsets preserve the gaps, while uniform division changes the gaps. That is the cue for distinguishing a shift from a scale when more than one key time is present.

## Identifying Translation vs Compression from Key-Time Spacing

**Example:** The signal $x(t)$ has key times at $t=1$ and $t=5$. One candidate transformed signal has them at $t=4$ and $t=8$; another has them at $t=1/2$ and $t=5/2$. Decide which candidate is a translation and which is a compression, and name the shift or scale factor.

**Explanation**

The cue is the spacing between the key times. In the reference signal, the distance from $t=1$ to $t=5$ is $4$, so a translation must keep that gap unchanged, while a compression must change it by a uniform factor.

$$
5-1=4
$$

$$
8-4=4
$$

Candidate 1 keeps the spacing at $4$, so it is a translation. The key times move from $1$ and $5$ to $4$ and $8$, which is a shift right by $3$.

$$
5/2-1/2=2
$$

Candidate 2 cuts the spacing from $4$ to $2$, so it is a compression by factor $2$. The key times move from $1$ and $5$ to $1/2$ and $5/2$, which is the same as dividing every time by $2$.

So the classification is: candidate 1 is a translation with offset $3$, and candidate 2 is a compression with factor $2$. The spacing test is the rule to remember: unchanged gaps mean translation; uniformly reduced gaps mean compression.

## Distinguishing Shift and Scale in Piecewise Signals

**Example:** The piecewise signal $x(t)$ equals $2$ from $t=1$ to $t=4$ and $0$ elsewhere. Compare the breakpoints in $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ and $y_2(t)=x(2t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the breakpoint pair $t=1$ and $t=4$ that turns the level $2$ on and off. The original width is $4-1=3$, so a shift must move both breakpoints by the same offset, while a scale must solve the transformed argument at each breakpoint.

$$
1+2=3,\quad 4+2=6
$$

So $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ is $2$ on the interval from $t=3$ to $t=6$ and $0$ elsewhere. The width stays $3$, so this is a translation by $2$.

$$
2t=1,\quad 2t=4
$$

$$
t=1/2,\quad t=2
$$

So $y_2(t)=x(2t)$ is $2$ on the interval from $t=1/2$ to $t=2$ and $0$ elsewhere. The width shrinks from $3$ to $3/2$, so this is a compression by factor $2$. The piecewise levels stay the same; only the breakpoint locations change.

The comparison rule is now clear: equal additive offsets mean translation, while uniformly divided breakpoint spacing means compression or expansion. Use the breakpoints and the interval width to classify the transformed piecewise signal.

## Distinguishing Shift and Scale in Exponential Signals

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{-t}$, the value $e^{-2}$ occurs at $t=2$. Compare the time of that same level in $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ and in $y_2(t)=x(3t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the level $e^{-2}$ in the exponential signal. For $x(t)=e^{-t}$, that level occurs at $t=2$, so we track the time at which the same level appears after each transformation.

$$
x(2)=e^{-2}
$$

$$
t-2=2
$$

$$
t=4
$$

So $y_1(t)=x(t-2)$ reaches the level $e^{-2}$ at $t=4$. The shift adds the same offset to the level time.

$$
3t=2
$$

$$
t=2/3
$$

So $y_2(t)=x(3t)$ reaches the level $e^{-2}$ at $t=2/3$. The scale divides the original level time by $3$.

The comparison rule is the same one used before: a shift moves the landmark by addition, while a scale moves it by division. For an exponential signal, the landmark is a level rather than a geometric feature.
