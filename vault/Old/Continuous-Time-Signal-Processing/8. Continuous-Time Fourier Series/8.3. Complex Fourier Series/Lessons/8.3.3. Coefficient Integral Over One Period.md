## Introduction to Coefficient Integral Over One Period

A periodic signal in complex-exponential Fourier series form is written with one coefficient per harmonic. Here $x(t)$ is the signal, $C_k$ is the coefficient, $k$ is the integer harmonic index, and $\omega_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency.

$$
x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} C_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
$$

It is often helpful to read the same series as a DC term plus positive and negative harmonic pairs. The $k=0$ term is the DC component, and the nonzero indices appear symmetrically as matched positive and negative harmonics.

$$
x(t)=C_0+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(C_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}+C_{-k} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\big)
$$

To isolate one coefficient $C_k$, multiply the signal by $e^{-jk\omega_0 t}$ and integrate over any interval of length $T_0$. The matching harmonic survives, while the other harmonics cancel over a full period.

$$
C_k=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

That cancellation is orthogonality over one period: the matching exponential integrates to $1$, and every mismatched harmonic integrates to $0$. Because the signal repeats every $T_0$, the start time can be any $t_0$ as long as the interval length is $T_0$.

$$
\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}e^{j(n-k)\omega_0 t}\,dt=\begin{cases}1,&n=k\\0,&n\ne k\end{cases}
$$

The working recipe is simple: choose $k$, multiply by $e^{-jk\omega_0 t}$, integrate across any one period, and divide by $T_0$. That gives the coefficient $C_k$ for the $k$th harmonic directly.

## Identifying $T_0$, $\omega$_0, and k in the Coefficient Formula

**Example:** A periodic signal has $T_0=2$. Identify the coefficient formula for $C_3$ over the one-period interval $[-1,1]$ and state the corresponding $\omega_0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the one-period interval $[-1,1]$. Its length is $2$, so $T_0=2$, and that means $\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T_0}=\pi$.

$$
T_0=2,\quad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T_0}=\pi
$$

The coefficient formula stays in normalized one-period form. For $C_3$, set $k=3$ and keep the matching exponential $e^{-j3\omega_0 t}$.

$$
C_3=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{-1}^{1} x(t)e^{-j3\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Substituting $T_0=2$ and $\omega_0=\pi$ gives the final setup on the stated interval. The integral is ready to use, but it is not evaluated yet.

$$
C_3=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} x(t)e^{-j3\pi t}\,dt
$$

This is the correct coefficient formula setup: identify the period, compute the fundamental frequency, choose the harmonic index, and write the normalized integral over one full period.

## Computing $C_k$ from a Smooth Periodic Formula

**Example:** Compute $C_2$ for $x(t)=2+3\cos(\pi t)-4\sin(2\pi t)$ over one period.

**Explanation**

The cue is a smooth periodic sum with one DC term and two harmonics. For $C_2$, use the one-period coefficient formula with $T_0=2$ and the matching exponential $e^{-j2\pi t}$.

$$
C_2=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 \bigl(2+3\cos(\pi t)-4\sin(2\pi t)\bigr)e^{-j2\pi t}\,dt
$$

Rewrite the trigonometric terms in complex-exponential form so the target harmonic is visible. The only part that can match $k=2$ is the $e^{j2\pi t}$ contribution from the sine term.

$$
x(t)=2+\frac{3}{2}\bigl(e^{j\pi t}+e^{-j\pi t}\bigr)+2j\,e^{j2\pi t}-2j\,e^{-j2\pi t}
$$

After multiplying by $e^{-j2\pi t}$, every nonmatching exponential averages to zero over $[0,2]$. Only the constant term created by $2j\,e^{j2\pi t}$ survives the integral.

$$
C_2=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 \bigl(2j-2j\,e^{-j4\pi t}\bigr)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}(4j)=2j
$$

So the coefficient is $C_2=2j$. The pattern is: write the one-period integral, expose the matching exponential, and drop the orthogonal terms.

## Computing $C_k$ Over a Shifted One-Period Interval

**Example:** Compute $C_2$ for the same signal as in the previous example, but integrate over $[1,3]$ instead of $[0,2]$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same signal and the same harmonic, with only the one-period window shifted. The coefficient integrand is still one-period periodic, so moving the start of the interval does not change the average.

$$
f(t)=\bigl(2+3\cos(\pi t)-4\sin(2\pi t)\bigr)e^{-j2\pi t},\quad f(t+2)=f(t)
$$

Because $f(t)$ has period $2$, the integral over any interval of length $2$ is the same. The shifted window $[1,3]$ therefore gives the same coefficient as the original one-period window $[0,2]$.

$$
C_2=\frac{1}{2}\int_1^3 f(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 f(t)\,dt
$$

That is exactly the coefficient computed in the previous example, so the shifted interval does not change the result.

$$
C_2=2j
$$

So the one-period coefficient is invariant under the interval shift: any length-$2$ integration window gives the same $C_2$.

## Splitting the Coefficient Integral Across Pieces of One Period

**Example:** Compute $C_1$ for the piecewise periodic signal $x(t)=1$ on $0\le t<1$ and $x(t)=2$ on $1\le t<2$, with $T_0=2$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a one-period signal whose formula changes at $t=1$. Split the coefficient integral at that boundary before simplifying anything.

$$
C_1=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_0^1 1\cdot e^{-j\pi t}\,dt+\int_1^2 2\cdot e^{-j\pi t}\,dt\right)
$$

Evaluate each piece separately. The two pieces share the same exponential factor, so the only difference is the constant multiplier and the endpoint values.

$$
C_1=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left[\frac{e^{-j\pi t}}{-j\pi}\right]_0^1+\left[\frac{2e^{-j\pi t}}{-j\pi}\right]_1^2\right)
$$

Now use $e^{-j\pi}=-1$ and $e^{-j2\pi}=1$ to simplify the endpoints. The two piecewise contributions combine into one coefficient.

$$
C_1=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-2}{-j\pi}+\frac{4}{-j\pi}\right)=-\frac{1}{j\pi}
$$

So the coefficient is $C_1=-\frac{1}{j\pi}$. The procedure is the same for any piecewise signal: split at every boundary, integrate each piece, and then add the results.

## Checking That the Integral Isolates the kth Harmonic

**Example:** Compute $C_2$ for $x(t)=1+2e^{j2\pi t}+7e^{j5\pi t}$ with $T_0=2$.

**Explanation**

The cue is already written as a sum of complex exponentials. For $C_2$, multiply by the matching kernel $e^{-j2\pi t}$ and look for the term that becomes constant.

$$
C_2=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 \bigl(1+2e^{j2\pi t}+7e^{j5\pi t}\bigr)e^{-j2\pi t}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 \bigl(e^{-j2\pi t}+2+7e^{j3\pi t}\bigr)\,dt
$$

The DC term and the $k=5$ term are nonmatching exponentials. Over one full period, each integrates to zero, so only the constant $2$ survives.

$$
\int_0^2 e^{-j2\pi t}\,dt=0,\quad \int_0^2 e^{j3\pi t}\,dt=0
$$

That leaves only the matching harmonic. The coefficient integral is just the average of the constant term over the period.

$$
C_2=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 2\,dt=2
$$

So the integral isolates exactly the $k=2$ harmonic and returns its coefficient, $2$.
