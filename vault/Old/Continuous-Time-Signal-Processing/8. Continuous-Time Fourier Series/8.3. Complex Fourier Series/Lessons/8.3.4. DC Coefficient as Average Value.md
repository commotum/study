## Introduction to DC Coefficient as Average Value

The DC coefficient $C_0$ is the $k=0$ Fourier coefficient in the complex Fourier series. It is the constant term, so it represents the average level that remains after the oscillatory harmonics are removed.

$$
C_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0} x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

To isolate $C_0$, set $k=0$ in that coefficient formula. That is the only change, and it tells you to look at the zero-frequency term rather than a nonzero harmonic.

$$
e^{-j0\omega_0 t}=1
$$

Because the exponential factor becomes $1$, the coefficient integral simplifies to the average of $x(t)$ over one full period. The oscillatory weighting disappears, so only the period-average remains.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0} x(t)\,dt
$$

Any interval of length $T_0$ gives the same value, so the starting point $t_0$ can shift without changing $C_0$. The only requirement is that the integration interval span one complete period.

So $C_0$ is the DC level, or average value, of the periodic signal. The next examples will compute that average directly from the integral.

## Computing $C_0$ by Setting $k=0 in the Coefficient Integral$

**Example:** Compute $C_0$ for the periodic signal that equals $5$ on $0 \le t < T_0/4$ and $1$ on $T_0/4 \le t < T_0$ by setting $k=0$ in the coefficient integral.

**Explanation**

The cue is the $k=0$ coefficient, so start from the complex Fourier coefficient formula and remove the exponential weighting. For this example, use one full period on $0 \le t < T_0$ and compute the average value over that interval.

$$
C_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{0}^{T_0} x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Set $k=0$ first, then split the period integral across the two constant pieces. Each piece contributes area, because $x(t)$ is constant on each subinterval.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(\int_{0}^{T_0/4} 5\,dt + \int_{T_0/4}^{T_0} 1\,dt\right)
$$

Now evaluate the two areas: the first contributes $5 \cdot \frac{T_0}{4}$ and the second contributes $1 \cdot \frac{3T_0}{4}$. Add them, then divide by $T_0$ to get the average level.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(5\frac{T_0}{4} + 1\cdot \frac{3T_0}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{T_0}\cdot 2T_0 = 2
$$

So the DC coefficient is $C_0=2$. This is the constant average value of the signal over one period.

## Interpreting $C_0$ as the Average Value Over One Period

**Example:** A periodic signal equals $4$ on $0 \le t < T_0/3$, $1$ on $T_0/3 \le t < 2T_0/3$, and $0$ on $2T_0/3 \le t < T_0$. Compute $C_0$ and state the signal's average value.

**Explanation**

The average value is the normalized one-period area, so compute the area of each constant piece and divide by $T_0$. The zero-height section still matters because it occupies part of the period, even though it contributes no area.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(\int_{0}^{T_0/3} 4\,dt + \int_{T_0/3}^{2T_0/3} 1\,dt + \int_{2T_0/3}^{T_0} 0\,dt\right)
$$

Now read each piece as a rectangle area: the first contributes $4\cdot \frac{T_0}{3}$, the second contributes $1\cdot \frac{T_0}{3}$, and the third contributes $0$. Add the areas before normalizing.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(4\frac{T_0}{3} + 1\frac{T_0}{3} + 0\right) = \frac{1}{T_0}\cdot \frac{5T_0}{3} = \frac{5}{3}
$$

So the signal's average value is $\frac{5}{3}$. Because $C_0$ is the one-period average, the DC coefficient is also $C_0=\frac{5}{3}$.

## Choosing a Convenient Period for Computing $C_0$

**Example:** A periodic signal with period $T_0=4$ is defined by $x(t)=3$ on $2 \le t < 4$ and $x(t)=1$ on $4 \le t < 6$, repeated periodically. Compute $C_0$ by integrating over the convenient one-period interval $[2,6]$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $C_0$ is a one-period average, so choose any interval of length $T_0=4$ that makes the pieces line up. Here $[2,6]$ is convenient because it matches the given breakpoints exactly.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{4}\int_{2}^{6} x(t)\,dt
$$

On $[2,6]$, the signal is $3$ for length $2$ and $1$ for length $2$, so the integral is just the sum of two rectangle areas. No change of variables is needed because the chosen interval already matches the piecewise definition.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{2}^{4} 3\,dt + \int_{4}^{6} 1\,dt\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(3\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 2\right)
$$

Now normalize the total area by the period length. The result is the same average value you would get from any other full period, because periodicity makes all one-period intervals equivalent.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{4}(8) = 2
$$

So the convenient shifted interval gives $C_0=2$, and that is the signal's average value over one period.

## Recognizing Zero and Nonzero DC Coefficients from Signal Balance

**Example:** A periodic signal equals $5$ on $0 \le t < T_0/4$ and $-1$ on $T_0/4 \le t < T_0$. Determine whether $C_0$ is zero or nonzero and compute its value from the balance of positive and negative area.

**Explanation**

The cue is the signed area balance, so compare the positive and negative contributions over one period before looking at the height alone. If the total signed area does not cancel, then $C_0$ is nonzero.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(\int_{0}^{T_0/4} 5\,dt + \int_{T_0/4}^{T_0} (-1)\,dt\right)
$$

The positive piece contributes $5\cdot \frac{T_0}{4}$, while the negative piece contributes $-1\cdot \frac{3T_0}{4}$. The positive area is larger, so the cancellation is incomplete and the average stays above zero.

$$
C_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}\left(5\frac{T_0}{4} - 1\cdot \frac{3T_0}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{T_0}\cdot \frac{T_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

So $C_0$ is nonzero, and in fact $C_0=\frac{1}{2}$. The signal's average value is positive because its positive area outweighs its negative area over one period.
