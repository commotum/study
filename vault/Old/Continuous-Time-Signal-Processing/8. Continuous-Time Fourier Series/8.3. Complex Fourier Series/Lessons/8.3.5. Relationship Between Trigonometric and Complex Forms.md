## Introduction to Relationship Between Trigonometric and Complex Forms

The trigonometric and complex Fourier series are two notations for the same periodic signal. Start with the simplest case: one DC term and one harmonic pair, so the coefficient mapping is easy to see before the full sum appears.

$$
x(t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big[a_k\cos(k\omega_0 t)+b_k\sin(k\omega_0 t)\big]
$$

$$
x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} C_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
$$

The DC term matches directly: $a_0/2$ in trigonometric form is $C_0$ in complex form. That is the base case for the whole conversion rule.

$$
C_0=\frac{a_0}{2}
$$

For each harmonic index $k \ge 1$, use Euler's identities to turn the cosine-sine pair into the positive and negative exponential pair. The forward map sends one trigonometric pair to two complex coefficients.

$$
C_k=\frac{1}{2}(a_k-jb_k),\quad C_{-k}=\frac{1}{2}(a_k+jb_k)
$$

Going back the other way is just as direct. Add the pair to recover $a_k$, and subtract to recover $b_k$.

$$
a_k=C_k+C_{-k},\quad b_k=j(C_k-C_{-k})
$$

So the workflow is: match $a_0/2$ with $C_0$, then match each $a_k\cos(k\omega_0 t)+b_k\sin(k\omega_0 t)$ pair with $C_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}+C_{-k}e^{-jk\omega_0 t}$. The next examples will apply the same mapping one harmonic at a time.

## Mapping the DC Term Between Fourier Series Forms

**Example:** If a trigonometric Fourier series has $a_0=18$, what is the corresponding complex DC coefficient $C_0$?

**Explanation**

This is the simplest mapping: only the DC term changes, and no harmonic pair is involved. The cue is the constant term, which is $a_0/2$ in trigonometric form and $C_0$ in complex form.

$$
C_0=\frac{a_0}{2}
$$

Substitute the given value $a_0=18$ into the DC relationship. That gives the complex coefficient directly.

$$
C_0=\frac{18}{2}=9
$$

So the corresponding complex DC coefficient is $C_0=9$. The reverse check is immediate: doubling $C_0$ gives back $a_0=18$, so the signal itself has not changed.

## Converting a Cosine Harmonic to Complex Coefficients

**Example:** Convert the cosine harmonic $x(t)=8\cos(3\omega_0 t)$ into its complex coefficient pair $(C_3,C_{-3})$.

**Explanation**

A cosine-only harmonic is the cue. With no sine term, the complex pair must be real and symmetric, so the positive- and negative-index coefficients will match.

$$
8\cos(3\omega_0 t)=4e^{j3\omega_0 t}+4e^{-j3\omega_0 t}
$$

This rewrite isolates the two exponentials at $+3$ and $-3$. Reading off the coefficients gives the same value on both sides of the pair.

$$
C_3=4,\quad C_{-3}=4
$$

So the complex form of the cosine harmonic is $C_3e^{j3\omega_0 t}+C_{-3}e^{-j3\omega_0 t}=4e^{j3\omega_0 t}+4e^{-j3\omega_0 t}$. The signal is unchanged; only the representation has changed.

## Converting a Sine Harmonic to Complex Coefficients

**Example:** Convert the sine harmonic $x(t)=6\sin(2\omega_0 t)$ into its complex coefficient pair $(C_2,C_{-2})$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a pure sine harmonic, so the complex pair must be purely imaginary with opposite signs. Rewrite the sine term in exponential form and match each exponential to its coefficient.

$$
6\sin(2\omega_0 t)=6\left(\frac{e^{j2\omega_0 t}-e^{-j2\omega_0 t}}{2j}\right)=-3j\,e^{j2\omega_0 t}+3j\,e^{-j2\omega_0 t}
$$

Now read off the coefficients term by term: the $e^{j2\omega_0 t}$ term gives $C_2=-3j$, and the $e^{-j2\omega_0 t}$ term gives $C_{-2}=3j$. The two coefficients are conjugates with opposite signs, which is the sine pattern.

$$
C_2=-3j,\quad C_{-2}=3j
$$

So the sine harmonic is represented by the same signal in complex form, with its content carried by a purely imaginary positive-negative coefficient pair.

## Converting a Sine-Cosine Harmonic Pair to Complex Coefficients

**Example:** Convert $x(t)=4\cos(4\omega_0 t)+10\sin(4\omega_0 t)$ into its complex coefficient pair $(C_4,C_{-4})$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a single harmonic index with both cosine and sine pieces. Use the cosine rule for the real part and the sine rule for the imaginary part, then combine them into one pair of complex coefficients.

$$
4\cos(4\omega_0 t)+10\sin(4\omega_0 t)=2e^{j4\omega_0 t}+2e^{-j4\omega_0 t}-5j\,e^{j4\omega_0 t}+5j\,e^{-j4\omega_0 t}=(2-5j)e^{j4\omega_0 t}+(2+5j)e^{-j4\omega_0 t}
$$

Now read off the two coefficients from the combined exponential form. The positive-index coefficient is $C_4=2-5j$, and the negative-index coefficient is $C_{-4}=2+5j$.

$$
C_4=2-5j,\quad C_{-4}=2+5j
$$

This mixed case shows the full pattern: the cosine part contributes the shared real value, and the sine part contributes opposite imaginary parts, producing a conjugate pair.

## Converting Complex Coefficient Pairs Back to Sine and Cosine Coefficients

**Example:** Given $C_5=1-3j$ and $C_{-5}=1+3j$, recover the trigonometric coefficients $a_5$ and $b_5$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a conjugate pair of complex coefficients. Use the reverse mapping formulas to recover the trigonometric coefficients from the real and imaginary parts.

$$
a_5=C_5+C_{-5}=(1-3j)+(1+3j)=2,\qquad b_5=j(C_5-C_{-5})=j\big[(1-3j)-(1+3j)\big]=j(-6j)=6
$$

So the trigonometric coefficients are $a_5=2$ and $b_5=6$. The same harmonic can therefore be written in trigonometric form as a cosine term plus a sine term.

$$
a_5=2,\quad b_5=6\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 2\cos(5\omega_0 t)+6\sin(5\omega_0 t)
$$

This closes the mapping in the reverse direction: a conjugate complex pair gives back the matching sine-cosine coefficients for the same harmonic index.
