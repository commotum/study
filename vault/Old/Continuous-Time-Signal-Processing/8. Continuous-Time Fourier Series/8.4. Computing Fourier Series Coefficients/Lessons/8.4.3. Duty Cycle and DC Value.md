## Introduction to Duty Cycle and DC Value

For a rectangular periodic signal, the duty cycle $D$ is the fraction of each period that the signal spends at the high level. If the signal is high for a time $\tau$ inside a period $T$, then $D = \frac{\tau}{T}$.

$$
D = \frac{\tau}{T}
$$

The DC Fourier-series coefficient is the average value over one period. For a periodic signal $x(t)$, compute that average by integrating over one full period and dividing by $T$.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\,dt
$$

In the simplest pulse train, $x(t)=A$ on the on-time of length $\tau$ and $x(t)=0$ elsewhere. Only the high interval contributes to the integral, so the average becomes the high level times the duty cycle.

$$
\bar{x} = A D
$$

So the rule to carry forward is simple: read the duty cycle from the on-time, then take the one-period average to get the DC value. Later rectangular signals will use the same average-value idea, even when the signal levels change.

<a id="computing-dc-value-from-a-given-duty-cycle"></a>
## Computing DC Value from a Given Duty Cycle

**Example:** A periodic rectangular signal is $12$ for $\frac{1}{3}$ of each period and $0$ for the rest. What is its DC value?

**Explanation**

The cue is that the duty cycle is already given: $D=\frac{1}{3}$. The signal is high for that fraction of each period and $0$ for the rest, so the DC value is the one-period average.

$$
\bar{x} = 12\cdot\frac{1}{3} + 0\cdot\frac{2}{3}
$$

The high level contributes $12$ for the first third of the period, and the low level contributes $0$ for the remaining two thirds. So the average becomes the high level times the duty cycle.

$$
\bar{x} = 4
$$

So the DC value is $4$. In Fourier-series language, that average is the DC coefficient for this rectangular signal.

## Finding Duty Cycle from Pulse Width and Period

**Example:** A rectangular signal is high for $2$ ms of every $5$ ms period and is $0$ otherwise. If the high level is $10$, what is its DC value?

**Explanation**

The cue is that the pulse width and period are already given: the signal is high for $2$ ms out of every $5$ ms period. That means the first step is to convert the geometry into duty cycle $D = \tau/T$.

$$
D = \frac{\tau}{T} = \frac{2}{5}
$$

Now use the average-value formula for the rectangular signal. With high level $10$ and low level $0$, the DC value is the high level weighted by the high-time fraction and the low level weighted by the remaining fraction.

$$
\bar{x} = 10\cdot\frac{2}{5} + 0\cdot\frac{3}{5}
$$

Simplifying gives $\bar{x}=4$. So the DC value is $4$, and the key move is converting pulse width and period into duty cycle before averaging.

<a id="computing-dc-value-for-a-two-level-rectangular-signal"></a>
## Computing DC Value for a Two-Level Rectangular Signal

**Example:** A periodic rectangular signal is $6$ for $\frac{1}{4}$ of each period and $-1$ for the rest. What is its DC value?

**Explanation**

The cue is a two-level rectangular signal. The high level is $6$ for a fraction $\frac{1}{4}$ of the period, so the low level $-1$ fills the remaining $\frac{3}{4}$.

$$
\bar{x} = 6 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + (-1) \cdot \frac{3}{4}
$$

Now simplify each contribution. The high level contributes $\frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$, and the low level contributes $-\frac{3}{4}$.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{3}{2} - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{4}
$$

So the DC value is $\frac{3}{4}$. The key move is the weighted average of the two levels, with each level multiplied by the fraction of time it appears.

<a id="recognizing-zero-dc-value-from-balanced-signal-levels"></a>
## Recognizing Zero DC Value from Balanced Signal Levels

**Example:** A periodic rectangular signal is $4$ for $\frac{1}{3}$ of each period and $-2$ for the rest. What is its DC value?

**Explanation**

The cue is that the signal has two levels and the weighted contributions are meant to cancel. The high level is $4$ for $\frac{1}{3}$ of the period, so the low level $-2$ fills the remaining $\frac{2}{3}$.

$$
\bar{x} = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + (-2) \cdot \frac{2}{3}
$$

Now compare the two weighted terms. The positive contribution is $\frac{4}{3}$, and the negative contribution is $-\frac{4}{3}$, so they have equal magnitude and opposite sign.

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{4}{3} - \frac{4}{3} = 0
$$

So the DC value is $0$. The key recognition is that balanced weighted levels cancel exactly, so the average over one period is zero.
