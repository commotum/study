## Introduction to Fourier Coefficients of a Periodic Exponential

A periodic complex exponential such as $x(t)=e^{jm\omega_0 t}$ on $0 \le t < T$ is already a single Fourier harmonic. In this section, the goal is to see which coefficient survives when we apply the coefficient formula over one period.

$$
c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Use the standard period relation $\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}$ so the coefficient is measured over exactly one cycle. That keeps the setup tied to one full period and makes the matching-harmonic test meaningful.

$$
\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

Now substitute $x(t)=e^{jm\omega_0 t}$ into the integral. The two exponentials combine, and the exponent becomes the difference $m-k$.

$$
x(t)=e^{jm\omega_0 t}
$$

$$
c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{j(m-k)\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

That difference tells you the two outcomes to watch for later: if $m-k=0$, the matching harmonic remains; if $m-k\ne0$, the exponential averages away over the full period. The next step will use that split to evaluate specific coefficients.

## Setting Up the One-Period Coefficient Integral

**Example:** For the periodic exponential $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$ over one period $0 \le t < T$, set up the complex Fourier-series coefficient integral for $c_2$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a periodic complex exponential on one full period, so the right starting point is the standard one-period Fourier coefficient formula. Keep the normalization factor and the full-period limits exactly as written, with $\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}$.

$$
c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Now specialize to the given signal $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$ and the target coefficient $c_2$. Substitute the signal and the harmonic kernel $e^{-j2\omega_0 t}$, but do not simplify the exponent yet.

$$
c_2=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{j3\omega_0 t}e^{-j2\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

This is the complete setup: the coefficient is written over one full period, the $1/T$ factor is present, and the harmonic kernel is left intact for the next step. The only job here is to set up the integral correctly.

## Combining Exponents in the Integrand

**Example:** For the same periodic exponential $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$, combine the signal exponential and the harmonic kernel in the coefficient integral for $c_2$ and rewrite the integrand as one exponential.

**Explanation**

Start from the setup in Section 2. The cue is the product inside $c_2$: the periodic exponential $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$ times the harmonic kernel $e^{-j2\omega_0 t}$.

$$
c_2=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{j3\omega_0 t}e^{-j2\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Multiply the exponentials by adding exponents. Here the signal index is $m=3$ and the harmonic index is $k=2$, so the exponent becomes $j(m-k)\omega_0 t=j(3-2)\omega_0 t$.

$$
e^{j3\omega_0 t}e^{-j2\omega_0 t}=e^{j(3-2)\omega_0 t}=e^{j\omega_0 t}
$$

The integrand is now a single complex exponential with the mismatch made explicit. That is the form the next step can integrate directly.

## Evaluating Coefficients When the Combined Exponent Is Nonzero

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$, evaluate $c_2$ by integrating the nonzero combined exponent over one period and showing that the result cancels to zero.

**Explanation**

Start from the simplified integrand in Section 3. The cue is the single exponential $e^{j(3-2)\omega_0 t}=e^{j\omega_0 t}$ inside the one-period coefficient integral.

$$
c_2=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{j\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Integrate the exponential directly. An antiderivative is $\frac{1}{j\omega_0}e^{j\omega_0 t}$, so the coefficient is the endpoint difference divided by $T$.

$$
c_2=\frac{1}{Tj\omega_0}\Bigl[e^{j\omega_0 t}\Bigr]_0^T
$$

Now use $\omega_0T=2\pi$. The upper limit gives $e^{j\omega_0T}=e^{j2\pi}=1$, so the endpoint terms cancel and the nonmatching coefficient is zero.

$$
c_2=\frac{1}{Tj\omega_0}(e^{j2\pi}-1)=0
$$

The result is the expected full-period cancellation: a nonzero combined exponent still averages to zero over one period when the harmonic does not match the signal index.

## Evaluating the Coefficient When the Combined Exponent Is Zero

**Example:** For $x(t)=e^{j3\omega_0 t}$, evaluate $c_3$ when the combined exponent is zero.

**Explanation**

Set $k=3$ so the combined exponent is $j(3-3)\omega_0 t=0$. The cue is the constant integrand $1$ inside the one-period coefficient integral.

$$
c_3=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T 1\,dt
$$

Because the integrand is constant, the integral over one period is $T$, and the outer factor $1/T$ cancels it.

$$
c_3=\frac{1}{T}[t]_0^T=1
$$

The matching harmonic survives with coefficient $1$. That is the zero-exponent case that contrasts with the previous nonmatching branch.
