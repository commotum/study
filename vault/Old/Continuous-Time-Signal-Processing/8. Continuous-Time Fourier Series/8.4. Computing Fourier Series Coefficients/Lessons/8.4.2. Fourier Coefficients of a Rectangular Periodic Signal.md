## Introduction to Fourier Coefficients of a Rectangular Periodic Signal

For a rectangular periodic signal with amplitude $A$, pulse width $\tau$, and period $T$, the complex Fourier coefficient formula uses the fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0=2\pi/T$. The base case is a single pulse per period: the signal equals $A$ on the pulse-support interval and is $0$ everywhere else in the period.

$$
c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Because the signal is zero outside the pulse, only the pulse-support interval can contribute to the integral. That means we can replace the full-period description with the piecewise rectangular form and focus only on the interval $0\le t<\tau$.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}A,&0\le t<\tau\\0,&\tau\le t<T\end{cases}
$$

With that description in place, the one-period coefficient integral collapses to the pulse-support interval, and the constant amplitude factors out immediately. This is the step that turns a full-period integral into a short, reusable expression in $A$, $\tau$, $T$, and $k$.

$$
c_k=\frac{A}{T}\int_0^\tau e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

From here, the nonzero-harmonic case comes from evaluating the exponential antiderivative on the support interval. The special case $k=0$ is the average value over one period, so it stays separate from the $k\ne0$ formula.

$$
c_0=\frac{A\tau}{T},\qquad c_k=\frac{A}{-jk\omega_0 T}\left(e^{-jk\omega_0\tau}-1\right),\ k\ne 0
$$

The reusable pattern is simple: identify the pulse support, restrict the coefficient integral to that interval, factor out the constant amplitude, and then use the same exponential integral for every nonzero harmonic. Once the support interval is recognized, the coefficient formula becomes a short expression in $A$, $\tau$, $T$, and $k$.

## Reading Amplitude, Pulse Width, and Period from a Rectangular Signal

**Example:** A rectangular periodic signal is shown with height $3$, pulse width $2$, and period $8$. Read off $A$, $\tau$, and $T$ from the plot.

**Explanation**

When a signal is a repeated rectangular pulse, read the amplitude from the pulse height, the pulse width from the on-interval, and the period from the spacing between identical pulses. This plot gives one clean pulse per period, so the three numbers can be read directly from the picture before any coefficient computation starts.

![](<../Source/8.4.2. Fourier Coefficients of a Rectangular Periodic Signal/Images/l002-s002-te-section-002-plot-1.png>)

The pulse reaches a height of $3$, so the amplitude is $A=3$. The pulse runs from $t=0$ to $t=2$, so the pulse width is $\tau=2$.

The next identical pulse begins $8$ time units later, so the period is $T=8$. The rectangular signal is now fully specified by the parameter triple $(A,\tau,T)=(3,2,8)$.

This is the only information needed from the plot at this step: identify the height, the on-interval, and the repeat spacing. Once those three values are read correctly, the rectangular signal is fully specified for this example.

## Restricting the Coefficient Integral to the Pulse Support

**Example:** Given $x(t)=A$ on $0\le t<\tau$ and $0$ on $\tau\le t<T$, rewrite the coefficient integral so it runs only over the pulse support.

**Explanation**

Start with the one-period Fourier coefficient formula. The only new cue in this step is the pulse support: $x(t)$ is constant on $0\le t<\tau$ and zero everywhere else in the period.

$$
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Substitute the piecewise definition of the rectangular signal and split the integral at the pulse boundary. The second interval carries no contribution because $x(t)=0$ there.

$$
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\left(\int_0^\tau A e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt + \int_\tau^T 0\cdot e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt\right)
$$

Drop the zero-valued interval and factor out the constant amplitude $A$. The full-period coefficient integral collapses to the pulse-support integral.

$$
c_k = \frac{A}{T}\int_0^\tau e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

This restricted form is the pattern to reuse: keep the coefficient kernel, keep the support interval, and ignore the rest of the period because it contributes nothing.

## Evaluating the Exponential Integral for Nonzero Harmonics

**Example:** For $x(t)=A$ on $0\le t<\tau$ and $0$ otherwise over period $T$, compute $c_1$ by evaluating the restricted exponential integral.

**Explanation**

Use the restricted-support form from the previous step, then set $k=1$ for the first nonzero harmonic. The only task now is to evaluate the exponential integral on $0\le t<\tau$.

$$
c_1 = \frac{A}{T}\int_0^\tau e^{-j\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

Antidifferentiate $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ as $\frac{e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{-j\omega_0}$ and evaluate it at the pulse endpoints. This keeps the amplitude factor outside the integral and uses the support interval as the only integration range.

$$
c_1 = \frac{A}{T}\left[\frac{e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{-j\omega_0}\right]_0^\tau = \frac{A}{T}\frac{e^{-j\omega_0 \tau}-1}{-j\omega_0}
$$

Rewrite the difference in the standard nonzero-harmonic form. The result is a closed-form coefficient in terms of $A$, $\tau$, $T$, and $\omega_0$.

$$
c_1 = \frac{A}{T}\frac{1-e^{-j\omega_0 \tau}}{j\omega_0}
$$

This is the pattern to reuse for any nonzero harmonic: substitute the index, integrate the exponential on the support interval, and simplify the endpoint difference.

## Writing Rectangular-Signal Coefficients Using Amplitude, Width, Period, and Harmonic Index

**Example:** For a rectangular periodic signal that equals $A$ on $0\le t<\tau$ and $0$ otherwise over period $T$, write the formula for $c_k$ and separate the $k=0$ case from the $k\ne0$ case.

**Explanation**

Start from the restricted-support result and keep the harmonic index symbolic for now. The coefficient has one special case at $k=0$ and one closed form for $k\ne0$.

$$
c_k = \frac{A}{T}\left[\frac{e^{-jk\omega_0 t}}{-jk\omega_0}\right]_0^\tau = \frac{A}{-jk\omega_0 T}\left(e^{-jk\omega_0\tau}-1\right), \quad k\ne0
$$

For the DC term, set $k=0$ before evaluating the integral. That turns the coefficient into the pulse area divided by the period.

$$
c_0 = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^\tau A\,dt = \frac{A\tau}{T}
$$

Now replace $\omega_0$ with $2\pi/T$ and combine the two cases into one formula. The result is expressed entirely with $A$, $\tau$, $T$, and $k$.

$$
c_k = \begin{cases}
\frac{A\tau}{T}, & k=0,\\[4pt]
\frac{A}{-j2\pi k}\left(e^{-j2\pi k\tau/T}-1\right), & k\ne0.
\end{cases}
$$

That is the reusable rectangular-signal coefficient formula: one average-value coefficient at $k=0$ and one exponential closed form for every $k\ne0$.
