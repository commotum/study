## Introduction to Fourier Coefficients of a Sine Wave

Start from the complex Fourier series form $x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}$. For the unit-amplitude, zero-phase sine wave $x(t)=\sin(\omega_0 t)$ with period $T_0=2\pi/\omega_0$, the target is to show that only two harmonic slots matter.

$$
x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
$$

Use Euler's identity for sine to rewrite the signal in terms of the positive and negative fundamental-frequency complex exponentials. That rewrite is the key recognition step, because it shows the sine wave already has the exact basis functions used in the Fourier series.

$$
\sin(\omega_0 t)=\frac{e^{j\omega_0 t}-e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{2j}
$$

The term $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ matches the $k=1$ basis function, and the term $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ matches the $k=-1$ basis function. So the sine wave places all of its Fourier-series weight on those two harmonics and nowhere else.

$$
c_1=\frac{1}{2j},\qquad c_{-1}=-\frac{1}{2j},\qquad c_k=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm1
$$

Read that coefficient pattern as the whole story for the base case: a unit sine wave produces one positive-harmonic coefficient and one negative-harmonic coefficient with equal magnitude and opposite sign. Later sections will keep the same two-harmonic structure and only rescale or rotate those coefficients when amplitude or phase changes.

## Converting a Sine Wave into Complex Exponentials

**Example:** Rewrite $x(t)=\sin(\omega_0 t)$ as a sum of positive and negative fundamental-frequency complex exponentials.

**Explanation**

Use the unit-amplitude, zero-phase sine wave $x(t)=\sin(\omega_0 t)$ as the base case. The cue is the fundamental-frequency sine, and the job is to rewrite it in the same exponential basis used by the complex Fourier series.

$$
\sin(\omega_0 t)=\frac{e^{j\omega_0 t}-e^{-j\omega_0 t}}{2j}
$$

Here $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ is the $k=1$ harmonic basis function and $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ is the $k=-1$ harmonic basis function. No other harmonics appear in this rewrite, so the sine wave is already isolated to its two fundamental-frequency slots.

$$
x(t)=\frac{1}{2j}e^{j\omega_0 t}-\frac{1}{2j}e^{-j\omega_0 t}
$$

This is the exponential form we want: the sine wave is now written as one positive-frequency term and one negative-frequency term, with no extra harmonic content. The next step will read those two terms as the only nonzero Fourier coefficients.

## Identifying the $k = 1 and k = -1 Coefficients$

**Example:** Given $x(t)=\frac{1}{2j}e^{j\omega_0 t}-\frac{1}{2j}e^{-j\omega_0 t}$, identify the nonzero Fourier coefficients.

**Explanation**

Start from the rewritten exponential form. The positive-frequency term $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ belongs to the $k=1$ slot, and the negative-frequency term $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ belongs to the $k=-1$ slot, so each coefficient is read directly from the factor in front of its harmonic basis function.

$$
c_1=\frac{1}{2j},\qquad c_{-1}=-\frac{1}{2j}
$$

If useful, simplify those coefficients into rectangular form: $\frac{1}{2j}=-\frac{j}{2}$ and $-\frac{1}{2j}=\frac{j}{2}$. No other harmonics appear in the exponential form, so every coefficient except the $k=\pm1$ pair is zero.

$$
c_1=-\frac{j}{2},\qquad c_{-1}=\frac{j}{2},\qquad c_k=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm1
$$

This coefficient pair is the complete Fourier-series signature of the unit sine wave: one nonzero positive-harmonic coefficient and one nonzero negative-harmonic coefficient with matching magnitude and opposite sign pattern. The next steps will change amplitude or phase, but they will keep the same two harmonic slots.

## Including Amplitude in Sine-Wave Coefficients

**Example:** Find the complex Fourier coefficients of $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t)$.

**Explanation**

Start from the amplitude-scaled sine wave $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t)$. The cue is the same fundamental-frequency sine as before, but now the entire waveform is multiplied by $A$, so the coefficient pair should be scaled by that same factor.

$$
A\sin(\omega_0 t)=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j\omega_0 t}-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j\omega_0 t}
$$

Because amplitude multiplies the whole signal, it multiplies both harmonic slots equally. So the $k=1$ term becomes $c_1=\frac{A}{2j}$ and the $k=-1$ term becomes $c_{-1}=-\frac{A}{2j}$.

$$
c_1=\frac{A}{2j}=-\frac{Aj}{2},\qquad c_{-1}=-\frac{A}{2j}=\frac{Aj}{2},\qquad c_k=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm1
$$

That is the whole effect of amplitude in this case: the two nonzero coefficients are the unit-sine coefficients scaled by $A$, and no new harmonics appear. The next variation will keep the same two slots and change how the coefficients rotate with phase.

## Including Sinusoidal Phase in the Coefficients

**Example:** Find the complex Fourier coefficients of $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi)$.

**Explanation**

Start from the phase-shifted sine wave $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi)$. The cue is the same two-slot harmonic pattern as before; the only new ingredient is that the phase sits inside each exponential and rotates the coefficients.

$$
A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi)=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j(\omega_0 t+\phi)}-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j(\omega_0 t+\phi)}
$$

Separate the phase from the time factor so the Fourier-series slots are visible. This gives one $k=1$ term and one $k=-1$ term, each multiplied by its own complex phase factor.

$$
A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi)=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega_0 t}-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega_0 t}
$$

$$
c_1=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j\phi}=-\frac{Aj}{2}e^{j\phi},\qquad c_{-1}=-\frac{A}{2j}e^{-j\phi}=\frac{Aj}{2}e^{-j\phi},\qquad c_k=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm 1
$$

The phase changes the complex values of the two nonzero coefficients, not their locations. The harmonic pattern is still limited to $k=\pm 1$, so the only effect of $\phi$ is to rotate the coefficient pair in opposite directions.

## Reading Magnitude and Phase from Sine-Wave Coefficients

**Example:** From the coefficients of $x(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi)$, determine the magnitude and phase spectrum entries at $k=\pm1$ and state which harmonics are nonzero.

**Explanation**

Start from the nonzero coefficient pair for the phase-shifted sine wave. The cue is a two-line spectrum, so read magnitude from absolute value and phase from the coefficient angle.

$$
c_1=\frac{A}{2j}e^{j\phi},\qquad c_{-1}=\frac{Aj}{2}e^{-j\phi},\qquad c_k=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm1
$$

Take magnitudes first. Both nonzero harmonics have the same magnitude because the phase factors have unit length, so the magnitude spectrum has equal lines at $k=1$ and $k=-1$.

$$
|c_1|=\frac{A}{2},\qquad |c_{-1}|=\frac{A}{2},\qquad |c_k|=0\text{ for }k\neq \pm1
$$

Now read the phases. The $k=1$ line has angle $\phi-\frac{\pi}{2}$, while the $k=-1$ line has angle $\frac{\pi}{2}-\phi$ modulo $2\pi$. The phase shift rotates the two lines in opposite directions, but it does not create any new harmonics.

$$
\arg(c_1)=\phi-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad \arg(c_{-1})=\frac{\pi}{2}-\phi\pmod{2\pi}
$$

So the spectrum is sparse: two equal magnitude lines at $k=\pm1$, with opposite phase rotations set by $\phi$, and every other coefficient is zero.
