## Introduction to Fourier Transform of a Constant Signal

A constant signal is a flat waveform with no time variation. In the Fourier transform, that kind of signal is read as pure DC content concentrated at $\omega=0$.

$$
x(t)=1
$$

Use the unit-constant case first: because the signal never changes with time, all of its spectral weight lands at zero frequency. So the Fourier transform of $x(t)=1$ is the zero-frequency impulse pair.

$$
X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)
$$

Once the unit case is clear, any constant amplitude $A$ just scales the spectrum by $A$. The impulse stays at $\omega=0$; only its weight changes.

$$
x(t)=A
$$

$$
X(j\omega)=2\pi A\delta(\omega)
$$

Read the pair in reverse as well: a zero-frequency impulse with weight $2\pi A$ means the time-domain signal is the constant $A$.

## Converting the Unit Constant Signal to a Zero-Frequency Impulse

**Example:** Given $x(t)=1$, choose the correct Fourier transform pair and identify the impulse at $\omega=0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a unit constant signal: $x(t)=1$ is flat for all $t$, so it contains only DC content. That means the spectrum must collapse to the zero-frequency line.

$$
x(t)=1
$$

Apply the constant-signal rule from the tutorial: all spectral weight sits at $\omega=0$, and the correct transform pair is a zero-frequency impulse with weight $2\pi$.

$$
X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)
$$

No other frequency content appears. The unit constant signal maps directly to $2\pi\delta(\omega)$ centered at $\omega=0$.

## Converting a Scaled Constant Signal to a Weighted Impulse

**Example:** Given $x(t)=A$, determine the Fourier transform and explain how the amplitude changes the impulse weight.

**Explanation**

The cue is still a constant signal: $x(t)=A$ is flat for all $t$, so it carries only DC content. The new wrinkle is just the constant level $A$ instead of $1$.

$$
x(t)=A
$$

Apply linearity to the unit-constant result from the previous step: multiplying the time-domain signal by $A$ multiplies the spectrum by $A$ while leaving the impulse at $\omega=0$.

$$
X(j\omega)=2\pi A\delta(\omega)
$$

So the weighted impulse pair is the same zero-frequency location as before, with the amplitude carried into the coefficient. The spectrum is still concentrated at $\omega=0$.

```quiz
- id: b

  correct: true
  feedback: |-
    A constant signal is still pure DC, so the spectrum stays at $\omega=0$. Multiply the unit-constant pair $X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)$ by the amplitude $4$.

    $$
    X(j\omega)=4\cdot 2\pi\delta(\omega)=8\pi\delta(\omega)
    $$

    So the impulse weight at $\omega=0$ is $8\pi$.

- id: c
  content: |-
    $X(j\omega)=4\delta(\omega)$

- id: d
  content: |-
    $X(j\omega)=8\pi\delta(t)$

- id: e
  content: |-
    $X(j\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)$
```

## Finding the Constant Amplitude from a Zero-Frequency Impulse Spectrum

**Example:** Given $X(j\omega)=2\pi A\delta(\omega)$, choose the matching constant signal $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a zero-frequency impulse spectrum: $X(j\omega)=2\pi A\delta(\omega)$ means all of the spectral weight is concentrated at $\omega=0$. That is the frequency-domain signature of a constant time-domain signal.

$$
X(j\omega)=2\pi A\delta(\omega)
$$

Read the coefficient of $\delta(\omega)$ as the constant amplitude. The weight is $2\pi A$, so the matching time-domain signal has amplitude $A$.

$$
x(t)=A
$$

So the inverse read is direct: a zero-frequency impulse with weight $2\pi A$ corresponds to the constant signal $x(t)=A$. No other frequency content is present.
