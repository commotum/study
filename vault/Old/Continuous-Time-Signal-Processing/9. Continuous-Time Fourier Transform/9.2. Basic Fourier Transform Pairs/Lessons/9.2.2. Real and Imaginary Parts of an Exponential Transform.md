## Introduction to Real and Imaginary Parts of an Exponential Transform

When you see $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}$ with $a>0$, the conjugate trick is the direct way to expose its real and imaginary parts. Multiplying by the complex conjugate keeps the transform unchanged and prepares the denominator to become real.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}
$$

Write the same transform as a product with $\frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega}$. That step does not change the value; it only sets up the numerator and denominator for simplification.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}\cdot\frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega}
$$

Now expand the denominator. The imaginary terms cancel, so the denominator becomes the real quantity $a^2+\omega^2$.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
$$

Once the denominator is real, the split is immediate: the real part is $\Re\{X(j\omega)\}=\frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2}$ and the imaginary part is $\Im\{X(j\omega)\}=-\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}$. That is the pattern you will reuse whenever this transform appears.

$$
\Re\{X(j\omega)\}=\frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2},\qquad \Im\{X(j\omega)\}=-\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
$$

## Multiplying the Exponential Transform by the Complex Conjugate

**Example:** Starting from $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}$, multiply by the complex conjugate of the denominator and write the unsimplified product form.

**Explanation**

Because the denominator is $a+j\omega$, the matching conjugate is $a-j\omega$. Multiply by $\frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega}$ so the value stays the same while the algebra is set up for the next step.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}\cdot\frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega}
$$

Now write the result in unsimplified product form: the numerator is $a-j\omega$, and the denominator is still the product $(a+j\omega)(a-j\omega)$. Leave it in this form so the next section can simplify the denominator.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{a-j\omega}{(a+j\omega)(a-j\omega)}
$$

## Simplifying the Denominator to a Real Quantity

**Example:** Simplify $(a+j\omega)(a-j\omega)$ into a real denominator and show the result inside the transformed fraction.

**Explanation**

The next move is to simplify the denominator product from the conjugate step. Expand it directly, then use the fact that $j^2=-1$ so the imaginary terms cancel.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a+j\omega)(a-j\omega) &= a^2 - aj\omega + aj\omega - j^2\omega^2 \\
&= a^2 + \omega^2
\end{aligned}
$$

That leaves a real denominator, $a^2+\omega^2$. The numerator stays $a-j\omega$, so the fraction is now ready to be split into real and imaginary parts.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
$$

<a id="separating-the-real-and-imaginary-parts"></a>
## Separating the Real and Imaginary Parts

**Example:** Take $\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}$ and identify its real part and imaginary part as separate terms.

**Explanation**

The denominator is already real, so the remaining job is to split the numerator. Keep the same denominator on both terms and write the fraction as a real term plus a pure imaginary term.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2} &= \frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2} - j\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now the term without $j$ is the real part, and the coefficient of $j$ is the imaginary part. The negative sign stays with the imaginary part.

$$
\Re\left\{\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}\right\}=\frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2},\qquad \Im\left\{\frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}\right\}=-\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
$$

<a id="decomposing-an-exponential-transform-with-a-specific-decay-constant"></a>
## Decomposing an Exponential Transform with a Specific Decay Constant

**Example:** Decompose $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$ into its real and imaginary parts.

**Explanation**

Set the decay constant to $2$. The algebraic pattern stays the same, but the coefficients now simplify to concrete numbers.

$$
\begin{aligned}
X(j\omega) &= \frac{1}{2+j\omega}\cdot\frac{2-j\omega}{2-j\omega} \\
&= \frac{2-j\omega}{(2+j\omega)(2-j\omega)} \\
&= \frac{2-j\omega}{4+\omega^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now the numerator splits over the real denominator. The real part is the constant term, and the imaginary part is the coefficient of $j$ with its negative sign preserved.

$$
\Re\{X(j\omega)\}=\frac{2}{4+\omega^2},\qquad \Im\{X(j\omega)\}=-\frac{\omega}{4+\omega^2}
$$

## Checking the Sign of the Imaginary Part

**Example:** Check the sign of the imaginary part when $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{a+j\omega}$ and confirm the correct final form.

**Explanation**

The only thing left to verify is the sign of the imaginary term. After multiplying by the conjugate, the numerator carries $-j\omega$, so the negative sign must stay attached to the imaginary part.

$$
\begin{aligned}
X(j\omega) &= \frac{1}{a+j\omega}\cdot\frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega} \\
&= \frac{a-j\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
\end{aligned}
$$

The real part is still $\frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2}$, but the imaginary part is negative because the numerator contains $-j\omega$.

$$
\Im\{X(j\omega)\}=-\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}
$$
