## Introduction to Fourier Transform of a Time-Domain Impulse

When the input is a time-domain impulse, the CTFT is handled directly by the sifting property. The integral does not stay as a frequency-dependent expression; it collapses to whatever the exponential evaluates to at the impulse location.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-t_0)f(t)\,dt=f(t_0)
$$

Start with the base case $x(t)=\delta(t)$. Substituting into the CTFT gives $X(\omega)=1$, so the spectrum is flat across all frequencies.

$$
\delta(t) \xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} 1
$$

The same rule shows what scale and shift do. Multiplying the impulse by $A$ multiplies the flat spectrum by $A$, and shifting the impulse to $t_0$ contributes only the phase factor $e^{-j\omega t_0}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}A\,\delta(t) &\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} A \\ \delta(t-t_0) &\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} e^{-j\omega t_0} \\ A\,\delta(t-t_0) &\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} A e^{-j\omega t_0}\end{aligned}
$$

For this lesson, keep the cue simple: a scaled or shifted impulse never changes the spectrum shape, only its height and phase. That is the transform pair to carry forward into the worked examples.

## Transforming the Unit Impulse at the Origin

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $x(t)=\delta(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the simplest impulse case: the impulse sits at the origin, so the CTFT integral collapses at $t=0$.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Apply the sifting property at the origin. The impulse picks out the value of the exponential at $t=0$, so the frequency variable contributes no extra dependence.

$$
X(\omega)=e^{-j\omega\cdot 0}=1
$$

So the unit impulse produces a flat spectrum. In compact transform-pair form, $\delta(t)\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}1$.

## Transforming a Scaled Time-Domain Impulse

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $x(t)=3\delta(t)$.

**Explanation**

This is the same origin-impulse case with one new wrinkle: the impulse is multiplied by $3$. The CTFT integral still collapses at $t=0$, so only the spectrum height changes.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}3\delta(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Pull the constant $3$ outside the integral and reuse the unit-impulse result from the previous example. The exponential is still evaluated at $t=0$, so there is no frequency dependence to track.

$$
X(\omega)=3\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=3
$$

So scaling the time-domain impulse scales the flat spectrum by the same factor. In transform-pair form, $3\delta(t)\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}3$.

## Transforming a Shifted Time-Domain Impulse

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $x(t)=\delta(t-t_0)$.

**Explanation**

This is the same impulse-transform pattern again, but now the impulse is shifted to $t_0$. The only change is where the sifting property samples the exponential.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-t_0)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Apply the sifting property at $t=t_0$. That replaces $t$ in the exponential with the shift location and leaves a pure phase factor.

$$
X(\omega)=e^{-j\omega t_0}
$$

So a shifted time-domain impulse transforms to unit magnitude with a frequency-dependent phase: $\delta(t-t_0)\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}e^{-j\omega t_0}$.

## Transforming a Scaled and Shifted Time-Domain Impulse

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $x(t)=A\delta(t-t_0)$.

**Explanation**

This combines the two earlier cases: the impulse is shifted to $t_0$ and scaled by $A$. The transform will therefore keep the phase factor from the shift and the amplitude factor from the scale.

$$
X(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A\delta(t-t_0)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Pull the constant $A$ outside the integral, then reuse the shifted-impulse result. The sifting property still evaluates the exponential at $t=t_0$.

$$
X(\omega)=A\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-t_0)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=Ae^{-j\omega t_0}
$$

So the combined effect is just amplitude times phase: $A\delta(t-t_0)\xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}Ae^{-j\omega t_0}$.
