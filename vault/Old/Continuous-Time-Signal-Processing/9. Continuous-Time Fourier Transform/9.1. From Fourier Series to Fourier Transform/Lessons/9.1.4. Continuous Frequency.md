## Introduction to Continuous Frequency

For a periodic signal with period $T$, the Fourier-series spectrum is not continuous. It sits on harmonic frequencies $\omega_n = n\omega_0$, where $n$ is an integer label and $\omega_0$ is the spacing between adjacent lines.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi n}{T}
$$

The spacing is the important part: adjacent harmonics differ by $\Delta\omega = \omega_{n+1} - \omega_n = \omega_0$, and $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ ties that spacing directly to the period.

$$
\Delta\omega = \omega_{n+1} - \omega_n = \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

As $T$ grows, $\omega_0$ shrinks, so the same harmonic labels are packed more tightly on the frequency axis. The spectrum still uses integers $n$, but the frequency samples sit closer together.

$$
\lim_{T\to\infty} \Delta\omega = 0
$$

That is the point of continuous frequency: when the grid becomes arbitrarily dense, the integer index stops being the natural coordinate, and the spectrum is better read with a continuous variable $\omega$.

## Identifying Discrete Harmonic Frequencies

**Example:** A periodic signal has period $T = 4$ s. Find $\omega_0$ and list the harmonic frequencies for $n = -2,-1,0,1,2$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the period $T$, which tells us to use the Fourier-series harmonic grid. The spectrum is not arbitrary; it sits at frequencies $\omega_n = n\omega_0$, with one line for each integer $n$.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi n}{T}
$$

For $T = 4$ s, the spacing is $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$ rad/s. That means each harmonic line is separated from the next one by $\frac{\pi}{2}$ rad/s.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$

Now multiply $\omega_0$ by the listed integer labels. The harmonic frequencies are $-\pi$, $-\frac{\pi}{2}$, $0$, $\frac{\pi}{2}$, and $\pi$ rad/s, written from negative to positive.

$$
\{-\pi,\,-\frac{\pi}{2},\,0,\,\frac{\pi}{2},\,\pi\}
$$

This is a discrete Fourier-series grid: only the harmonic frequencies at those integer labels belong to the spectrum, and nothing sits between the lines.

## Seeing Frequency Samples Become Denser

**Example:** Compare periodic signals with periods $T = 2$ s and $T = 8$ s. Compute $\omega_0$ for each and decide which spectrum has the denser harmonic grid over the same frequency interval.

**Explanation**

The cue is a comparison of two periods, so use the same harmonic spacing rule from the previous step. For each signal, compute $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ and compare the line spacing on the Fourier-series grid.

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi,\qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{8} = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

For $T = 2$ s, the harmonic lines are $\pi$ rad/s apart. For $T = 8$ s, they are only $\frac{\pi}{4}$ rad/s apart, so the larger-period signal has much tighter spacing.

$$
\Delta\omega_{T=2} = \pi,\qquad \Delta\omega_{T=8} = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

That spacing is the gap between adjacent harmonic lines. Over the same frequency interval, the grid with the smaller gap fits more samples, so the $T = 8$ spectrum is denser than the $T = 2$ spectrum.

<a id="replacing-harmonic-indices-with-a-frequency-variable"></a>
## Replacing Harmonic Indices with a Frequency Variable

**Example:** A Fourier-series spectrum is written with harmonics at $\omega_n = n\omega_0$. Describe how the notation changes as $T \to \infty$ and $\omega_0 \to 0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the harmonic grid $\omega_n = n\omega_0$. Here $n$ is only a discrete label, and $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ is the fixed spacing between adjacent lines.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0,\qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

As $T \to \infty$, the spacing collapses: $\Delta\omega = \omega_{n+1} - \omega_n = \omega_0 \to 0$. Once the gap vanishes, the integer index stops being the useful coordinate on the frequency axis.

$$
\Delta\omega = \omega_{n+1} - \omega_n = \omega_0 \to 0
$$

The relabeling is the main point: replace the harmonic index $n$ with the continuous frequency variable $\omega$. In the Fourier-transform setting, the spectrum is read on the $\omega$ axis, not as a list of integer harmonic numbers.

## Reading a Spectrum on a Continuous Frequency Axis

**Example:** A spectrum is described as $X(\omega)$ on a continuous frequency axis, and you are asked to read the value at $\omega = 1.3\pi$ rad/s. Explain how to interpret that request.

**Explanation**

The cue is the continuous axis in $X(\omega)$. Read $\omega$ as the coordinate itself, so $\omega = 1.3\pi$ rad/s names a location on the spectrum.

$$
\omega = 1.3\pi\ \text{rad/s}
$$

In a Fourier series, you would be restricted to harmonic locations $\omega_n = n\omega_0$. Here, the point is that $1.3\pi$ does not have to equal an integer multiple of a spacing to be meaningful.

$$
\omega_n = n\omega_0
$$

So the correct reading is: take the spectrum value at that frequency coordinate. That is the bridge to Fourier-transform notation, where $\omega$ is the axis label and not a harmonic index.
