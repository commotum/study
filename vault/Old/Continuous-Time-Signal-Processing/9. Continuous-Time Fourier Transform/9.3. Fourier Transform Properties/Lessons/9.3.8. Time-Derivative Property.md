## Introduction to Time-Derivative Property

When you already know $X(j\omega)$ for $x(t)$, the time-derivative property lets you get the spectrum of $x'(t)$ without redoing the transform integral. The point of the step is simple: differentiation in time becomes multiplication by $j\omega$ in the frequency domain.

Start with one differentiable signal $x(t)$ whose CTFT $X(j\omega)$ is already known. We want the transform of $x'(t)$, so write the CTFT integral for the derivative and keep the known spectrum in view.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{d}{dt}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt = \left[x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+j\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

The integration-by-parts step produces a boundary term and a frequency factor. Under the usual assumption that the boundary term vanishes, only the $j\omega$ factor remains multiplying the original transform.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}x(t)\right\}=j\omega X(j\omega)
$$

This means each frequency component is scaled by its own $j\omega$ factor. The shortcut is valid whenever the original transform pair is known and the boundary term goes to zero.

The operating rule for later steps is: differentiate in time first, then multiply the known spectrum by $j\omega$ instead of re-evaluating the integral.

## Transforming a First Derivative from a Known Spectrum

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega_0 t}$ using the known transform pair for $e^{j\omega_0 t}$.

**Explanation**

We already know the transform pair $e^{j\omega_0 t} \leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)$, so the derivative property lets us move straight to the frequency domain. The cue is that a time derivative is being taken on a signal whose spectrum is already known.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega_0 t}\right\}=j\omega\left(2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)\right)
$$

The $\delta$ spike is supported only at $\omega=\omega_0$, so the $j\omega$ factor evaluates to $j\omega_0$ at the spike. That turns the product into a single scaled impulse.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega_0 t}\right\}=j\omega_0\,2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)
$$

So the derivative spectrum is the same spike at $\omega_0$, scaled by the derivative constant $j\omega_0$. The example shows the shortcut in its cleanest form: use the known pair, multiply by $j\omega$, and simplify at the spike.

<a id="applying-the-j-omega-factor-to-a-known-transform-pair"></a>
## Applying the j $\omega$ Factor to a Known Transform Pair

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega_0 t)$ by multiplying the known cosine spectrum by $j\omega$.

**Explanation**

We already know the transform pair $\cos(\omega_0 t) \leftrightarrow \pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-\omega_0)+\delta(\omega+\omega_0)\bigr]$, so the cue is a derivative applied to a known pair. That means we can stay in the frequency domain and multiply the cosine spectrum by $j\omega$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega_0 t)\right\}=j\omega\,\pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-\omega_0)+\delta(\omega+\omega_0)\bigr]
$$

The spike at $\omega=\omega_0$ picks up the factor $j\omega_0$, while the spike at $\omega=-\omega_0$ picks up the factor $-j\omega_0$. That is why the two terms end up with opposite signs after simplification.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega_0 t)\right\}=j\omega_0\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)-j\omega_0\pi\delta(\omega+\omega_0)
$$

So the derivative spectrum keeps the same two-sided support as the cosine, but each spike is scaled by the frequency where it lives. This is the clean two-spike version of the $j\omega$ shortcut.

## Combining Linearity with the Time-Derivative Property

**Example:** Find the CTFT of $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(3\cos(2t)-2\cos(5t)\bigr)$ by combining linearity with the derivative property.

**Explanation**

The cue is a weighted sum inside the derivative, so split the expression first. Linearity lets you transform each cosine term separately before you recombine the pieces.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(3\cos(2t)-2\cos(5t)\bigr)\right\}=3\,\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(2t)\right\}-2\,\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(5t)\right\}
$$

From the previous example, use $\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\Omega t)\right\}=j\Omega\pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-\Omega)-\delta(\omega+\Omega)\bigr]$. Apply it once with $\Omega=2$ and once with $\Omega=5$, keeping the original coefficients $3$ and $-2$ attached.

$$
3\bigl(2j\pi[\delta(\omega-2)-\delta(\omega+2)]\bigr)-2\bigl(5j\pi[\delta(\omega-5)-\delta(\omega+5)]\bigr)
$$

Now simplify the coefficients. The first pair becomes $6j\pi[\delta(\omega-2)-\delta(\omega+2)]$, and the second pair becomes $-10j\pi[\delta(\omega-5)-\delta(\omega+5)]$.

$$
6j\pi\delta(\omega-2)-6j\pi\delta(\omega+2)-10j\pi\delta(\omega-5)+10j\pi\delta(\omega+5)
$$

The result stays separated by frequency pair, which makes the linearity step visible. That is the spectrum of the derivative of the weighted sum without redoing any transform integral.

<a id="using-the-time-derivative-property-to-avoid-the-transform-integral"></a>
## Using the Time-Derivative Property to Avoid the Transform Integral

**Example:** Without evaluating any transform integrals, find the CTFT of $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(2\cos(3t)-4\sin(2t)+e^{j5t}\bigr)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a composite derivative built from signals with known transform pairs, so do not start an integral. Split the sum first, then apply linearity and the derivative property term by term.

$$
\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(2\cos(3t)-4\sin(2t)+e^{j5t}\bigr)\right\}=2\,\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\cos(3t)\right\}-4\,\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}\sin(2t)\right\}+\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{d}{dt}e^{j5t}\right\}
$$

Use the known pairs from the earlier steps: $\cos(3t)\leftrightarrow \pi\bigl[\delta(\omega-3)+\delta(\omega+3)\bigr]$, $\sin(2t)\leftrightarrow j\pi\bigl[\delta(\omega+2)-\delta(\omega-2)\bigr]$, and $e^{j5t}\leftrightarrow 2\pi\delta(\omega-5)$. Multiplying each pair by $j\omega$ gives the derivative spectra without any new integration.

$$
2\bigl(3j\pi[\delta(\omega-3)-\delta(\omega+3)]\bigr)-4\bigl(2\pi[\delta(\omega-2)+\delta(\omega+2)]\bigr)+10j\pi\delta(\omega-5)
$$

The coefficients stay attached to their own frequency spikes: the cosine term becomes a signed $j\pi$ pair, the sine term becomes a real two-spike pair, and the complex exponential becomes a single shifted spike scaled by $j5$. Now combine the pieces into one spectrum.

$$
6j\pi\delta(\omega-3)-6j\pi\delta(\omega+3)-8\pi\delta(\omega-2)-8\pi\delta(\omega+2)+10j\pi\delta(\omega-5)
$$

This is the CTFT with no transform integral at all. The only work was decomposition, reuse of known pairs, and recombination under linearity.
