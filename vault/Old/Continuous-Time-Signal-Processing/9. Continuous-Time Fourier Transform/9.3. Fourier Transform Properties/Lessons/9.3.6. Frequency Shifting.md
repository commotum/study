## Introduction to Frequency Shifting

A complex exponential in time does not change the shape of a Fourier transform; it shifts the spectrum along the $\omega$ axis. For a known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, the sign on the exponential tells you which direction the shift goes.

$$
x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)
$$

Start from the known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and multiply by $e^{j\omega_0 t}$. In the transform integral, that factor combines with the kernel $e^{-j\omega t}$, so the frequency variable appears as $\omega-\omega_0$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

That is the same transform as $X(j(\omega-\omega_0))$. So $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ shifts the spectrum to the right by $\omega_0$ without changing its shape.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}\} = X(j(\omega-\omega_0))
$$

The companion rule is the mirror image: $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ produces $X(j(\omega+\omega_0))$, which shifts the spectrum left by $\omega_0$. Keep those two sign patterns fixed.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\} = X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
$$

The next examples will only use these two cases: apply the shift rule and read the sign of the modulating exponential.

<a id="shifting-xj-omega-to-xjomega-omega0"></a>
## Shifting $X(j \omega)$ to $X(j(\omega - omega0)$)

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, find the Fourier transform of $x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}$.

**Explanation**

Start from the known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$. The cue is the positive modulating factor $e^{j\omega_0 t}$, so use the right-shift rule from the introduction.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Combine the exponentials inside the integral: $e^{j\omega_0 t}e^{-j\omega t}=e^{-j(\omega-\omega_0)t}$. That is the same kernel as the original transform, but with $\omega$ replaced by $\omega-\omega_0$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}\} = X(j(\omega-\omega_0))
$$

So multiplying by $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ shifts the spectrum to the right by $\omega_0$ without changing its shape. The result is the original spectrum evaluated at the shifted frequency argument.

## Shifting $X(j \omega)$ to $X(j(\omega + omega0)$)

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, find the Fourier transform of $x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}$.

**Explanation**

Start from the known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$. The cue is the negative modulating factor $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$, so use the left-shift rule from the introduction. This is the mirror image of the previous example: only the sign on the exponential changes, and the spectral shift flips with it.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Combine the exponentials inside the integral: $e^{-j\omega_0 t}e^{-j\omega t}=e^{-j(\omega+\omega_0)t}$. That is the same kernel as the original transform, but with $\omega$ replaced by $\omega+\omega_0$.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\} = X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
$$

So multiplying by $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ shifts the spectrum to the left by $\omega_0$ without changing its shape. The result is the original spectrum evaluated at the shifted frequency argument.

## Applying a Numeric Frequency Shift to a Known Pair

**Example:** Given $e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}$, find the Fourier transform of $e^{-t}u(t)e^{j3t}$.

**Explanation**

Start from the known pair $e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}$. The cue is the positive modulating factor $e^{j3t}$, so use the right-shift rule from the introduction. This is the same shift rule as before, but now the shift amount is a concrete number.

$$
e^{-t}u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

The right-shift rule replaces $\omega$ with $\omega-3$ inside the known spectrum. So the shifted transform is the original expression evaluated at the shifted frequency argument.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{e^{-t}u(t)e^{j3t}\} = \frac{1}{1+j(\omega-3)}
$$

So multiplying by $e^{j3t}$ shifts the spectrum to the right by $3$ without changing its shape. The only change is the frequency argument in the denominator.

## Choosing the Correct Shift Direction from the Modulating Exponential

**Example:** Given $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, determine the Fourier transform of $x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}$.

**Explanation**

Start from the known pair $x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$. The cue is the negative modulating factor $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$, so use the left-shift rule from the introduction. This is the sign-recognition version of the rule: the minus sign in time produces a plus sign inside the frequency argument.

$$
x(t) \leftrightarrow X(j\omega)
$$

The left-shift rule replaces $\omega$ with $\omega+\omega_0$ inside the spectrum. So do not keep the spectrum unchanged and do not copy the sign directly from the exponential; reverse it in the frequency argument instead.

$$
\mathcal{F}\{x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}\} = X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
$$

So the negative modulating exponential produces the opposite frequency shift. The mnemonic is simple: a minus sign in time gives a plus sign inside the frequency argument.
