## Introduction to Cosine Modulation

When you see $y(t)=x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)$ and you already know $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$, read the cosine as a modulation cue: the spectrum will split into two shifted copies of $X(j\omega)$, each with half weight.

$$
y(t)=x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)
$$

Rewrite the cosine first. Euler's formula makes the two modulation directions visible at once, so the cosine becomes an average of one positive-frequency exponential and one negative-frequency exponential.

$$
\cos(\omega_0 t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j\omega_0 t}+e^{-j\omega_0 t}\right)
$$

Now use linearity. The product becomes the sum of two terms, and the factor $1/2$ stays attached to both terms instead of disappearing.

Apply the frequency-shift rule to each exponential factor. The $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ term shifts the spectrum to $X(j(\omega-\omega_0))$, and the $e^{-j\omega_0 t}$ term shifts it to $X(j(\omega+\omega_0))$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega-\omega_0))+\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
$$

So cosine modulation does not create a new spectrum shape. It produces two half-weighted copies of the original spectrum centered at $+\omega_0$ and $-\omega_0$.

## Writing a Cosine Product as Two Complex-Exponential Shifts

**Example:** Given $y(t)=x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)$, rewrite the product as a half-weighted sum of two complex-exponential modulations of $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

Start from $y(t)=x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)$. The cue is the cosine factor, and the goal is to rewrite it as two equal-weight complex-exponential modulations of the same signal.

$$
\cos(\omega_0 t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{j\omega_0 t}+e^{-j\omega_0 t}\right)
$$

Substitute that identity into the product and distribute $x(t)$ across both terms. Keep the factor $1/2$ on each term.

$$
y(t)=\frac{1}{2}x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}+\frac{1}{2}x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}
$$

That is the decomposition the next step will use: one cosine product becomes two half-weighted exponential modulations, one with $+\omega_0$ and one with $-\omega_0$.

## Computing the Two Half-Weighted Shifted Spectra

**Example:** Given $x(t)$ has CTFT $X(j\omega)$, compute the CTFT of $x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)$.

**Explanation**

Start from $x(t)\leftrightarrow X(j\omega)$ and the decomposition from the previous step. The cue is the two exponential factors: each one shifts the same spectrum instead of changing its shape.

$$
y(t)=\frac{1}{2}x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t}+\frac{1}{2}x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t}
$$

Apply the frequency-shift rule to the positive exponential term first. Multiplying by $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ moves the spectrum to the right by $\omega_0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t)e^{j\omega_0 t} &\leftrightarrow X(j(\omega-\omega_0)) \\
x(t)e^{-j\omega_0 t} &\leftrightarrow X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
\end{aligned}
$$

Now attach the common $1/2$ weight to both shifted spectra and combine them. The negative exponential term shifts the spectrum to the left by $\omega_0$, so both pieces stay balanced and separate.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega-\omega_0))+\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega+\omega_0))
$$

So cosine modulation turns one product into two half-weighted spectral copies centered at $+\omega_0$ and $-\omega_0$.

## Applying Cosine Modulation to a Standard Transform Pair

**Example:** Given $e^{-t}u(t)$ has CTFT $1/(1+j\omega)$, find the CTFT of $e^{-t}u(t)\cos(3t)$.

**Explanation**

Start from the known pair $e^{-t}u(t)\leftrightarrow 1/(1+j\omega)$. The cue is the cosine factor, so the earlier cosine-modulation rule applies directly and we do not re-derive the transform from scratch.

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}u(t),\qquad X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

Use the cosine-modulation formula with the numeric modulation frequency $\omega_0=3$. That gives two shifted copies of the same standard spectrum, each with weight $1/2$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega-3))+\frac{1}{2}X(j(\omega+3))
$$

Now substitute the standard pair into both shifted terms. The transform is just the original rational expression evaluated at $\omega-3$ and $\omega+3$.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{1+j(\omega-3)}+\frac{1}{1+j(\omega+3)}\right]
$$

So multiplying $e^{-t}u(t)$ by $\cos(3t)$ does not change the base spectrum shape. It produces the same spectrum centered at $+3$ and $-3$, with half weight on each copy.

## Interpreting Cosine Modulation as Two Shifted Spectral Copies

**Example:** For $y(t)=e^{-t}u(t)\cos(3t)$, interpret the spectrum as two half-height copies of the original spectrum centered at $\omega=\pm 3$.

**Explanation**

Start from the base spectrum $X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ for $e^{-t}u(t)$. The cue is the shifted arguments in the cosine-modulation formula, so this step is about reading the final spectrum as a pair of copies of that same base shape.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{1+j(\omega-3)}+\frac{1}{1+j(\omega+3)}\right]
$$

The term with $\omega-3$ is the original spectrum shifted right by $3$, and the term with $\omega+3$ is the same spectrum shifted left by $3$. Cosine modulation does not create a new shape; it places two copies of the base spectrum on the frequency axis.

Each shifted copy carries the factor $\frac{1}{2}$, so each one has half the original height. That scaling is part of the cosine-modulation rule and applies to both sidebands equally.

So the interpretation is two identical spectral copies centered at $\omega=3$ and $\omega=-3$, each with half amplitude. This is the reusable reading rule for cosine modulation: one base spectrum becomes two half-height sidebands at the modulation frequencies.
