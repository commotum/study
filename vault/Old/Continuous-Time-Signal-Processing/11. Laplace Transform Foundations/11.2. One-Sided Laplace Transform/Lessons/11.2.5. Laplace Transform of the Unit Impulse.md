## Introduction to Laplace Transform of the Unit Impulse

Use the one-sided definition as your starting template: $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{x(t)\}=\int_{0^-}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$, where the integral starts at $0^-$ rather than $0$.

For impulse calculations, the key cue is the sifting identity: the integral of $\delta(t)$ against a test function returns the function value at zero, and the $0^-$ lower limit keeps this rule aligned with one-sided causal setup.

$$
\\mathcal{L}_{+}\\{\\delta(t)\\}=\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}\\,dt
$$

Apply the sifting step directly: $\int_{0^-}^{\infty}\delta(t)e^{-st}\,dt$ collapses to $e^{-s\cdot 0}$, because the sampled point is $t=0$.

$$
\\mathcal{L}_{+}\\{\\delta(t)\\}=e^{-s\\cdot 0}=1
$$

So this section’s result is $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{\delta(t)\}=1$, and keeping $0^-$ is the boundary rule that preserves origin impulses in later shifted-impulse and signal-composition steps.

## Applying Sifting to the One-Sided Transform of $\delta(t)$

**Example:** Find $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{\delta(t)\}$.

**Explanation**

The goal here is to compute a concrete one-sided impulse transform by direct substitution, starting from the definition of the operation and then applying the sifting rule at the impulse location.

$$
\\mathcal{L}_{+}\\{\\delta(t)\\}=\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt
$$

Now recognize the cue: this is an impulse multiplied by a smooth test function, so use sifting. With $f(t)=e^{-st}$, the term $\delta(t)f(t)$ evaluates to $f(0)$ inside the one-sided interval.

$$
\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=e^{-s\cdot 0}
$$

Since the lower limit is $0^-$, the origin impulse is included in the one-sided interval, so this sampled value is retained. Simplify to $1$.

$$
\\mathcal{L}_{+}\\{\\delta(t)\\}=1
$$

## Including the Origin Impulse with the $0^-$ Lower Limit

**Example:** Show why using $\int_{0^-}^{\infty}$ keeps $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{\delta(t)\}=1$, while $\int_{0^+}^{\infty}$ would omit the origin impulse.

**Explanation**

Use both one-sided candidates so the boundary rule is explicit before evaluating the transform value.

$$
\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=1,\quad \int_{0^+}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=0
$$

For one-sided transforms, the convention rule is: use the lower limit $0^-$ when an impulse or jump at the origin must be included in the signal interval.

$$
\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=e^{-s\cdot 0}=1
$$

Only the $0^-$ interval samples the boundary impulse at $t=0$, so it returns the sifting contribution; the $0^+$ interval starts strictly after the impulse and contributes $0$.

$$
\\int_{0^+}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=0
$$

This topic’s core convention is therefore operational: with one-sided transforms, include the origin impulse via $0^-$ so that $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{\delta(t)\}=1$.

## Transforming Scaled Unit Impulses at the Origin

**Example:** Find $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{5\delta(t)\}$.

**Explanation**

This is the same one-sided impulse setup as before, now with an amplitude coefficient: treat $x(t)=5\delta(t)$ and keep the $0^-$ lower limit convention.

$$
\\mathcal{L}_{+}\{5\delta(t)\}=\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}5\delta(t)e^{-st}dt
$$

Use the sifting rule and pull out the constant $5$ from the integral, since constants are linear under integration.

$$
5\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\delta(t)e^{-st}dt
$$

Apply the same impulse-at-the-origin evaluation as earlier: $\delta(t)$ samples $e^{-st}$ at $t=0$ and the lower limit includes the boundary value.

$$
\\int_{0^-}^{\\infty}\\delta(t)e^{-st}dt=e^{-s\cdot 0}=1
$$

Therefore, $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{5\delta(t)\}=5\cdot 1 =5$, and the same pattern is $\mathcal{L}_{+}\{A\delta(t)\}=A$.
