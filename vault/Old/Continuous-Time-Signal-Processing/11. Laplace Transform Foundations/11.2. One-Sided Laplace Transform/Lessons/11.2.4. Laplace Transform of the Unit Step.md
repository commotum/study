## Introduction to Laplace Transform of the Unit Step

The target object in this step is the one-sided Laplace transform of the unit step. You should recognize that $u(t)$ is the special case of $e^{-at}u(t)$ when $a=0$, so this section reuses the same transform structure from the previous lesson and narrows it to the simplest base signal.

Write the one-sided transform setup directly from the definition: start with the time-domain signal, attach the exponential kernel, and keep the $0^-$ lower limit so the setup is valid for causal signals in one-sided Laplace notation.

$$
U(s)=\int_{0^-}^{\infty} u(t)e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Because $u(t)=1$ for $t\ge 0$, the integral reduces to a plain exponential integral over the one-sided interval.

$$
U(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Integrate once to get $U(s)=\left[-\frac{1}{s}e^{-st}\right]_{0^-}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{s}$, and then require the convergence condition $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$ so the upper-limit term vanishes. This gives the unit-step pair result, which we will reuse throughout later examples in this lesson.

## Computing the Unit Step Transform as the $a=0$ Exponential Case

**Example:** Compute the one-sided Laplace transform of $x(t)=u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The key cue is that this is the same one-sided exponential-pattern transform from the previous section, with the new wrinkle $a=0$. Use the standard causal definition and write the transform directly from the signal.

$$
U(s)=\int_{0^-}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-st}\,dt
$$

For $t\ge 0$, $u(t)=1$. Replace the signal with that constant and simplify the setup to an exponential-only integral over the one-sided interval.

$$
U(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Integrate, then evaluate the antiderivative at the bounds.

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{s}e^{-st}\right]_{0}^{\infty} = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{s}e^{-sb}\right)+\frac{1}{s}
$$

Require $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$ so the term $e^{-sb}$ decays to zero as $b\to\infty$, leaving only $\frac{1}{s}$.

$$
U(s)=\frac{1}{s},\quad \operatorname{Re}(s)>0
$$

State the transform pair as $u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{s}$, and note that this is explicitly a one-sided result with lower limit $0^-$ because we are using the unilateral definition.

## Deriving $U(s)=1/s$ from the One-Sided Integral

**Example:** Evaluate $\int_{0^-}^{\infty} e^{-st}\,dt$ and obtain the one-sided transform $U(s)$.

**Explanation**

Use this as a bound-checking verification pass. The cue is still the unit-step transform, but now the goal is to make the one-sided limit handling explicit so it can be reproduced step by step.

$$
U(s)=\int_{0^-}^{\infty}u(t)e^{-st}\,dt
$$

Since $u(t)$ is one on $t\ge 0$, the integral is the same as evaluating the antiderivative of $e^{-st}=e^{-(s+0)t}$, with the lower limit kept as $0^-$.

$$
\int_{0^-}^{\infty}e^{-st}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{s}e^{-st}\right]_{0^-}^{\infty}
$$

Evaluate the lower bound first: at $t\to 0^-$, $\lim_{t\to 0^-}e^{-st}=1$, so the lower-term contribution is $-(-1/s\cdot 1)=1/s$.

Now evaluate the upper bound with a parameter $b$ to isolate convergence: write $\int_{0^-}^{\infty}e^{-st}\,dt=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{s}e^{-sb}\right)+\frac{1}{s}$.

For convergence the first term must vanish; this happens only when $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$. If $\operatorname{Re}(s)\le 0$, the upper expression does not decay and the one-sided integral does not produce a finite transform.

$$
U(s)=\frac{1}{s},\quad \operatorname{Re}(s)>0
$$

Write the final reusable statement as $u(t)\leftrightarrow 1/s$ with explicit ROC tag $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$, which is the form students will reuse directly in the next practice question model.

<a id="checking-the-operatornameres0-convergence-condition"></a>
## Checking the $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$ Convergence Condition

**Example:** For $U(s)=\int_{0^-}^{\infty} e^{-st}\,dt$, determine where the integral converges.

**Explanation**

The cue is a convergence check rather than a new integration. Keep the same expression from the previous step and test when the upper-limit term decays.

$$
s=\sigma+j\omega
$$

Substitute into the exponential to isolate decay: $e^{-st}=e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}$. Only $e^{-\sigma t}$ controls the magnitude as $t\to\infty$.

$$
\left|e^{-st}\right|=\left|e^{-\sigma t}e^{-j\omega t}\right|=e^{-\sigma t}
$$

Evaluate the limit at infinity: if $\sigma>0$, then $\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\sigma t}=0$ and the improper integral term vanishes. If $\sigma\le 0$, the term does not decay, so the one-sided integral is not finite.

State the region of convergence as a required part of the pair knowledge, not a decoration: $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$, i.e. $\sigma>0$.

$$
\text{ROC: }\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
$$

Carry this forward by attaching the same condition to the stored pair $u(t)\leftrightarrow 1/s$, so later inverse lookup and inverse-check questions remain mathematically valid.

## Recognizing $1/s$ as the Unit Step Transform Pair

**Example:** Match $U(s)=1/s$, $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$, to its one-sided time-domain signal and explain the match.

**Explanation**

The cue is inversion-retrieval: use the computed $U(s)$ and convert it back to the time-domain signal family from this lesson, keeping the ROC as part of the answer.

Start from nearby causal pairs in this sequence: $\mathcal{L}\{u(t)\}=1/s$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s)>0$, and $\mathcal{L}\{e^{-at}u(t)\}=1/(s+a)$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s)>-a$.

Reject nonmatching forms by checking the denominator pattern and ROC. A shifted exponential like $1/(s+1)$ is not $1/s$, and missing or opposite-sided ROC also disqualifies the exact unit-step match.

The exact pair is therefore

$$
u(t) \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{s},\quad \operatorname{Re}(s)>0
$$

Record this as a reusable inverse rule: $\frac{1}{s}$ with right-half-plane ROC is the causal unit step, while $\operatorname{Re}(s)$ tag errors (for example $\operatorname{Re}(s)<0$) should be flagged before selecting an answer.
