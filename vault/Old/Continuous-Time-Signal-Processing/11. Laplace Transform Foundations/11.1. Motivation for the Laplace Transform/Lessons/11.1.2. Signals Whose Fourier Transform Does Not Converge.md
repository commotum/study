## Introduction to Signals Whose Fourier Transform Does Not Converge

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

To use Fourier analysis, start by writing the transform definition exactly as $X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt$. The first object the learner must recognize is this integral template, and the first rule is that it is the decision point for every signal in this lesson.

Because $|e^{-j\omega t}|=1$, convergence is controlled only by $x(t)$. The convergence test is an absolute-value check on the same support in the transform, written as $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|\,dt < \infty$. This is the practical rule used on each unbounded interval of time support.

$$
\int_0^{\infty} e^{-at} \,dt = \frac{1}{a},\quad a>0
$$

Use the base case $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$ with $a>0$ as the convergent anchor. It satisfies the magnitude test, so it has a valid Fourier-transform result and sets the minimum standard for this lesson.

Contrast that with signals that do not decay: constant or growing tails can make $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|dt$ diverge, so the Fourier transform is not guaranteed in the ordinary $j\omega$ sense. The move is to classify the signal first, before doing any additional manipulation.

After this screen, keep only convergent signals for the ordinary Fourier-frequency viewpoint, and flag nondecaying or growing behavior as cases that trigger the upcoming Laplace-domain exponential weighting move.

## Testing Fourier-Transform Integral Convergence

**Example:** Test convergence of $X(j\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}dt$.

**Explanation**

The signal is one-sided because of $u(t)$, so write the Fourier integral as a one-sided improper integral: $X(j\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$.

Use the cue that the exponential kernel has unit magnitude, so $\left|e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}\right|=e^{-2t}$. Test convergence on this envelope instead of the full complex signal.

$$
\int_0^\infty |e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}|\,dt=\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt
$$

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2t}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{1}{2}
$$

Since the absolute-value integral is finite, the Fourier-transform integral is convergent in the standard sense. Therefore this decaying one-sided signal is accepted by the $j\omega$-axis rule.

## Recognizing Nondecaying Signals That Do Not Converge

**Example:** Determine convergence for $x(t)=\cos(\omega_0 t)$ over $(-\infty,\infty)$ using magnitude behavior.

**Explanation**

Write the ordinary Fourier-transform integral over the full time axis: $X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos(\omega_0 t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt$. The support is unbounded, so convergence depends on what happens along the tails.

The Fourier kernel has unit magnitude, so it cannot make a nondecaying signal shrink: $|e^{-j\omega t}|=1$. The cosine factor remains bounded but does not decay toward zero.

$$
|\cos(\omega_0 t)e^{-j\omega t}|=|\cos(\omega_0 t)|
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\cos(\omega_0 t)|\,dt=\infty
$$

Because the magnitude integral has infinite area on the unbounded interval, the Fourier transform does not converge in the ordinary improper-integral sense. Boundedness alone is not enough; the signal must decay enough for the integral test to pass.

This is the diagnostic pattern for nondecaying periodic signals: they may be finite in amplitude, but their continuing tails keep the Fourier integral from converging. Growing signals are the next extreme and also fail this standard Fourier test.

## Recognizing Growing Signals That Do Not Converge

**Example:** Test convergence of $x(t)=e^{3t}u(t)$ by evaluating the implied weighted-free magnitude integral.

**Explanation**

Start with the ordinary Fourier-transform setup. Because $u(t)$ restricts the signal to $t\ge 0$, the integral only has a right-sided tail to check.

$$
X(j\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{3t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

The Fourier kernel does not damp the signal because $|e^{-j\omega t}|=1$. So the magnitude behavior is controlled by the growing factor $e^{3t}$.

$$
\left|e^{3t}e^{-j\omega t}\right|=e^{3t}
$$

Now test the corresponding magnitude integral on $[0,\infty)$. Its antiderivative grows without bound as the upper limit increases.

$$
\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{3t}\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}e^{3t}\,dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{e^{3T}-1}{3}=\infty
$$

Therefore $x(t)=e^{3t}u(t)$ does not have a convergent ordinary Fourier transform. This is a growth-induced failure: the signal needs exponential weighting before a transform integral can converge.

## Identifying Signals That Need Exponential Weighting

**Example:** Find the condition on $\sigma$ for $x(t)=e^{2t}u(t)$ so that $\int_0^{\infty}e^{(2-\sigma)t}dt$ converges.

**Explanation**

Cue: the signal $x(t)=e^{2t}u(t)$ grows on the right, so the previous unweighted Fourier test fails. Introduce exponential weighting and test the modified integral with parameter $\sigma$.

Choose the weighted Fourier-style form that isolates the real exponential factor: for a causal support, write

$$
X_{\sigma}(j\omega)=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-\sigma t}\,e^{2t}u(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt = \int_0^{\infty} e^{(2-\sigma)t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Use magnitude logic next: $\left|e^{-j\omega t}\right|=1$, so the oscillatory part does not affect convergence. Convergence depends on $\int_0^{\infty} e^{(2-\sigma)t}\,dt$.

$$
\int_0^{\infty}\left|e^{-\sigma t}e^{2t}u(t)e^{-j\omega t}\right|\,dt=\int_0^{\infty}e^{(2-\sigma)t}\,dt
$$

For an improper exponential tail, the one-sided integral is finite only when the exponent is negative: $2-\sigma<0$. Convert this to a condition on $\sigma$.

$$
2-\sigma<0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \sigma>2
$$

Result: $x(t)=e^{2t}u(t)$ needs a weight with $\sigma>2$ for weighted-convergence. This is the first corrective template before generalizing to $e^{at}u(t)$ with condition $\sigma>a$.
