## Introduction to Representing Signals with Shifted Impulses

A shifted impulse component is a compact symbolic way to record one time location and one signal value. Later, these components will be useful for LTI-system analysis, but the first move is only to read and write the component data.

The basic form is a weighted shifted impulse:

$$
a\delta(t-t_0)
$$

The shifted impulse $\delta(t-t_0)$ marks the time location $t_0$. The multiplier $a$ is the weight, which records the signal value being encoded at that location.

For example, if one listed signal value is $x(2)=3$, then the time location is $2$ and the weight is $3$. The matching shifted impulse component is

$$
x(2)=3 \quad \longrightarrow \quad 3\delta(t-2)
$$

A short list of signal values is encoded by writing one shifted impulse component for each listed value and adding the components. Each term keeps its own weight and its own time location.

$$
3\delta(t-2)-\delta(t+1)
$$

In this representation, $3\delta(t-2)$ records weight $3$ at $t=2$, while $-\delta(t+1)$ records weight $-1$ at $t=-1$. The shift gives the location, and the multiplier gives the encoded signal value.

## Identifying the Time Location of a Shifted Impulse

**Example:** Identify the time location of the shifted impulse $\delta(t-4)$.

**Explanation**

A shifted impulse matches the pattern $\delta(t-t_0)$. In that pattern, $t_0$ is the time location marked by the impulse.

$$
\delta(t-t_0)
$$

The given expression is $\delta(t-4)$. Comparing it with $\delta(t-t_0)$, the shift value is $t_0=4$.

$$
\delta(t-4)=\delta(t-t_0)\quad\text{with}\quad t_0=4
$$

So the impulse is located at $t=4$. Equivalently, the impulse argument becomes zero at that time:

$$
t-4=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad t=4
$$

The sign inside the parentheses belongs to the shift, not to the location itself. That is why $\delta(t-4)$ marks $t=4$, while a plus form such as $\delta(t+a)$ would mark $t=-a$.

<a id="interpreting-the-weight-on-a-shifted-impulse"></a>
## Interpreting the Weight on a Shifted Impulse

**Example:** In $-3\delta(t-2)$, identify the impulse weight and the time location.

**Explanation**

Use the weighted shifted-impulse pattern $a\delta(t-t_0)$. The multiplier outside $\delta$ is the weight, and the shift inside $\delta(t-t_0)$ gives the time location.

$$
a\delta(t-t_0)
$$

In $-3\delta(t-2)$, the multiplier outside the $\delta$ expression is $-3$. That multiplier is the impulse weight.

$$
a=-3
$$

Now read the location from the shifted impulse part. The expression $\delta(t-2)$ matches $\delta(t-t_0)$, so $t_0=2$.

$$
\delta(t-2)=\delta(t-t_0)\quad\text{with}\quad t_0=2
$$

Therefore, the impulse has weight $-3$ and is located at $t=2$. The negative sign belongs to the weight; it does not change the time location.

$$
\text{weight}=-3,\qquad \text{location: }t=2
$$

## Encoding One Signal Value with a Shifted Impulse

**Example:** Write the shifted-impulse component that encodes $x(3)=-2$.

**Explanation**

Use the component pattern $a\delta(t-t_0)$. The time in $x(t_0)$ gives the impulse location, and the signal value gives the coefficient.

$$
x(t_0)=a\quad\longrightarrow\quad a\delta(t-t_0)
$$

In $x(3)=-2$, the target time is $t_0=3$. That means the shifted impulse part should be $\delta(t-3)$.

$$
t_0=3\quad\longrightarrow\quad \delta(t-3)
$$

The signal value is $-2$, so $-2$ becomes the coefficient multiplying the shifted impulse.

$$
a=-2
$$

Combining the coefficient with the shifted impulse gives the component that encodes the value. The negative sign stays on the coefficient; it does not change the time location.

$$
-2\delta(t-3)
$$

## Encoding Multiple Signal Values with Shifted Impulses

**Example:** Write a shifted-impulse representation for $x(-1)=2$, $x(0)=3$, and $x(4)=-1$.

**Explanation**

Use the same one-value rule for each listed signal value: $x(t_0)=a$ becomes the component $a\delta(t-t_0)$. Each time-value pair gets its own shifted impulse component.

$$
x(t_0)=a\quad\longrightarrow\quad a\delta(t-t_0)
$$

Convert each pair separately. For $x(-1)=2$, the weight is $2$ and the location is $t_0=-1$, so the shift becomes $t-(-1)=t+1$.

$$
x(-1)=2\quad\longrightarrow\quad 2\delta(t+1)
$$

For $x(0)=3$, the weight is $3$ and the location is $t_0=0$. The shifted impulse is unshifted, so the component uses $\delta(t)$.

$$
x(0)=3\quad\longrightarrow\quad 3\delta(t)
$$

For $x(4)=-1$, the location is $t_0=4$ and the negative signal value stays as the coefficient.

$$
x(4)=-1\quad\longrightarrow\quad -\delta(t-4)
$$

Assemble the representation by adding the three components. Do not combine components at different time locations.

$$
x(t)=2\delta(t+1)+3\delta(t)-\delta(t-4)
$$

<a id="reading-a-shifted-impulse-representation-for-lti-analysis"></a>
## Reading a Shifted-Impulse Representation for LTI Analysis

**Example:** Read the component list in $x(t)=2\delta(t+1)-3\delta(t-2)$ for later LTI analysis.

**Explanation**

To read a shifted-impulse representation, separate it into individual terms. Each term has the form $a\delta(t-t_0)$, where $a$ is the weight and $t_0$ is the time location.

$$
a\delta(t-t_0)\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{weight }a\text{ at location }t=t_0
$$

The given representation has two shifted-impulse terms:

$$
x(t)=2\delta(t+1)-3\delta(t-2)
$$

For the first term, $2\delta(t+1)$, rewrite the shift as $t-(-1)$. The coefficient is $2$, so this component has weight $2$ at $t=-1$.

$$
2\delta(t+1)=2\delta(t-(-1))
$$

For the second term, $-3\delta(t-2)$, the coefficient is $-3$ and the shift matches $\delta(t-t_0)$ with $t_0=2$.

$$
-3\delta(t-2)\quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{weight }-3\text{ at }t=2
$$

So the component list for later LTI analysis is the two input components below. This is only the input data; do not replace the impulses with impulse responses yet.

$$
\begin{aligned}2\delta(t+1)&:\ \text{weight }2,\ \text{location }t=-1\\-3\delta(t-2)&:\ \text{weight }-3,\ \text{location }t=2\end{aligned}
$$
