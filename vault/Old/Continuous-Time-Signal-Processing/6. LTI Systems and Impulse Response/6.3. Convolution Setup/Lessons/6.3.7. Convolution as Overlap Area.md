## Introduction to Convolution as Overlap Area

The convolution integral can be read as an area calculation when the output time is fixed. For one chosen value of t, $\tau$ is the variable that runs along the signal timeline.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

At that fixed output time, the two factors $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$ only contribute on $\tau$-values where both signals are nonzero. Outside their overlap, at least one factor is zero, so the product contributes no area.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s001-te-section-001.png>)

On the overlap interval, multiply the two signal values point by point. This creates the product signal $x(\tau)$$h(t-\tau)$. The signed area under that product, measured along the $\tau$-axis, is the single output value for the chosen time.

$$
\text{fixed }t:\quad y(t)=\text{signed area under }x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\text{ over }\tau
$$

If the product is above the axis, its area adds to $y(t)$. If the product is below the axis, its area subtracts from $y(t)$. If there is no overlap, the product is zero everywhere, so that output value is zero.

In this lesson, the main task is to read convolution output values from overlap area. The focus is the area of the product for one fixed output time, not the full piecewise convolution curve.

<a id="interpreting-the-product-area-for-a-fixed-output-time"></a>
## Interpreting the Product Area for a Fixed Output Time

**Example:** At fixed output time $t=2$, a graph shows $x(\tau)=1$ on $0\le \tau\le 3$ and $h(2-\tau)=2$ on $1\le \tau\le 4$, with both signals zero elsewhere. Interpret $y(2)$ as an overlap area.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $t$ is already fixed at 2. That means $h(2-\tau)$ is a fixed shifted response, and the only variable being scanned is $\tau$.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s002-te-section-002.png>)

Read the two nonzero intervals from the graph. The input $x(\tau)$ is nonzero on $0\le \tau\le 3$, and the shifted response $h(2-\tau)$ is nonzero on $1\le \tau\le 4$.

$$
x(\tau)\ne 0\text{ on }0\le \tau\le 3,\qquad h(2-\tau)\ne 0\text{ on }1\le \tau\le 4
$$

The product can be nonzero only where both intervals are active. The common part is $1\le \tau\le 3$.

$$
0\le \tau\le 3\quad\text{and}\quad 1\le \tau\le 4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le \tau\le 3
$$

On that overlap, $x(\tau)=1$ and $h(2-\tau)=2$, so the product height is 2. The overlap width is $3-1=2$.

$$
x(\tau)h(2-\tau)=1\cdot 2=2,\qquad \text{width}=3-1=2
$$

The output value $y(2)$ is the signed area under the product over the overlap interval. Here the product is positive, so the area is positive.

$$
y(2)=2\cdot 2=4
$$

So for this fixed output time, the overlap area gives $y(2)=4$.

## Reading Positive Overlap Area from Two Positive Signals

**Example:** At fixed output time $t=3$, a graph shows $x(\tau)=2$ on $0\le \tau\le 4$ and $h(3-\tau)=1$ on $1\le \tau\le 3$, with both signals zero elsewhere. Use the overlap area to find $y(3)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that both plotted signals are above the axis wherever they overlap. When both signal values are positive, their product is positive, so the overlap area adds to the convolution output.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s003-te-section-003.png>)

Read the overlap interval from the two supports. The signal $x(\tau)$ is nonzero on $0\le \tau\le 4$, and $h(3-\tau)$ is nonzero on $1\le \tau\le 3$. Their common interval is $1\le \tau\le 3$.

$$
0\le \tau\le 4\quad\text{and}\quad 1\le \tau\le 3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le \tau\le 3
$$

On this overlap, the heights are $x(\tau)=2$ and $h(3-\tau)=1$. Multiplying two positive heights gives a positive product height.

$$
x(\tau)h(3-\tau)=2\cdot 1=2>0
$$

The product rectangle has width $3-1=2$ and height 2. Because the rectangle is above the axis, its signed area is positive.

$$
y(3)=\text{positive area}=2\cdot 2=4
$$

So the positive overlap area contributes $+4$, and the convolution output at this fixed time is $y(3)=4$.

## Accounting for Signed Area When the Product Is Negative

**Example:** At fixed output time $t=1$, a graph shows $x(\tau)=2$ on $0\le \tau\le 3$ and $h(1-\tau)=-1$ on $1\le \tau\le 4$, with both signals zero elsewhere. Use the signed overlap area to find $y(1)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the two overlapping signal values have opposite signs. The overlap area is still found the same way as before, but the product lies below the axis, so its signed area is negative.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s004-te-section-004.png>)

Read the two nonzero intervals from the graph. The input $x(\tau)$ is nonzero on $0\le \tau\le 3$, and the shifted response $h(1-\tau)$ is nonzero on $1\le \tau\le 4$.

$$
0\le \tau\le 3\quad\text{and}\quad 1\le \tau\le 4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le \tau\le 3
$$

On the overlap, $x(\tau)=2$ is positive and $h(1-\tau)=-1$ is negative. The sign of the area comes from the sign of their product.

$$
x(\tau)h(1-\tau)=2\cdot(-1)=-2
$$

The product rectangle has width $3-1=2$ and height $-2$. Since the product is below the axis, the signed area is negative.

$$
y(1)=(-2)(3-1)=-4
$$

So the signed overlap area contributes $-4$, and the convolution output at this fixed time is $y(1)=-4$.

## Comparing Output Values from Different Amounts of Overlap

**Example:** Two panels show the same input $x(\tau)=1$ on $1\le \tau\le 4$ with $h(1-\tau)=2$ on $-1\le \tau\le 2$ in the first panel and $h(3-\tau)=2$ on $1\le \tau\le 4$ in the second panel, with all signals zero elsewhere. Compare $y(1)$ and $y(3)$ by comparing the two overlap areas.

**Explanation**

The cue is that each panel represents a different fixed output time. Use the same signed-area rule in each panel, then compare the resulting product areas.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s005-te-section-005.png>)

For the first panel, the fixed output time is $t=1$. The input is active on $1\le \tau\le 4$, while $h(1-\tau)$ is active on $-1\le \tau\le 2$, so the overlap is $1\le \tau\le 2$.

$$
1\le \tau\le 4\quad\text{and}\quad -1\le \tau\le 2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le \tau\le 2
$$

On that overlap, the product height is $1\cdot 2=2$. The overlap width is $2-1=1$, so the signed product area is positive and equals 2.

$$
y(1)=2(2-1)=2
$$

For the second panel, the fixed output time is $t=3$. The same input is active on $1\le \tau\le 4$, and $h(3-\tau)$ is also active on $1\le \tau\le 4$, so the overlap is wider.

$$
1\le \tau\le 4\quad\text{and}\quad 1\le \tau\le 4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1\le \tau\le 4
$$

The product height is still $1\cdot 2=2$, but the overlap width is $4-1=3$. That gives a larger positive product area.

$$
y(3)=2(4-1)=6
$$

Both product areas are positive, so the larger area gives the larger output value. Therefore, $y(3)>y(1)$.

<a id="recognizing-zero-output-from-no-overlap-area"></a>
## Recognizing Zero Output from No Overlap Area

**Example:** At fixed output time $t=5$, a graph shows $x(\tau)$ supported on $0\le \tau\le 2$ and $h(5-\tau)$ supported on $3\le \tau\le 4$. Use overlap area to determine $y(5)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the two nonzero support intervals are separated. The output does not come from the area of either individual signal; it comes from the signed area of the product where both signals are nonzero at the same $\tau$-values.

![](<../Source/6.3.7. Convolution as Overlap Area/Images/l007-s006-te-section-006.png>)

Read the two supports from the graph. The input $x(\tau)$ is nonzero only on $0\le \tau\le 2$, and the shifted response $h(5-\tau)$ is nonzero only on $3\le \tau\le 4$.

$$
x(\tau)\ne 0\text{ only on }0\le \tau\le 2,\qquad h(5-\tau)\ne 0\text{ only on }3\le \tau\le 4
$$

These intervals do not overlap. There is a gap between the end of the support of $x(\tau)$ and the start of the support of $h(5-\tau)$.

$$
[0,2]\cap[3,4]=\varnothing
$$

Since there is no $\tau$-value where both factors are nonzero, their product is zero for every value of $\tau$.

$$
x(\tau)h(5-\tau)=0\quad\text{for every }\tau
$$

The signed area under a product that is zero everywhere is zero. Therefore the convolution output at this fixed time is $y(5)=0$.

$$
y(5)=0
$$
