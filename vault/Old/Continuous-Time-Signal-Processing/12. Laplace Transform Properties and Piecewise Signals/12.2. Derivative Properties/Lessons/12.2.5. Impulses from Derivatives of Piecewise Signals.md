## Introduction to Impulses from Derivatives of Piecewise Signals

Your target is to find a derivative template for piecewise signals: identify each jump discontinuity, attach the matching shifted impulse, and combine those impulses with the smooth interval derivative.

For a jump at time $t_k$, compute the signed jump size using left and right limits, then add that value as an impulse coefficient: a jump of $\Delta x_k=x(t_k^+)-x(t_k^-)$ becomes $\Delta x_k\,\delta(t-t_k)$ in the derivative representation.

$$
\frac{dx(t)}{dt}=x'_c(t)+\sum_k \Delta x_k\delta(t-t_k)
$$

Use the base case $x(t)=0$ for $t<0$ and $x(t)=3$ for $t\ge 0$. The only jump is at $0$, with $\Delta x= x(0^+)-x(0^-)=3-0=3$, so $\frac{dx(t)}{dt}=3\,\delta(t)$ plus no smooth interval term.

Keep the same split in Laplace work: transform the smooth piece with $\mathcal{L}\{x'_c(t)\}$ and transform each impulse term separately; then add the impulse factors with their shifts.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\mathcal{L}\{x'_c(t)\}+\sum_k \Delta x_k e^{-s t_k}
$$

By the end of this section, you should be able to scan a piecewise signal, list jump points, and immediately write each jump as a signed shifted impulse in $x'(t)$.

## Finding the Jump Size at a Discontinuity

**Example:** For $x(t)=3$ for $t<0$, $x(t)=5$ for $0\le t<2$, $x(t)=1$ for $2\le t<4$, and $x(t)=4$ for $t\ge 4$, compute each jump size.

**Explanation**

The cue is every time the piecewise rule changes. For this signal, list the discontinuity candidates from the interval boundaries: $t=0$, $t=2$, and $t=4$.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}3, & t<0\\5, & 0\le t<2\\1, & 2\le t<4\\4, & t\ge 4\end{cases}
$$

Use the fixed convention from the tutorial, $\Delta x_k=x(t_k^+)-x(t_k^-)$, so each size is right-limit minus left-limit.

$$
\Delta x_0=x(0^+)-x(0^-)=5-3=2
$$

$$
\Delta x_2=x(2^+)-x(2^-)=1-5=-4
$$

$$
\Delta x_4=x(4^+)-x(4^-)=4-1=3
$$

The ordered jump list is $\Delta x_0=2$, $\Delta x_2=-4$, and $\Delta x_4=3$, each to be used as the impulse coefficients at their corresponding shifted times.

## Writing an Impulse for an Upward Jump

**Example:** For $x(t)=1$ for $t<0$, $x(t)=1$ for $0\le t<1$, and $x(t)=5$ for $t\ge1$, find the impulse term in $\frac{d x(t)}{dt}$ at $t=1$.

**Explanation**

This is the upward-jump pattern from a single discontinuity: find the value change at the jump and convert it into a shifted impulse term in $x'(t)$.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}1, & t<0\\1, & 0\le t<1\\5, & t\ge1\end{cases}
$$

Use the left and right limits at the discontinuity point: $x(1^-)$ is the value just before $1$, and $x(1^+)$ is the value just after $1$.

$$
x(1^-)=1,\quad x(1^+)=5
$$

Apply the fixed jump convention $\Delta x = x(1^+)-x(1^-)$ so the jump coefficient comes from right minus left.

$$
\Delta x= x(1^+)-x(1^-)=5-1=4
$$

Translate the jump directly into the derivative as a shifted impulse with that coefficient: the result is

$$
4\delta(t-1)
$$

Final result: the derivative contributes $+4\delta(t-1)$ at $t=1$, with a positive sign because the jump size is positive.

## Writing an Impulse for a Downward Jump

**Example:** For $x(t)=7$ for $t<1$ and $x(t)=2$ for $t\ge1$, find the impulse term in the derivative and write its sign clearly.

**Explanation**

This extends the same jump-to-impulse rule to a downward jump: locate the discontinuity, compute the signed jump size, and write the corresponding impulse term in $x'(t)$.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}7, & t<1\\2, & t\ge1\end{cases}
$$

For the jump at $t=1$, first write the one-sided values: the signal is $x(1^-)=7$ just before $1$ and $x(1^+)=2$ just after $1$.

$$
x(1^-)=7,\quad x(1^+)=2
$$

Apply the sign-safe jump convention $\Delta x=x(1^+)-x(1^-)$.

$$
\Delta x=2-7=-5
$$

Translate the jump to an impulse with the same shift: $\Delta x\delta(t-1)$. Because $\Delta x$ is negative, the impulse coefficient is negative.

$$
-5\delta(t-1)
$$

Final result: the impulse term is $-5\delta(t-1)$ at $t=1$, with the negative sign preserving the downward jump direction.

## Combining Impulses from Multiple Jump Discontinuities

**Example:** For $x(t)=0$ for $t<0$, $x(t)=4$ for $0\le t<1$, $x(t)=1$ for $1\le t<3$, and $x(t)=5$ for $t\ge3$, express only the impulsive part of $x'(t)$.

**Explanation**

This extends the previous step by combining all discontinuities in one signal. The impulse rule is applied at each jump point and then added.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0\\4, & 0\le t<1\\1, & 1\le t<3\\5, & t\ge3\end{cases}
$$

List discontinuity points: $t=0$, $t=1$, and $t=3$. Use $\Delta x=x(t_k^+)-x(t_k^-)$ at each one.

$$
x(0^-)=0,\; x(0^+)=4,\;\Delta x_0=4-0=4
$$

$$
x(1^-)=4,\; x(1^+)=1,\;\Delta x_1=1-4=-3
$$

$$
x(3^-)=1,\; x(3^+)=5,\;\Delta x_3=5-1=4
$$

Translate each jump into a shifted impulse term and keep the signs and locations tied to each jump time.

$$
x'_d(t)=\Delta x_0\,\delta(t-0)+\Delta x_1\,\delta(t-1)+\Delta x_3\,\delta(t-3)
$$

$$
x'_d(t)=4\,\delta(t)-3\,\delta(t-1)+4\,\delta(t-3)
$$

Final result: the impulsive part is $4\delta(t)-3\delta(t-1)+4\delta(t-3)$, with a negative middle term from the downward jump at $t=1$.

<a id="using-jump-impulse-terms-in-a-laplace-transform-derivative"></a>
## Using Jump-Impulse Terms in a Laplace-Transform Derivative

**Example:** For $x(t)=0$ for $t<0$, $x(t)=2t$ for $0\le t<1$, $x(t)=2$ for $1\le t<3$, and $x(t)=t-2$ for $t\ge3$, form $x'(t)$ including impulses and then compute $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}$.

**Explanation**

Use the derivative workflow: write the interval derivative first, then add signed impulse terms for jump points, and finally transform both parts.

$$
x(t)=\begin{cases}0, & t<0\\2t, & 0\le t<1\\2, & 1\le t<3\\t-2, & t\ge3\end{cases}
$$

From the piecewise intervals, the smooth derivative is $x'_c(t)=2u(t)-2u(t-1)+u(t-3)$.

$$
x'_c(t)=\begin{cases}0,& t<0\\2,& 0<t<1\\0,& 1<t<3\\1,& t>3\end{cases}=2u(t)-2u(t-1)+u(t-3)
$$

Next, compute jumps at discontinuities from right minus left: $\Delta x(1)=0$ and $\Delta x(3)=1-2=-1$. So the impulse part is only $-\delta(t-3)$.

$$
x'(t)=x'_c(t)-\delta(t-3)=\left(2u(t)-2u(t-1)+u(t-3)\right)-\delta(t-3)
$$

Now map each piece to Laplace form using linearity and $\mathcal{L}\{u(t-a)\}=\frac{e^{-as}}{s}$ and $\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\}=e^{-as}$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\frac{2}{s}-\frac{2e^{-s}}{s}+\frac{e^{-3s}}{s}-e^{-3s}=\frac{2(1-e^{-s})}{s}+\frac{e^{-3s}}{s}-e^{-3s}
$$

Final result: $x'(t)=2u(t)-2u(t-1)+u(t-3)-\delta(t-3)$ and $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\frac{2}{s}-\frac{2e^{-s}}{s}+\frac{e^{-3s}}{s}-e^{-3s}$.
