## Introduction to First-Derivative Property

Use the one-sided Laplace viewpoint first. We work on causal signals over $[0^-,\infty)$, so the key transform is $\mathcal{L}\{x(t)\}=\int_{0^-}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-st}\,dt$.

To transform $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}$, define $u=e^{-st}$ and $dv=x'(t)dt$ and apply one-step integration by parts. Then treat the base causal case from this topic: if $x(t)=t$ for $t\ge 0$, then $x(0^-)=0$ and the boundary correction does not appear as an extra nonzero constant.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x(t)\}=\int_{0^-}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-st} \, dt
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\left.x(t)e^{-st}\right|_{0^-}^{\infty}+s\int_{0^-}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-st}\,dt=sX(s)-x(0^-)
$$

So the derivative in time maps to multiplication by $s$ in the $s$-domain, then you subtract the pre-initial value. In this one-sided form, the result is $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)$.

Read this as a template: whenever you see $x'(t)$ under a one-sided Laplace, write the $sX(s)$ term first, then always attach the correction $-x(0^-)$. The symbol $x(0^-)$ is the left-limit value at the causal boundary, not a post-origin value.

## Transforming a Single First Derivative

**Example:** Find the Laplace transform of the derivative when $x(t)=t^2u(t)$.

**Explanation**

Use the cue from the prior section: when you see a first derivative, apply the one-sided identity to convert it into an algebraic $s$-domain form. Write it first as $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)$.

Start with the boundary term for this signal. Since $x(t)=t^2u(t)$ is causal with value $0$ at the left of the origin, $x(0^-)=0$.

$$
X(s)=\mathcal{L}\{t^2u(t)\}=\frac{2}{s^3}
$$

Substitute the transform pair into the rule and keep the correction term in the template form.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)=s\left(\frac{2}{s^3}\right)-0
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\frac{2}{s^2}
$$

So for this step, the result is $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=\frac{2}{s^2}$, and the only new wrinkle from the tutorial is that you now use a concrete $X(s)$ value before simplifying.

## Transforming a Scaled First-Derivative Term

**Example:** Find $\mathcal{L}\{5x'(t)\}$ when $x(t)=t^2u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is the scalar multiple: when the derivative is scaled by a constant, first transform the inner derivative and apply linearity to the whole result.

For $x(t)=t^2u(t)$, compute the one-sided boundary term first: $x(0^-)=0$. From the previous step, we already have $X(s)=\mathcal{L}\{t^2u(t)\}=2/s^3$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{5x'(t)\}=5\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}
$$

Now substitute the one-sided derivative rule for $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}$ and preserve the initial-condition structure.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{5x'(t)\}=5\left(sX(s)-x(0^- )\right)=5\left(s\cdot\frac{2}{s^3}-0\right)
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}\{5x'(t)\}=\frac{10}{s^2}
$$

The result is $\mathcal{L}\{5x'(t)\}=10/s^2$. This is exactly the same rule as before, with one extra wrinkle: a constant multiplier must scale the full derivative transform, including the subtraction term form.

## Replacing First Derivatives in a Laplace-Domain Equation

**Example:** Transform $y'(t)+3y(t)=\cos t\,u(t)$ with $y(0^-)=2$ into an $s$-domain equation.

**Explanation**

The cue in this step is a differential equation that contains one derivative term and linear time-domain terms. Treat each term separately in the Laplace domain, then combine them into one algebraic equation.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{y'(t)+3y(t)\} = \mathcal{L}\{\cos t\,u(t)\},
$$

For the derivative, use the same cue-activated rule as before and keep the pre-initial term in place: $\mathcal{L}\{y'(t)\}=sY(s)-y(0^-)$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{y'(t)\}=sY(s)-y(0^-)=sY(s)-2
$$

Transform the remaining left-hand and right-hand terms with linearity and the provided table identities.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{3y(t)\}=3Y(s), \quad \mathcal{L}\{\cos t\,u(t)\}=\frac{s}{s^2+1}
$$

Substitute these pieces into the transformed equation and collect unknown terms on the left.

$$
(sY(s)-2)+3Y(s)=\frac{s}{s^2+1}
$$

$$
(s+3)Y(s)-2=\frac{s}{s^2+1}
$$

Result: the $s$-domain equation is $(s+3)Y(s)-2=s/(s^2+1)$. If a solved form is needed next, isolate $Y(s)$ as a single fraction.
