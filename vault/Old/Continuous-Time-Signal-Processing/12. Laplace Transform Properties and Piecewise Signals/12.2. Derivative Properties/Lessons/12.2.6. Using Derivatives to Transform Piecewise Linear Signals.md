## Introduction to Using Derivatives to Transform Piecewise Linear Signals

This section establishes the piecewise-linear derivative workflow: recognize a piecewise-linear $x(t)$, differentiate it, and then use one-sided derivative and shift rules in the $s$ domain to solve for $X(s)$.

Use the core identity exactly as written: $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)$, and reuse the shifted-step and shifted-impulse pairs, $\mathcal{L}\{u(t-a)\}=\frac{e^{-as}}{s}$ and $\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\}=e^{-as}$.

![](<../Source/12.2.6. Using Derivatives to Transform Piecewise Linear Signals/Images/l006-s001-te-section-001.png>)

The base case is $x(t)=t\,u(t)$, so $x'(t)=u(t)$ and $x(0^-)=0$. Transforming by the one-sided derivative rule gives $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)=sX(s)$. The transform of the derivative side is also $\mathcal{L}\{u(t)\}=\frac{1}{s}$, so the workflow is to set $\frac{1}{s}=sX(s)$ and isolate $X(s)$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)
$$

When converting $x'(t)$ from a piecewise-linear signal, separate two term types: constant-step pieces from slope segments and impulse terms from slope jumps or endpoints. This is the same template you will reuse in every later example.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{u(t-a)\}=\frac{e^{-as}}{s}
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t-a)\}=e^{-as}
$$

Keep the pre-initial value visible at every step, because one-sided Laplace uses the causal boundary term and this section’s examples avoid bilateral shortcuting. After this, each example should follow the same move: identify $x'(t)$ terms, map each term, combine algebra, then solve for $X(s)$.

## Transforming a Causal Ramp from Its First Derivative

**Example:** Find $X(s)$ for $x(t)=3t\,u(t)$. Use only the derivative property and algebraic isolation.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $x(t)$ is a causal ramp. Instead of transforming the ramp directly, differentiate once so the signal becomes a constant step.

$$
x'(t)=3u(t), \qquad x(0^-)=0
$$

Apply the one-sided derivative property $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)$. The derivative side is the transform of $3u(t)$, so it contributes $\frac{3}{s}$.

$$
\frac{3}{s}=sX(s)-0
$$

Now isolate $X(s)$ by dividing by the remaining factor of $s$. The extra power of $s$ comes from solving the derivative-property equation, not from a shift.

$$
X(s)=\frac{3}{s^2}
$$

## Transforming a Shifted Ramp from a Shifted-Step Derivative

**Example:** Find $X(s)$ for $x(t)=2(t-1)u(t-1)$.

**Explanation**

The signal is a ramp that starts at $t=1$ instead of at the origin. The derivative removes the ramp shape and leaves a shifted step, which is easier to transform.

$$
x'(t)=2u(t-1)
$$

Use the one-sided derivative property. Because the shifted ramp is zero before it turns on, $x(0^-)=0$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)=sX(s)
$$

Now transform the shifted step. The delay by $1$ contributes the factor $e^{-s}$, so $\mathcal{L}\{u(t-1)\}=e^{-s}/s$.

$$
sX(s)=\mathcal{L}\{2u(t-1)\}=\frac{2e^{-s}}{s}
$$

Solve by dividing by $s$. The shifted ramp has the same $1/s^2$ ramp structure as the unshifted case, with the extra shift factor $e^{-s}$.

$$
X(s)=\frac{2e^{-s}}{s^2}
$$

## Transforming a Finite Ramp with Endpoint Impulses

**Example:** Find $X(s)$ for $x(t)=2t\,(u(t)-u(t-2))$ and keep endpoint impulses explicit.

**Explanation**

The new feature is the finite right endpoint at $t=2$. Differentiating the ramp still produces step terms, but the sudden drop from $4$ to $0$ also produces a negative impulse at the endpoint.

$$
x'(t)=2u(t)-2u(t-2)-4\delta(t-2), \qquad x(0^-)=0
$$

Use the one-sided derivative property $\mathcal{L}\{x'(t)\}=sX(s)-x(0^-)$. Since the signal is zero before the origin, the initial-value term contributes nothing.

$$
sX(s)=\mathcal{L}\{2u(t)-2u(t-2)-4\delta(t-2)\}
$$

Now transform each derivative term. The two step terms form the finite window contribution, and the endpoint impulse contributes $-4e^{-2s}$.

$$
sX(s)=\frac{2}{s}-\frac{2e^{-2s}}{s}-4e^{-2s}=\frac{2(1-e^{-2s})}{s}-4e^{-2s}
$$

Finally divide by $s$. The endpoint impulse must remain in the derivative equation; dropping it would miss the $-4e^{-2s}/s$ term in $X(s)$.

$$
X(s)=\frac{2(1-e^{-2s})}{s^2}-\frac{4e^{-2s}}{s}
$$

## Transforming a Triangular Signal from Slope-Change Impulses

**Example:** Find $X(s)$ for $x(t)=2t\,u(t)-4(t-1)u(t-1)+2(t-2)u(t-2)$ by using derivatives to expose slope-change impulses.

**Explanation**

The signal is triangular because the slope changes from $0$ to $2$ at $t=0$, from $2$ to $-2$ at $t=1$, and from $-2$ back to $0$ at $t=2$. A second derivative turns those slope changes into impulses, which are easier to transform.

$$
x'(t)=2u(t)-4u(t-1)+2u(t-2)
$$

Differentiating once has removed the ramp pieces. Differentiating again leaves one impulse at each corner, weighted by the change in slope.

$$
x''(t)=2\delta(t)-4\delta(t-1)+2\delta(t-2)
$$

Apply the one-sided second-derivative property $\mathcal{L}\{x''(t)\}=s^2X(s)-sx(0^-)-x'(0^-)$. Since the signal is zero before the origin, $x(0^-)=0$ and $x'(0^-)=0$.

$$
\mathcal{L}\{x''(t)\}=s^2X(s)-sx(0^-)-x'(0^-)=s^2X(s)
$$

Now transform the impulse train. A shifted impulse at $t=a$ contributes $e^{-as}$, so the delays at $t=1$ and $t=2$ attach factors $e^{-s}$ and $e^{-2s}$.

$$
s^2X(s)=2-4e^{-s}+2e^{-2s}
$$

Divide by $s^2$ to recover the transform. The numerator records the slope jumps, and the denominator records that two differentiations were used.

$$
X(s)=\frac{2-4e^{-s}+2e^{-2s}}{s^2}=\frac{2(1-e^{-s})^2}{s^2}
$$
