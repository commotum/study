## Introduction to Frequency Response as Fourier Transform of $h(t)$

For an LTI system, each angular frequency $\omega$ is handled separately. If a sinusoid at that frequency goes in, the system's effect at that same frequency can be summarized by one complex number, $H(j\omega)$.

That complex number is the frequency response, and it is defined by taking the Fourier transform of the impulse response $h(t)$. In other words, $h(t)$ is the time-domain description of the system, and $H(j\omega)$ is the frequency-by-frequency complex gain extracted from it.

$$
H(j\omega)=\mathcal{F}\{h(t)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

This is the rule to remember for the rest of the topic: compute $H(j\omega)$ by transforming $h(t)$, then read the result as the complex gain applied to each sinusoidal component. Later steps will show how to compute that transform for specific impulse responses and interpret the result as magnitude scaling and phase shift.

## Writing $H(j\omega)$ as the Fourier Transform Integral of $h(t)$

**Example:** Given $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$, write $H(j\omega)$ as the Fourier transform integral that defines the frequency response.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit step $u(t)$: it makes the impulse response causal, so the frequency response is still set up from the Fourier transform definition of $h(t)$. For this step, the goal is only to write the correct integral form, not to evaluate it.

$$
H(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Now substitute the given impulse response and use the fact that $u(t)=0$ for $t<0$ and $u(t)=1$ for $t\ge 0$. That changes the support to a one-sided integral over $t\ge 0$.

$$
H(j\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(1+j\omega)t}\,dt
$$

This is the exact setup the next step will use. The only move here is to recognize the causal support, insert $h(t)$ into the transform kernel, and stop before carrying out the integration.

## Computing $H(j\omega)$ from a Simple Impulse Response

**Example:** Compute $H(j\omega)$ for $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit step $u(t)$: it tells you the impulse response is causal, so the frequency response comes from the Fourier transform definition of $h(t)$ with one-sided support.

$$
H(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-2t}e^{-j\omega t}\,dt
$$

Now combine the decay term and the Fourier kernel into one exponential. That gives a standard one-sided exponential integral with exponent $-(2+j\omega)t$.

$$
H(j\omega)=\int_0^{\infty} e^{-(2+j\omega)t}\,dt=\left[\frac{-1}{2+j\omega}e^{-(2+j\omega)t}\right]_0^{\infty}=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

So the simple causal exponential has frequency response $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$. This is the compact closed form the next steps can reuse when they compare different impulse responses.

## Using a Known Fourier Transform Pair to Find $H(j\omega)$

**Example:** Using the known pairs $e^{-t}u(t)\leftrightarrow \frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ and $e^{-2t}u(t)\leftrightarrow \frac{1}{2+j\omega}$, find $H(j\omega)$ for $h(t)=2e^{-t}u(t)+e^{-2t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that $h(t)$ is already written as a sum of standard causal exponentials. That means you should use linearity and map each term through a known Fourier transform pair instead of starting a new integral.

$$
H(j\omega)=2\,\mathcal{F}\{e^{-t}u(t)\}+\mathcal{F}\{e^{-2t}u(t)\}
$$

Now replace each transform with the matching known pair. Keep the coefficient 2 attached to the first term, and keep the denominator tied to the decay rate in each causal exponential.

$$
H(j\omega)=2\cdot\frac{1}{1+j\omega}+\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

So the frequency response is the sum of the two transformed pieces, carried over directly from the known pairs. This is the compact closed form that later questions will vary by changing the coefficients or the standard exponential terms.

## Interpreting a Computed $H(j\omega)$ as Complex Gain

**Example:** If $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}$, interpret $H(j2)$ as the complex gain applied to the input $e^{j2t}$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the input $e^{j2t}$, so the input angular frequency is $\omega_0=2$. For a complex exponential, the system effect is read directly from the frequency response at that same frequency: use $H(j\omega_0)$.

$$
H(j2)=\frac{1}{2+j2}=\frac{1}{2+2j}
$$

To interpret it as a complex gain, put the result in a standard complex form. That makes the multiplier on the sinusoidal component explicit.

$$
H(j2)=\frac{1}{2+2j}\cdot\frac{2-2j}{2-2j}=\frac{1-j}{4}
$$

So the complex gain on $e^{j2t}$ is $\frac{1-j}{4}$. The output from this sinusoidal component is the same exponential multiplied by that factor.
