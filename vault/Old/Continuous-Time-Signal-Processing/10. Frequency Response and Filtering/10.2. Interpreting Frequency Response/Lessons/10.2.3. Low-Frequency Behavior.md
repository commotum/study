## Introduction to Low-Frequency Behavior

Low-frequency behavior means asking what the frequency response does near DC, so we examine $H(j\omega)$ as $\omega \to 0$. For slowly varying inputs, that neighborhood tells us the system's effective scaling and phase shift.

$$
\lim_{\omega \to 0} H(j\omega)
$$

The value at $\omega = 0$, written $H(j0)$, is the DC reference point. Its magnitude gives the low-frequency gain, and its angle gives the low-frequency phase shift.

$$
H(j0)
$$

In the simplest base case, if $H(j\omega)=K$ near $\omega=0$, then a slow sinusoid $A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ comes out approximately multiplied by $|K|$ and shifted by $\angle K$. If $K$ is negative, that phase shift includes an inversion.

$$
y(t) \approx |H(j0)|A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi + \angle H(j0))
$$

So the rule for the rest of the topic is fixed: read the limit at $\omega=0$, then interpret magnitude as scaling and phase as the shift applied to the slow part of the input.

## Evaluating the Low-Frequency Limit of $H(j\omega)$

**Example:** Given $H(j\omega)=\frac{2-j\omega}{4+j\omega}$, evaluate $\lim_{\omega\to 0}H(j\omega)$ and state the low-frequency gain and phase.

**Explanation**

The cue is the low-frequency limit, so we evaluate the transfer function at $\omega=0$. Because this rational expression is continuous there, direct substitution gives the DC value.

$$
\begin{aligned}\lim_{\omega \to 0} H(j\omega) &= \lim_{\omega \to 0} \frac{2 - j\omega}{4 + j\omega} \\ &= \frac{2 - j0}{4 + j0} \\ &= \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\end{aligned}
$$

So $H(j0)=\frac{1}{2}$. The low-frequency gain is $\frac{1}{2}$, and the low-frequency phase is $0$ because the limit is a positive real number.

$$
H(j0)=\frac{1}{2}, \qquad |H(j0)|=\frac{1}{2}, \qquad \angle H(j0)=0
$$

That means slowly varying input components are halved with no phase shift. For a slow sinusoid $A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$, the output is approximately $\frac{1}{2}A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$.

$$
y(t) \approx \frac{1}{2}A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)
$$

## Interpreting Low-Frequency Magnitude Scaling

**Example:** Suppose $H(j0)=\frac{1}{2}$. For a slowly varying sinusoidal input, decide whether the system attenuates, passes, or amplifies the component and by what factor.

**Explanation**

The cue is the low-frequency gain, so we read the magnitude of $H(j0)$ directly. Here $|H(j0)|=\frac{1}{2}$, and the key comparison is with 1.

$$
|H(j0)| = \frac{1}{2} < 1
$$

Since the magnitude is less than 1, the system attenuates the slowly varying sinusoidal component. The output amplitude is multiplied by $\frac{1}{2}$.

$$
y(t) \approx \frac{1}{2}A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)
$$

So the low-frequency magnitude scaling factor is $\frac{1}{2}$, and the classification is attenuation. In general, values below 1 attenuate, values equal to 1 pass, and values above 1 amplify.

## Interpreting Low-Frequency Phase Shift

**Example:** Suppose $H(j0)=-2$. For a slowly varying sinusoidal input, determine the low-frequency phase shift and describe the resulting output component.

**Explanation**

The cue is the low-frequency gain, and here the key feature is the negative sign in $H(j0)=-2$. A negative real constant has angle $\pi$, so the phase shift is an inversion rather than a delay.

$$
H(j0)=-2=2e^{j\pi}
$$

The magnitude is 2, so the slow sinusoid is doubled in amplitude, and the phase adds $\pi$. That means the output keeps the same frequency but flips sign relative to the input.

$$
y(t) \approx 2A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi+\pi) = -2A\cos(\omega_0 t+\phi)
$$

So the low-frequency phase shift is $\pi$: the component is inverted while its amplitude is scaled by 2. The sign of $H(j0)$ gives the phase classification directly.

## Deciding How Slowly Varying Inputs Change Through the System

**Example:** If $H(j0)=-\frac{3}{2}$ and $x(t)=A\cos(0.02t+\phi)$, describe the approximate output for this slowly varying component.

**Explanation**

The cue is the low-frequency limit, so we read the slow sinusoid through the constant value $H(j0)=-\frac{3}{2}$. The magnitude gives the scaling and the negative sign gives a phase shift of $\pi$.

$$
H(j0)=-\frac{3}{2}=\frac{3}{2}e^{j\pi}
$$

Apply both parts together: multiply the amplitude by $\frac{3}{2}$ and add $\pi$ to the sinusoid's phase. The output keeps the same slow frequency, but it is scaled up and inverted.

$$
y(t) \approx \frac{3}{2}A\cos(0.02t+\phi+\pi) = -\frac{3}{2}A\cos(0.02t+\phi)
$$

So the slowly varying input is amplified by $1.5$ and shifted by $\pi$. The combined low-frequency rule is to use the magnitude for scale and the sign for inversion.
