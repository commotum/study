## Introduction to Convolution and Frequency-Response Approaches

To find the output $y(t)$ of a continuous-time LTI system, first compare the two standard routes: direct convolution in time or multiplication in frequency.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)
$$

Use direct convolution when $x(t)$ and $h(t)$ are easy to overlap and the integral stays short. In the simple rectangle-through-rectangle base case from the outline, the overlap interval is short enough that the time-domain route is the cleaner first choice.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

Use frequency-response multiplication when $X(j\omega)$ and $H(j\omega)$ come from standard transform pairs or are easy to form by linearity. In that case, the product is usually shorter than writing out and simplifying a piecewise convolution.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)
$$

Compare overlap complexity, availability of transform pairs, and inverse-transform difficulty. Pick the route that reaches $y(t)$ with the least case-splitting and the cleanest final algebra.

<a id="choosing-direct-convolution-from-simple-overlap-bounds"></a>
## Choosing Direct Convolution from Simple Overlap Bounds

**Example:** Compute $y(t)$ for $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)$ and $h(t)=u(t)-u(t-2)$ by direct convolution; the overlap interval is short and the time-domain route is the cleanest choice.

**Explanation**

The cue here is simple: both $x(t)$ and $h(t)$ are finite-duration rectangles, so the overlap bounds are easy to track. That makes direct convolution the shortest path to $y(t)$.

$$
y(t)=x(t)*h(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

Since $x(\tau)=1$ on $0\le \tau<1$ and $h(t-\tau)=1$ when $t-2\le \tau<t$, the integrand is 1 only where those two intervals overlap. So the output is determined by the overlap of $[0,1]$ and $[t-2,t]$.

That overlap has length $t$ for $0\le t<1$, length $1$ for $1\le t<2$, and length $3-t$ for $2\le t<3$. Outside that range, the intervals do not overlap and the output is 0.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0,& t<0\\ t,& 0\le t<1\\ 1,& 1\le t<2\\ 3-t,& 2\le t<3\\ 0,& t\ge 3\end{cases}
$$

This is the direct-convolution advantage in the simple overlap case: the bounds alone determine the piecewise answer, and no frequency-domain work is needed.

## Choosing Frequency-Response Multiplication from Known Transform Pairs

**Example:** Compute $y(t)$ for $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)$ by multiplying the known transform pairs and inverting the product.

**Explanation**

The cue here is the causal exponential form: both $e^{-t}u(t)$ and $e^{-2t}u(t)$ are standard transform pairs, so frequency-response multiplication is the shorter path to $y(t)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}X(j\omega)&=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}\\H(j\omega)&=\frac{1}{2+j\omega}\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply the transform pairs to form $Y(j\omega)$. Because each factor already has a standard inverse pair, the frequency-domain route avoids the time-domain overlap integral entirely.

$$
Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{(1+j\omega)(2+j\omega)}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}-\frac{1}{2+j\omega}
$$

Now invert term by term using the standard causal exponential pairs. Keeping the $u(t)$ factors makes the time-domain output causal, which matches the original signals.

$$
y(t)=\big(e^{-t}-e^{-2t}\big)u(t)
$$

This is the frequency-response advantage in the known-pair case: the transform table and a short algebraic simplification give $y(t)$ faster than a direct convolution calculation.

## Choosing Frequency-Response Multiplication to Avoid Piecewise Convolution

**Example:** Compute $y(t)$ for $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)+u(t-2)-u(t-3)$ and $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ by using frequency-response multiplication rather than a multi-interval convolution.

**Explanation**

The cue is the repeated pulse structure in $x(t)$. A direct convolution would split into several overlap cases, so the shorter route is to rewrite the input as shifted copies of one base pulse and use linearity plus the shift property.

$$
p(t)=u(t)-u(t-1), \qquad x(t)=p(t)+p(t-2), \qquad P(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega}, \qquad X(j\omega)=P(j\omega)\big(1+e^{-j2\omega}\big)
$$

Now use the known frequency response of the causal exponential system. Because the same base pulse appears twice, the transform work stays compact and the shift factor carries straight through the product.

$$
H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}, \qquad Y(j\omega)=X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{(1-e^{-j\omega})(1+e^{-j2\omega})}{j\omega(1+j\omega)}
$$

Let $g(t)$ be the output for the single pulse $p(t)$. The factor $e^{-j2\omega}$ becomes a time shift, so the second pulse contributes a copy of the same response delayed by 2.

$$
g(t)=\big(1-e^{-t}\big)u(t)-\big(1-e^{-(t-1)}\big)u(t-1)
$$

$$
y(t)=g(t)+g(t-2)=\big(1-e^{-t}\big)u(t)-\big(1-e^{-(t-1)}\big)u(t-1)+\big(1-e^{-(t-2)}\big)u(t-2)-\big(1-e^{-(t-3)}\big)u(t-3)
$$

This is the frequency-response advantage in the multi-shift case: the repeated pulse train turns into one base response plus one shift, instead of several convolution intervals and piecewise overlap calculations.

## Choosing Direct Convolution to Avoid Difficult Inverse Transforms

**Example:** Compute $y(t)$ for $x(t)=u(t)-u(t-1)$ and $h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ by direct convolution because the time-domain integral is shorter than inverting $X(j\omega)H(j\omega)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the single rectangular pulse in $x(t)$. Here the time-domain route is shorter because the overlap is easy to track, while the frequency-domain product would still need more inverse-transform work.

$$
y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(u(\tau)-u(\tau-1)\big)e^{-(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)\,d\tau
$$

Because $x(\tau)$ is nonzero only on $0\le \tau<1$ and $h(t-\tau)$ is nonzero only when $\tau\le t$, the overlap changes only at $t=0$ and $t=1$. That gives one zero case and two short causal cases.

$$
\begin{aligned}y(t)&=0, && t<0\\y(t)&=\int_0^t e^{-(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=1-e^{-t}, && 0\le t<1\\y(t)&=\int_0^1 e^{-(t-\tau)}\,d\tau=e^{-t}(e-1), && t\ge 1\end{aligned}
$$

So the direct-convolution answer is piecewise only where the overlap changes. That is shorter than inverting $X(j\omega)H(j\omega)=\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega(1+j\omega)}$.

<a id="computing-yt-after-selecting-the-shorter-approach"></a>
## Computing $y(t)$ After Selecting the Shorter Approach

**Example:** For $H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}$ and $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)+u(t)-u(t-1)$, choose the shorter approach and compute $y(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the mixed input: one causal exponential and one shifted rectangle. Each piece has a standard transform pair, so frequency-response multiplication is the shorter path to $y(t)$.

$$
X(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}+\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega}, \qquad H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega}
$$

Use linearity to multiply each input component by $H(j\omega)$. That gives one squared-denominator term from the exponential piece and one shifted pulse-response term from the rectangle.

$$
Y(j\omega)=\frac{1}{(1+j\omega)^2}+\frac{1-e^{-j\omega}}{j\omega(1+j\omega)}
$$

Invert the two standard factors separately. The squared denominator gives $t e^{-t}u(t)$, and the pulse term is the same unit-pulse response from the previous step.

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}0,& t<0\\ 1+(t-1)e^{-t},& 0\le t<1\\ e^{-t}(t+e-1),& t\ge 1\end{cases}
$$

That finishes the capstone: the selection rule points to frequency-response multiplication, and the full output comes from combining the transformed pieces without setting up a separate time-domain convolution for each term.
