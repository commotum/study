## Introduction to Comparing RC Low-Pass and High-Pass Filters

This step teaches the endpoint-comparison rule for RC magnitude responses. Start with one component far below cutoff and one component far above cutoff, and use the shared normalized plot to compare the low- and high-frequency endpoints. Do not re-derive the circuits here; read the response trend and decide which component is passed more strongly.

![](<../Source/10.3.9. Comparing RC Low-Pass and High-Pass Filters/Images/l009-s001-te-section-001-rc-comparison-plot.png>)

At the low-frequency endpoint, the RC low-pass response is near $1$ and the RC high-pass response is near $0$. That means a slowly varying component is passed by the low-pass filter and attenuated by the high-pass filter.

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \to 1 \text{ as } \omega \to 0
$$

$$
|H_{HP}(j\omega)| \to 0 \text{ as } \omega \to 0
$$

At the high-frequency endpoint, the roles reverse. The RC low-pass response falls toward $0$ and the RC high-pass response rises toward $1$, so a rapidly varying component is attenuated by the low-pass filter and passed by the high-pass filter.

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \to 0 \text{ as } \omega \to \infty
$$

$$
|H_{HP}(j\omega)| \to 1 \text{ as } \omega \to \infty
$$

The reusable rule is to compare the endpoint magnitudes and read the larger magnitude as the component that passes more strongly. Later comparisons can reuse the same endpoint check to classify a filter or predict how a mixed signal will behave.

## Comparing Low-Frequency Behavior

**Example:** Compare the low-frequency behavior of the RC low-pass and RC high-pass magnitude responses at $\omega \ll 1/RC$, and decide which filter passes a slowly varying sinusoid.

**Explanation**

The cue is $\omega \ll 1/RC$, so this example sits at the low-frequency endpoint of the magnitude responses. Use the same comparison rule from the introduction: read the low-pass and high-pass magnitudes at that endpoint, not the circuit equations.

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \to 1 \text{ as } \omega \to 0
$$

$$
|H_{HP}(j\omega)| \to 0 \text{ as } \omega \to 0
$$

Because the low-pass magnitude is near $1$ and the high-pass magnitude is near $0$ at this endpoint, the RC low-pass filter passes the slowly varying sinusoid more strongly. The RC high-pass filter attenuates that same component.

The reusable rule is simple: at a given frequency endpoint, the larger magnitude is the filter that passes that component more strongly. For low frequency, that filter is the RC low-pass filter.

## Comparing High-Frequency Behavior

**Example:** Compare the high-frequency behavior of the RC low-pass and RC high-pass magnitude responses at $\omega \gg 1/RC$, and decide which filter passes a rapidly varying sinusoid.

**Explanation**

The cue is $\omega \gg 1/RC$, so use the high-frequency endpoint of the same comparison rule from the previous example, but flipped to the high-frequency side. At that endpoint, compare the two magnitude responses directly and decide which one is larger.

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \to 0 \text{ as } \omega \to \infty
$$

$$
|H_{HP}(j\omega)| \to 1 \text{ as } \omega \to \infty
$$

Because $|H_{HP}(j\omega)|$ is near $1$ while $|H_{LP}(j\omega)|$ is near $0$, the high-pass filter passes a rapidly varying sinusoid more strongly and the low-pass filter attenuates it.

Reusable rule: identify the frequency regime, compare the two magnitudes at that endpoint, and assign 'pass' to the larger response and 'attenuate' to the smaller one.

## Identifying Filter Type from a Magnitude Response

**Example:** From the given magnitude-response plot, identify whether the filter is RC low-pass or RC high-pass by comparing the low-frequency and high-frequency ends of the curve.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.3.9. Comparing RC Low-Pass and High-Pass Filters/Images/l009-s004-te-section-004-magnitude-response-plot.png>)

Read the two ends of the curve first. The left end is high and the right end is low, so the visual trend matches the low-pass pattern rather than the high-pass pattern.

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \approx 1 \text{ for } \omega \ll 1/RC
$$

$$
|H_{LP}(j\omega)| \approx 0 \text{ for } \omega \gg 1/RC
$$

That endpoint behavior means the filter passes low frequencies and attenuates high frequencies, so the plot identifies an RC low-pass filter.

Reusable rule: use the low-frequency and high-frequency ends of the response, not the cutoff marker alone. High-left and low-right means low-pass; low-left and high-right means high-pass.

<a id="predicting-passed-and-attenuated-frequency-components"></a>
## Predicting Passed and Attenuated Frequency Components

**Example:** A signal has components at $100$ Hz and $10$ kHz; compare what an RC low-pass filter and an RC high-pass filter do to each component.

**Explanation**

The cue is the frequency split. Put the $100$ Hz component on the low-frequency side and the $10$ kHz component on the high-frequency side, then apply the same endpoint rule from the earlier examples.

$$
100 \text{ Hz} \rightarrow \text{passed by RC low-pass}, \quad 10 \text{ kHz} \rightarrow \text{attenuated by RC low-pass}
$$

$$
100 \text{ Hz} \rightarrow \text{attenuated by RC high-pass}, \quad 10 \text{ kHz} \rightarrow \text{passed by RC high-pass}
$$

So the low-pass filter keeps the low-frequency component and suppresses the high-frequency component, while the high-pass filter does the opposite.

Reusable rule: sort the components by frequency relative to cutoff, then assign the lower-frequency components to pass through the low-pass filter and the higher-frequency components to pass through the high-pass filter.
