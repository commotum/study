## Introduction to Effect of $RC$ on Filter Behavior

When you see an RC low-pass or high-pass filter, read the product $RC$ as the controlling quantity for cutoff location. The key idea is that changing $RC$ moves where the magnitude response turns.

$$
\omega_c = \frac{1}{RC}
$$

$$
f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}
$$

Because $\omega_c$ and $f_c$ are inversely proportional to $RC$, a larger $RC$ lowers the cutoff and a smaller $RC$ raises it. So the magnitude-response transition shifts to lower frequency when $RC$ increases and to higher frequency when $RC$ decreases.

This same cutoff-setting rule applies to both RC low-pass and RC high-pass filters, so later examples can use one inverse relationship to predict how the transition moves in either response.

<a id="identifying-rc-as-the-cutoff-setting-quantity"></a>
## Identifying $RC$ as the Cutoff-Setting Quantity

**Example:** Two RC low-pass filters have $(R,C) = (1\,\text{k}\Omega, 1\,\mu\text{F})$ and $(R,C) = (2\,\text{k}\Omega, 0.5\,\mu\text{F})$. Determine whether they have the same cutoff frequency and identify the controlling quantity.

**Explanation**

The cue is the product $RC$: the cutoff depends on $RC$ itself, not on $R$ or $C$ alone. Compare the two filters by computing each product first.

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1C_1 &= (1\,\text{k}\Omega)(1\,\mu\text{F}) = 10^{-3}\,\text{s} \\
R_2C_2 &= (2\,\text{k}\Omega)(0.5\,\mu\text{F}) = 10^{-3}\,\text{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the products match, substituting into $\omega_c = 1/RC$ gives the same cutoff frequency for both filters. Equal $RC$ means equal cutoff location.

So the controlling quantity is $RC$. When $RC$ changes, the magnitude-response transition moves; when $RC$ stays the same, the transition stays in the same place.

<a id="predicting-a-low-pass-magnitude-response-after-changing-rc"></a>
## Predicting a Low-Pass Magnitude Response After Changing $RC$

**Example:** A low-pass filter has cutoff $\omega_c = 1/RC$. If $RC$ doubles, determine the new cutoff frequency and state how the magnitude-response transition shifts relative to the original curve.

**Explanation**

The cue is the inverse cutoff rule: for a low-pass RC filter, $RC$ sets the cutoff location. If $RC$ doubles, the new cutoff must move in the opposite direction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_c' &= \frac{1}{2RC} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{RC} \\
&= \frac{\omega_c}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the new cutoff frequency is half the original cutoff frequency. The magnitude-response transition shifts to lower frequency, which means the bend in the low-pass curve moves left on the frequency axis.

The controlling quantity is still $RC$: changing $R$ or $C$ matters only through their product. When that product gets larger, the cutoff gets smaller and the transition moves left; when the product gets smaller, the cutoff gets larger and the transition moves right.

## Predicting a High-Pass Magnitude Response After Changing $RC$

**Example:** A high-pass filter has cutoff $\omega_c = 1/RC$. If $RC$ doubles, determine the new cutoff frequency and say whether a frequency just below the original cutoff is closer to or farther from the passband.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same inverse cutoff rule, but now we read it on a high-pass curve. For a high-pass RC filter, larger $RC$ still lowers the cutoff, so the transition moves to a lower frequency.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_c' &= \frac{1}{2RC} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{RC} \\
&= \frac{\omega_c}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the new cutoff frequency is half the original cutoff frequency. A frequency just below the original cutoff is now above the new cutoff, so it is closer to the passband and less attenuated than before.

The controlling quantity is still $RC$: changing $R$ or $C$ matters only through their product. The rule is the same for both RC filter types; the only difference is which side of the cutoff counts as the passband.

<a id="comparing-magnitude-response-transitions-for-different-rc-values"></a>
## Comparing Magnitude-Response Transitions for Different $RC$ Values

**Example:** Two RC filters satisfy $RC_1 < RC_2$. Compare their cutoff frequencies and magnitude-response transition locations for both the low-pass and high-pass responses.

**Explanation**

The cue is the ordering of the two $RC$ values. Since $\omega_c = 1/RC$, the larger product gives the lower cutoff frequency.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\omega_{c,1} &= \frac{1}{RC_1} \\
\omega_{c,2} &= \frac{1}{RC_2} \\
RC_1 < RC_2 &\Rightarrow \omega_{c,1} > \omega_{c,2}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the filter with $RC_2$ has the lower cutoff frequency. For the low-pass response, its magnitude transition occurs at the lower frequency, so that curve is shifted farther left than the curve with $RC_1$.

The same ordering holds for the high-pass response. The filter with $RC_2$ begins its transition at the lower frequency, so its rising edge is also shifted farther left.

The shared conclusion is that larger $RC$ lowers the cutoff and moves the magnitude-response transition to lower frequency for both RC filter types. The low-pass and high-pass curves differ in shape, but they follow the same cutoff-ordering rule.
