## Introduction to Low-Pass Differential Equation

In the standard RC low-pass circuit, use $x(t)$ for the source input and $y(t)$ for the capacitor-voltage output. The resistor and capacitor lie in one series branch, so the same branch current flows through both elements.

$$
i_R(t)=i_C(t)
$$

That branch current can be written from the resistor's voltage drop. Since the resistor sees the input voltage minus the output voltage, Ohm's law gives the resistor current as the drop divided by $R$.

$$
i_R(t)=\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R}
$$

The capacitor current uses the output voltage itself. With the output measured across the capacitor, the capacitor law gives current equal to $C$ times the time derivative of $y(t)$.

$$
i_C(t)=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}
$$

Equating those two branch-current expressions is the derivation step that produces the low-pass differential equation. Rearranging gives the standard first-order form used in the next step.

$$
RC\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=x(t)
$$

## Identifying the Low-Pass Input and Capacitor-Voltage Output

**Example:** A series resistor feeds a shunt capacitor to ground. The source is labeled $u(t)$ and the capacitor voltage is labeled $v_C(t)$. Identify the system input and the system output.

**Explanation**

Look at the source-side label $u(t)$ and the voltage marked across the capacitor, $v_C(t)$. In the RC low-pass model, the source voltage is the input and the capacitor voltage is the output.

$$
x(t)=u(t),\qquad y(t)=v_C(t)
$$

So the correct identification is input $u(t)$, or $x(t)$ in the lesson notation, and output $v_C(t)$, or $y(t)$. Keep that pairing fixed for the rest of the derivation.

Do not replace the output with the resistor voltage or any branch current. Those are internal circuit quantities that will appear later in the equations, but they are not the system input-output pair.

## Writing the Resistor Current from the Input-Output Voltage Difference

**Example:** For the RC low-pass circuit with input $x(t)$ and output $y(t)$, write the resistor current in terms of the voltage across the series resistor.

**Explanation**

The cue is the voltage drop across the series resistor. Its left end is at the input node $x(t)$, and its right end is at the output node $y(t)$. With current flowing from input to output, the resistor drop is $x(t)-y(t)$.

$$
v_R(t)=x(t)-y(t)
$$

Now apply Ohm's law to the resistor. The branch current equals the voltage across the resistor divided by $R$, using the same sign convention that the next step will keep for the capacitor branch.

$$
i_R(t)=\frac{v_R(t)}{R}=\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R}
$$

So the resistor current is $i_R(t)=\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R}$. Keep that branch direction fixed, because the capacitor-current step will match it exactly.

## Writing the Capacitor Current from the Output-Voltage Derivative

**Example:** For the same RC low-pass circuit, write the capacitor current in terms of the capacitor voltage $y(t)$ and capacitance $C$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the voltage across the capacitor, which we already fixed as $y(t)$. For a capacitor, the current is determined by the time derivative of that same voltage, using the same branch-current direction as the resistor current.

$$
i_C(t)=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}
$$

So the capacitor current is $i_C(t)=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}$. The point to keep is simple: resistor current used the voltage drop, while capacitor current uses the output-voltage derivative.

Do not replace the derivative with the voltage itself, and do not invert the factor of $C$. This step isolates the capacitor law so the next example can combine it with the resistor current.

<a id="equating-the-resistor-and-capacitor-currents"></a>
## Equating the Resistor and Capacitor Currents

**Example:** Combine the resistor and capacitor current formulas for the same RC low-pass branch and write the equality that follows from the series connection.

**Explanation**

The cue is the series branch: the resistor and capacitor sit on the same current path, so one branch current flows through both elements with the same direction.

$$
i_R(t)=\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R},\qquad i_C(t)=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}
$$

Because the branch is in series, set the two currents equal rather than adding them.

$$
\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R}=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}
$$

That equality is the raw low-pass differential equation before rearrangement. Keep this form for the next step, where it will be rewritten into standard first-order form.

<a id="rewriting-the-low-pass-differential-equation-in-standard-first-order-form"></a>
## Rewriting the Low-Pass Differential Equation in Standard First-Order Form

**Example:** Starting from $\frac{x(t)-y(t)}{R}=C\frac{dy(t)}{dt}$, rewrite the equation in standard first-order form.

**Explanation**

The cue is the current-equality equation from the previous step. The goal now is only algebraic cleanup: clear the denominator, isolate the derivative term, and expose the time constant $RC$.

$$
x(t)-y(t)=RC\frac{dy(t)}{dt}
$$

Move the output term to the left and leave the input term on the right. That gives the standard low-pass form with the derivative term multiplied by $RC$.

$$
RC\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+y(t)=x(t)
$$

The same equation can also be normalized by dividing through by $RC$. Either form is equivalent, but both keep the same input-output relationship.

$$
\frac{dy(t)}{dt}+\frac{1}{RC}y(t)=\frac{1}{RC}x(t)
$$

This closes the derivation: the RC low-pass circuit is now in standard first-order differential-equation form.
