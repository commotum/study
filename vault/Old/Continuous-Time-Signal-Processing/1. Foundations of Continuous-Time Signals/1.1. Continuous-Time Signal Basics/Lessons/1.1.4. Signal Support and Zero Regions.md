## Introduction to Signal Support and Zero Regions

A continuous-time signal is a function of time. At each time $t$, the signal value $x(t)$ is either zero or nonzero. Support and zero regions come from marking those two possibilities over intervals of time.

The support of a signal is the time region where the signal is nonzero. To identify support, scan along the time axis and mark the intervals where $x(t)\ne 0$.

$$
\operatorname{support of } x(t): \{t : x(t) \ne 0\}
$$

A zero region is an interval where the signal is identically zero. This is stronger than saying the signal equals zero at one isolated time; every time in the interval must have value $0$.

$$
\text{zero region: an interval on which } x(t)=0 \text{ for every } t
$$

![](<../Source/1.1.4. Signal Support and Zero Regions/Images/l004-s001-te-section-001.png>)

In the picture, the signal is nonzero only on the middle interval. That interval is the support. The flat parts on the time axis before and after the support are zero regions because $x(t)=0$ for every time in those intervals.

The repeatable procedure is: first find where $x(t)\ne 0$ and write those intervals as the support. Then write the intervals where $x(t)=0$ throughout as the zero regions. The same procedure applies whether the signal is shown by a graph, a piecewise definition, or a formula.

## Identifying Support from a Graph

**Example:** The graph of $x(t)$ is shown. Identify the support of $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.1.4. Signal Support and Zero Regions/Images/l004-s002-te-section-002.png>)

The cue for support is where the graph is off the time axis. In this graph, the signal is zero before $t=-2$, then it becomes nonzero at $t=-2$.

The graph stays nonzero until $t=3$, and after $t=3$ it returns to the time axis. So the nonzero part starts at $-2$ and ends at $3$.

$$
\operatorname{support of } x(t)=[-2,3]
$$

The support is only the interval where $x(t)\ne 0$. Times outside $[-2,3]$ are not part of the support because the graph lies on the time axis there.

## Identifying Zero Regions from a Graph

**Example:** The graph of $x(t)$ is shown. Identify the zero regions of $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.1.4. Signal Support and Zero Regions/Images/l004-s003-te-section-003.png>)

First locate the nonzero part of the graph so it does not get included in a zero region. Here, the graph is above the time axis only between $t=-1$ and $t=2$.

A zero region is an interval where $x(t)=0$ for every time in that interval. The graph lies on the time axis for all times up to $-1$, and it lies on the time axis again for all times from $2$ onward.

$$
\text{zero regions: } (-\infty,-1]\cup[2,\infty)
$$

The interval $(-1,2)$ is not a zero region because the graph is nonzero there. The zero regions are the outside intervals where the graph stays on $x(t)=0$ throughout.

## Finding Support and Zero Regions from a Piecewise Definition

**Example:** For the piecewise signal with $x(t)=0$ for $t<-1$, $x(t)=2$ for $-1\le t<2$, $x(t)=0$ for $2\le t<4$, $x(t)=-1$ for $4\le t\le 5$, and $x(t)=0$ for $t>5$, identify the support and the zero regions.

**Explanation**

The cue is that each row of the piecewise definition gives a signal value together with the time interval where that value applies. Rows with value $0$ contribute to zero regions, and rows with a nonzero value contribute to the support.

The nonzero rows are $x(t)=2$ on $-1\le t<2$ and $x(t)=-1$ on $4\le t\le 5$. Combining those intervals gives the support.

$$
\operatorname{support of }x(t)=[-1,2)\cup[4,5]
$$

The zero-valued rows are $x(t)=0$ on $t<-1$, on $2\le t<4$, and on $t>5$. Combining those intervals gives the zero regions.

$$
\text{zero regions: }(-\infty,-1)\cup[2,4)\cup(5,\infty)
$$

The endpoint symbols come directly from the original inequalities. For example, $-1$ belongs to the nonzero row, so it is included in the support, while $2$ belongs to the zero row, so it is included in a zero region.

<a id="finding-support-and-zero-regions-from-a-formula"></a>
## Finding Support and Zero Regions from a Formula

**Example:** A signal is given by $x(t)=2e^{-t}$ for $0\le t\le 4$ and $x(t)=0$ otherwise. Identify the support and the zero regions of $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the formula is active only on the stated interval. Here, $x(t)=2e^{-t}$ applies for $0\le t\le 4$, and the signal is defined to be $0$ otherwise.

Next check whether the active formula is actually nonzero on that interval. Since $e^{-t}>0$ for every real $t$, the product $2e^{-t}$ is also positive for every $t$ from $0$ to $4$.

$$
x(t)\ne 0 \quad \text{for }0\le t\le 4
$$

Because the signal is nonzero throughout the active interval, that interval is the support.

$$
\operatorname{support of }x(t)=[0,4]
$$

The phrase $x(t)=0$ otherwise means every time outside the active interval belongs to a zero region.

$$
\text{zero regions: }(-\infty,0)\cup(4,\infty)
$$

The endpoints $0$ and $4$ are included in the support because the formula applies at both endpoints and gives nonzero values there. The zero regions are open at those endpoints because $x(0)\ne 0$ and $x(4)\ne 0$.

<a id="distinguishing-isolated-zeros-from-zero-regions"></a>
## Distinguishing Isolated Zeros from Zero Regions

**Example:** A signal is given by $x(t)=t$ for $-2\le t\le 2$ and $x(t)=0$ otherwise. Identify the zero regions, and decide whether $t=0$ is a zero region.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same compact formula format as before: one formula is active on a stated interval, and the signal is zero otherwise. Here, $x(t)=t$ is active for $-2\le t\le 2$, so the otherwise-zero parts are outside that interval.

$$
\text{outside zero regions: }(-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)
$$

Now check whether the active formula itself ever equals zero. On the active interval, solving $x(t)=0$ means solving $t=0$.

$$
x(t)=t=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad t=0
$$

The time $t=0$ is a single zero value inside the active interval. It is not a zero region because a zero region must be an interval where $x(t)=0$ for every time in the interval. Near $0$, values such as $x(0.5)=0.5$ and $x(-0.5)=-0.5$ are not zero.

$$
t=0\text{ is an isolated zero, not a zero region.}
$$

So the zero regions are only the outside intervals where the signal is identically zero. Within the active interval, the nonzero portions are everything except the isolated zero at $t=0$.

$$
\text{nonzero portions: }[-2,0)\cup(0,2]
$$
