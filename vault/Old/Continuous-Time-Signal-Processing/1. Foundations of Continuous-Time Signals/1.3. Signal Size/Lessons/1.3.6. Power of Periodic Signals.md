## Introduction to Power of Periodic Signals

For a periodic signal $x(t)$ with fundamental period $T_0$, the cue is repetition: $x(t+T_0)=x(t)$. Periodic-signal power is the average value of $|x(t)|^2$ over that repeating pattern.

$$
x(t+T_0)=x(t)
$$

Start from the average-power definition from the previous topic. For a periodic signal, the long-time average can be built from repeated copies of one full period, so the simplest picture is a signal that repeats the same level every $T_0$ seconds.

$$
P=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Because each period is the same, the repeated copies cancel in the average. What remains is one fundamental-period average, so power can be computed from any interval of length $T_0$.

$$
P=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

The starting point $t_0$ does not matter as long as the interval spans exactly one fundamental period. That is the shortcut the later examples will reuse.

## Converting Average Power to a One-Period Integral

**Example:** For a $T_0$-periodic signal $x(t)$, show how the average-power limit reduces to a one-period integral over $[t_0,t_0+T_0]$.

**Explanation**

For a $T_0$-periodic signal $x(t)$, the cue is repetition: $x(t+T_0)=x(t)$. Start from the average-power limit from the previous section, because the goal is to replace that long-time average with a one-period average.

$$
P=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Now partition the long interval into whole periods. Because every block of length $T_0$ has the same squared-magnitude pattern, each block contributes the same one-period integral, so the repetition count cancels when you divide by the total interval length.

$$
P=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T_0}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

That is the one-period shortcut: once the signal is periodic, power is determined by any interval of length $T_0$. The starting point $t_0$ can be anywhere inside a fundamental-period window.

<a id="computing-power-from-a-given-fundamental-period"></a>
## Computing Power from a Given Fundamental Period

**Example:** Compute the power of the periodic signal $x(t)=2$ for $0\le t<\tfrac{T_0}{2}$ and $0$ for $\tfrac{T_0}{2}\le t<T_0$, repeated every $T_0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the one-period formula from the previous step. Because the signal is already given over one fundamental period and then repeated, compute power by integrating $|x(t)|^2$ over $[0,T_0]$ and dividing by $T_0$.

$$
P=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_0^{T_0}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

On $0\le t<\tfrac{T_0}{2}$, the signal equals $2$, so $|x(t)|^2=4$. On $\tfrac{T_0}{2}\le t<T_0$, the signal is $0$, so the squared magnitude is also $0$. Split the integral at the point where the waveform changes value.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\frac{1}{T_0}\left(\int_0^{T_0/2}4\,dt+\int_{T_0/2}^{T_0}0\,dt\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{T_0}\left(4\cdot\frac{T_0}{2}\right)\\
&=2
\end{aligned}
$$

The result is $P=2$. The only new work here is recognizing which subintervals are active and which contribute zero; the one-period power formula does the rest.

<a id="choosing-convenient-bounds-over-one-period"></a>
## Choosing Convenient Bounds Over One Period

**Example:** A $2$-periodic signal is defined on one convenient period by $x(t)=1$ for $-1\le t<0$ and $x(t)=3$ for $0\le t<1$. Compute its power by integrating over the shifted one-period interval $[-1,1]$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that any interval of length $T_0$ works. Here the shifted choice $[-1,1]$ is convenient because it matches the piecewise definition exactly, so each constant level stays on its own subinterval.

$$
P=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Split at the breakpoint $t=0$. On $-1\le t<0$, $x(t)=1$, so $|x(t)|^2=1$. On $0\le t<1$, $x(t)=3$, so $|x(t)|^2=9$. The shift changes the bounds, not the power.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{-1}^{0}1\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}9\,dt\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}(1+9)\\
&=5
\end{aligned}
$$

So the power is $P=5$. The practical rule is simple: pick any one-period interval, then choose the bounds that make the pieces easiest to integrate.

<a id="computing-power-of-a-piecewise-periodic-signal"></a>
## Computing Power of a Piecewise Periodic Signal

**Example:** Compute the power of the periodic signal $x(t)=1$ for $0\le t<1$, $x(t)=-2$ for $1\le t<3$, and $x(t)=0$ for $3\le t<4$, repeated every $4$ units.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same one-period formula, but now the period has three constant pieces. The job is to square each level, integrate on each subinterval, and add the contributions over the full period.

$$
P=\frac{1}{4}\int_0^4|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

On $0\le t<1$, the signal is $1$, so the squared value is $1$. On $1\le t<3$, the signal is $-2$, so the squared value is $4$. On $3\le t<4$, the signal is $0$, so that piece contributes nothing.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_0^1 1\,dt+\int_1^3 4\,dt+\int_3^4 0\,dt\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{4}(1+8+0)\\
&=\frac{9}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the power is $P=\tfrac{9}{4}$. The only bookkeeping is to keep each piece on its own interval, square the levels first, and then average over the full period.
