## Introduction to Signal Energy

Signal energy is the total accumulated size of a signal, not its average size. When the whole waveform matters, start by recognizing the instantaneous power built from $x(t)$.

$$
p(t)=|x(t)|^2
$$

Once you have $p(t)$, compute signal energy by integrating that power over all time to get one total number, $E$, for the signal.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

This same definition classifies signals: if the integral is finite, the signal has finite energy, and if the integral is infinite, it does not. The finite-versus-infinite total is the signal-size test used later in the module.

$$
E=\int_0^T A^2\,dt=A^2T
$$

For a constant pulse of amplitude $A$ on a finite interval from $0$ to $T$, the definition collapses to a concrete total: the energy is proportional to the pulse height squared and its duration. That is the simplest base case for the idea.

## Converting Instantaneous Power into an Energy Integral

**Example:** Given $x(t)=3$ for $0\le t<2$ and $0$ otherwise, write the total energy as an integral of $|x(t)|^2$ and simplify the integrand.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-duration pulse: it is constant on its support and zero elsewhere. For energy, keep the nonzero interval and use the squared magnitude as the integrand.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^2 |3|^2\,dt
$$

Because the signal equals $3$ on that interval, the instantaneous power is constant and equals $|3|^2=9$. The limits stay at the nonzero interval because the signal is zero everywhere else.

$$
E=\int_0^2 9\,dt
$$

That is the correct energy setup: square the magnitude, restrict the integral to the support, and leave the definite integral unevaluated for now.

## Evaluating a Finite Energy Integral

**Example:** Compute the total energy of $x(t)=2$ for $0\le t<3$ and $0$ otherwise.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-duration constant pulse, so use the all-time energy definition $E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt$ and restrict it to the nonzero interval.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^3 |2|^2\,dt
$$

Because the amplitude is constant, the integrand is the constant power level $|2|^2=4$ on $0\le t<3$.

$$
E=\int_0^3 4\,dt=12
$$

The total energy is $12$. This is the finished scalar value for the signal's size.

## Identifying Infinite Signal Energy

**Example:** Determine whether $x(t)=\cos(2\pi t)$ has finite or infinite energy, and justify the answer from the energy integral.

**Explanation**

The cue is a persistent periodic signal, so start from the all-time energy definition $E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt$ and check whether the integrand keeps contributing for every time.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos^2(2\pi t)\,dt
$$

$\cos^2(2\pi t)$ is nonnegative and repeats forever, so the signal never stops adding positive area to the energy integral. Bounded amplitude only keeps $|x(t)|$ small; it does not make the total energy finite.

$$
E=\infty
$$

So this signal has infinite energy.

## Comparing Signals by Total Energy

**Example:** Compare the total energies of $x_1(t)=2$ for $0\le t<2$ and $x_2(t)=1$ for $0\le t<5$, and state which is larger.

**Explanation**

The cue is a side-by-side comparison, so compute each signal's total energy with the same definition and compare the resulting scalars.

$$
E_1=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x_1(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^2 |2|^2\,dt=8
$$

For $x_2(t)$, the amplitude is smaller but the signal lasts longer, so square the amplitude and integrate over its full support.

$$
E_2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x_2(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^5 |1|^2\,dt=5
$$

Since $E_1=8$ and $E_2=5$, $x_1(t)$ has the larger total energy. The result shows that total energy depends on both amplitude squared and duration.
