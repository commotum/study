## Introduction to Computing Energy of Finite-Duration Signals

A finite-duration signal is one that is zero outside a finite support interval, so the energy rule stays the same but the integral only matters where the signal is nonzero. The quantity to recognize is total energy, and the action to perform is to integrate the squared magnitude over the signal's support.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Because the signal is zero outside its support, the zero regions contribute nothing to the total. That is why a finite-duration signal can be handled by replacing the all-time integral with an integral over the nonzero interval only.

$$
E=\int_{a}^{b}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

For the base case, take a constant pulse $x(t)=A$ on $[a,b]$ and $0$ elsewhere. On the support, $|x(t)|^2=|A|^2$, so the energy becomes a constant times the support length.

$$
x(t)=A\text{ on }[a,b]\quad\Rightarrow\quad E=|A|^2(b-a)
$$

Later examples keep this same support-limited procedure and only change the shape of the signal inside the nonzero interval. The first step is always the same: find the support, square the magnitude, and integrate only over that interval.

## Computing Energy of a Constant Finite-Duration Signal

**Example:** Compute the energy of $x(t)=2$ for $-1 \le t \le 2$, and $0$ otherwise.

**Explanation**

The cue is a constant signal with finite support, so the energy comes from the support-limited integral of the squared magnitude. Use the same rule from the tutorial: identify the nonzero interval first, then integrate only on that interval.

$$
E=\int_{-1}^{2}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_{-1}^{2}|2|^2\,dt
$$

Here the support is $[-1,2]$, and the constant magnitude is $|2|$. Since the signal is zero elsewhere, the integrand is $4$ across the entire support, so the only remaining job is to multiply by the interval length.

$$
E=\int_{-1}^{2}4\,dt=4\bigl(2-(-1)\bigr)=12
$$

So the total energy is $12$. The new wrinkle is only the shifted support; the procedure does not change. Find the support, square the magnitude, and evaluate the definite integral over that interval.

## Computing Energy of a Nonconstant Finite-Duration Signal

**Example:** Compute the energy of $x(t)=2t$ for $0 \le t \le 1$, and $0$ otherwise.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-duration signal whose value changes with time, so the energy still comes from integrating $|x(t)|^2$ only over the support. The only new wrinkle is that the signal is no longer constant inside that interval, so the integrand becomes a polynomial after squaring.

$$
E=\int_0^1 |2t|^2\,dt
$$

Because the signal is zero outside $[0,1]$, no other time values contribute to the total. Squaring the magnitude gives $|2t|^2=4t^2$, so the remaining step is a standard definite integral on the support.

$$
E=\int_0^1 4t^2\,dt=4\left[\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac{4}{3}
$$

So the total energy is $\frac{4}{3}$. The support-limited procedure stays the same as before: find the nonzero interval, square the signal magnitude, and evaluate the resulting integral on that interval.

## Computing Energy of a Piecewise Finite-Duration Signal

**Example:** Compute the energy of $x(t)=1$ for $0 \le t < 1$, $x(t)=2$ for $1 \le t \le 3$, and $0$ otherwise.

**Explanation**

The cue is a finite-duration signal whose formula changes across its support, so the energy must be split at the breakpoint. Use the same rule as before: identify the active intervals, square the magnitude on each interval, and add the interval energies.

$$
E=\int_0^1 |1|^2\,dt+\int_1^3 |2|^2\,dt
$$

On $[0,1)$, the signal value is $1$, so $|x(t)|^2=1$. On $[1,3]$, the signal value is $2$, so $|x(t)|^2=4$. Outside those intervals, the signal is zero, so there is no energy contribution there.

$$
E=\int_0^1 1\,dt+\int_1^3 4\,dt=1+8=9
$$

So the total energy is $9$. The new wrinkle is the internal breakpoint: split the support into pieces, square each piece separately, and add the results.

## Computing Energy of a Finite-Duration Signal from a Graph

**Example:** Compute the energy of the finite-duration signal shown in the graph: a triangular pulse that is zero outside $0 \le t \le 2$, rises linearly from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$, and falls linearly back to $(2,0)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is a graph of a finite-duration signal, so first read the support and the breakpoints from the plot. Then convert each visible segment into a formula and apply the same support-limited energy rule as before.

![](<../Source/1.3.3. Computing Energy of Finite-Duration Signals/Images/l003-s005-te-section-005-graph.png>)

From the graph, the support is $[0,2]$. The rising segment goes from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$, so its formula is $x(t)=2t$ on $0 \le t \le 1$. The falling segment goes from $(1,2)$ to $(2,0)$, so its formula is $x(t)=-2t+4$ on $1 \le t \le 2$.

$$
E=\int_0^1 |2t|^2\,dt+\int_1^2 |-2t+4|^2\,dt
$$

Now square each segment and integrate on its own interval. The signal is zero outside $[0,2]$, so nothing else contributes to the total energy.

$$
E=\int_0^1 4t^2\,dt+\int_1^2 (4t^2-16t+16)\,dt=\frac{4}{3}+\frac{4}{3}=\frac{8}{3}
$$

So the energy is $\frac{8}{3}$. The new wrinkle is that the graph hides the formulas, so the first job is to read support, breakpoints, and heights before applying the same piecewise integration procedure.
