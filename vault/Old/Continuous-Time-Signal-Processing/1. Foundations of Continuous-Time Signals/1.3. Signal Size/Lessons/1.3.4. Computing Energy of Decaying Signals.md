## Introduction to Computing Energy of Decaying Signals

Energy is still defined the same way for a decaying signal: integrate $|x(t)|^2$ over all time. The point is to keep the same definition while reading the support first, because a decaying signal usually lives on a semi-infinite interval.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Use $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$ with $a>0$ as the base case. The unit step makes the signal zero for $t<0$ and leaves the exponential tail on $[0,\infty)$.

Once the support is known, square the magnitude and collapse the full-time integral to the tail on the signal's support. That is the only new move here: convert the whole-line energy integral into an improper integral over the nonzero part.

$$
E=\int_0^\infty e^{-2at}\,dt=\frac{1}{2a},\qquad a>0
$$

The integral converges because $a>0$ makes the exponential decay on the support. If the exponent does not decay, the same energy setup does not produce a finite result.

The same pattern will keep working when the signal starts later or is scaled by a constant: find the support, square the magnitude, and integrate the tail from the correct lower limit.

$$
E=\int_{t_0}^\infty |A|^2 e^{-2a(t-t_0)}\,dt=\frac{|A|^2}{2a},\qquad a>0
$$

## Energy of a One-Sided Decaying Exponential

**Example:** Find the total energy of $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit step: $u(t)$ makes $x(t)=e^{-t}u(t)$ zero for $t<0$, so the signal lives on $[0,\infty)$. Energy still uses the same definition, so start with the full-time integral and then restrict it to the support.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty |e^{-t}|^2\,dt
$$

Now square the magnitude. Since $e^{-t}$ is real-valued, $|e^{-t}|^2=e^{-2t}$, so the energy becomes a convergent improper integral on the one-sided support.

$$
E=\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2t}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{1}{2}
$$

The result is finite because the exponential tail decays on its support. This is the pattern to reuse: read the support from the step function, square the magnitude, and integrate only the nonzero part.

## Energy of a Scaled Decaying Exponential

**Example:** Find the total energy of $x(t)=3e^{-t}u(t)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is still the unit step: $u(t)$ makes $x(t)=3e^{-t}u(t)$ zero for $t<0$, so the signal lives on $[0,\infty)$. The only new wrinkle is the amplitude $3$, which changes the magnitude but not the support.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty |3e^{-t}|^2\,dt
$$

Square the amplitude before integrating. Since $|3e^{-t}|^2=9e^{-2t}$, the energy becomes the same convergent improper integral as before, multiplied by the squared amplitude.

$$
E=9\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt=9\left[-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2t}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{9}{2}
$$

The result is finite because the exponential tail still decays on its support. The lesson to reuse is simple: read the support from $u(t)$, square the whole magnitude, and let the amplitude factor carry through as a square.

## Energy of a Decaying Exponential with a Nonzero Start Time

**Example:** Find the total energy of $x(t)=e^{-(t-3)}u(t-3)$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the unit step $u(t-3)$, so the signal is zero for $t<3$. That means the energy integral starts at $3$, not at $0$.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_3^\infty \left|e^{-(t-3)}\right|^2\,dt
$$

Squaring the magnitude keeps the same support and turns the integrand into $e^{-2(t-3)}$.

$$
E=\int_3^\infty e^{-2(t-3)}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2(t-3)}\right]_3^\infty=\frac{1}{2}
$$

The shift changes only the lower limit. The decay still makes the improper integral converge, so the total energy is $\frac{1}{2}$.

<a id="checking-convergence-of-a-decaying-signal-energy-integral"></a>
## Checking Convergence of a Decaying-Signal Energy Integral

**Example:** For which values of $a$ does $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$ have finite energy, and what is the energy when it converges?

**Explanation**

The unit step $u(t)$ fixes the support to $t\ge 0$, so the energy test starts on $[0,\infty)$.

$$
E=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt=\int_0^\infty e^{-2at}\,dt
$$

Now inspect the exponent in $e^{-2at}$. If $a>0$, the integrand decays and the improper integral can converge. If $a=0$, the integrand stays at $1$. If $a<0$, it grows, so the energy diverges.

$$
E=\int_0^\infty e^{-2at}\,dt=\left[-\frac{1}{2a}e^{-2at}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{1}{2a}\qquad (a>0)
$$

So the convergence decision comes first: $x(t)$ has finite energy exactly when $a>0$, and then $E=\frac{1}{2a}$. For $a\le 0$, the energy is infinite.
