## Introduction to Average Signal Power

Average signal power is the long-run measure of sustained signal size. For a continuous-time signal $x(t)$, we define it by averaging the squared magnitude over a symmetric window that grows without bound.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

The symmetric interval $[-T,T]$ matters because the average should reflect long-time behavior on both sides of the reference time, not accumulation over a finite window. Squaring $|x(t)|$ makes the quantity nonnegative and measures size rather than sign.

In the constant-signal base case $x(t)=A$, the squared magnitude is the same at every time, so the average does not change with the window length. That makes the result easy to read: a steady nonzero signal has average power $|A|^2$.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = |A|^2 \quad \text{when } x(t)=A
$$

This is why average power is different from total energy: it describes sustained size, not total accumulated area. Signals that only exist for a finite time or decay to zero will later collapse to zero average power, while steady periodic signals can keep a nonzero average power.

## Computing Average Power of a Constant Signal

**Example:** Compute the average signal power of the constant signal $x(t)=A$.

**Explanation**

The cue here is that $x(t)$ never changes with time. That means $|x(t)|^2$ is also constant, so the average-power definition should collapse to that same constant value.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

Substitute the constant signal $x(t)=A$. Then $|x(t)|^2=|A|^2$, and because that quantity does not depend on $t$, it can be pulled through the integral.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}|A|^2\int_{-T}^{T}dt
$$

The remaining integral is just the length of the interval $[-T,T]$, which is $2T$.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}|A|^2(2T) = |A|^2
$$

So the average signal power of a constant signal is the squared magnitude of the constant: $P_{\text{avg}}=|A|^2$. This is the simplest example of a sustained signal whose power stays nonzero over time.

<a id="computing-zero-average-power-for-a-finite-duration-signal"></a>
## Computing Zero Average Power for a Finite-Duration Signal

**Example:** Compute the average signal power of the finite-duration pulse $x(t)=A$ for $|t|\le T_0$ and $x(t)=0$ otherwise.

**Explanation**

The cue is finite support: this pulse is nonzero only on $[-T_0,T_0]$. That means the numerator in the average-power formula stays fixed while the averaging window keeps growing.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|x(t)|^2\,dt
$$

For any $T>T_0$, the signal is zero outside $[-T_0,T_0]$, so only the finite support interval contributes to the integral.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T_0}^{T_0}|A|^2\,dt = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{2T_0|A|^2}{2T}
$$

The numerator is a fixed finite number, but the denominator grows without bound. So the long-time average is driven to zero.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = 0
$$

This is the key contrast with a constant signal: a finite-duration pulse can have finite energy, but it has zero average power because its nonzero part gets diluted by the growing window.

<a id="computing-average-power-of-a-decaying-signal"></a>
## Computing Average Power of a Decaying Signal

**Example:** Compute the average signal power of the decaying signal $x(t)=e^{-a|t|}$ with $a>0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is the even decaying envelope. Since $x(t)=e^{-a|t|}$, the squared magnitude is $|x(t)|^2=e^{-2a|t|}$, which is still symmetric about $t=0$.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{-2a|t|}\,dt
$$

Use even symmetry to fold the integral onto $[0,T]$ and double it.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\cdot 2\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2at}\,dt = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2at}\,dt
$$

Now evaluate the exponential integral exactly. The result stays bounded while the averaging window length keeps growing.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{T}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-2aT}}{2a} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1-e^{-2aT}}{2aT} = 0
$$

So the decaying signal has zero average power. Its squared magnitude is integrable, but the long-time average still vanishes because the fixed numerator is divided by an unbounded window.

<a id="distinguishing-average-power-from-total-energy"></a>
## Distinguishing Average Power from Total Energy

**Example:** For the signal $x(t)=e^{-a|t|}$ with $a>0$, compute its total energy and its average signal power, then state which quantity is finite.

**Explanation**

The cue is the same even decaying signal, but now the target is a comparison. Compute the total-energy integral first, then apply the long-time average to the same $|x(t)|^2$.

$$
E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|^2\,dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2a|t|}\,dt = 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2at}\,dt = \frac{1}{a}
$$

The energy integral converges, so the total energy is finite.

$$
P_{\text{avg}} = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}e^{-2a|t|}\,dt = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1}{2T}\cdot 2\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2at}\,dt = \lim_{T \to \infty}\frac{1-e^{-2aT}}{2aT} = 0
$$

So the finite quantity is total energy, not average power. This signal has bounded total accumulation, but its long-time average power is zero because the fixed energy is spread over an unbounded window.
