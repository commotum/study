## Introduction to Converting Between Period and Angular Frequency

For a periodic continuous-time signal, $T_0$ is the time for one full repeat, measured in seconds, and $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency of that same repeat, measured in radians per second. Use $T_0$ when the problem is asking for a time period and use $\omega_0$ when it is asking for an angular-rate description.

$$
[T_0] = \text{s}, \quad [\omega_0] = \text{rad/s}
$$

The two quantities are tied together because one full cycle is $2\pi$ radians. That gives one identity and two equivalent conversion formulas, and they all describe the same repetition in different units.

$$
\omega_0 T_0 = 2\pi
$$

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T_0}
$$

$$
T_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}
$$

For the simplest base case, one full repeat that lasts $T_0 = 1\text{ s}$ corresponds to $\omega_0 = 2\pi\text{ rad/s}$, and the same relation works in reverse: $\omega_0 = 2\pi\text{ rad/s}$ corresponds to $T_0 = 1\text{ s}$. Keep both directions in mind, because later problems may give either quantity.

$$
\begin{aligned}T_0 &= 1\text{ s} \;\Rightarrow\; \omega_0 = 2\pi\text{ rad/s} \\[4pt]\omega_0 &= 2\pi\text{ rad/s} \;\Rightarrow\; T_0 = 1\text{ s}\end{aligned}
$$

This conversion changes only the representation of the repetition. It does not change the underlying signal, so later steps can move back and forth between period and angular frequency as needed.

## Converting a Period to an Angular Frequency

**Example:** A periodic signal has $T_0 = 4\text{ s}$. Find $\omega_0$ and state the units.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the period is already known in seconds, and the target is the angular frequency in radians per second. Use the conversion rule $\omega_0 = \dfrac{2\pi}{T_0}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}\omega_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{T_0} \\[4pt]&= \frac{2\pi}{4\text{ s}} \\[4pt]&= \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad/s}\end{aligned}
$$

So the angular frequency is $\omega_0 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad/s}$. The result is smaller than $2\pi\text{ rad/s}$ because a longer period means fewer radians per second for one full repeat.

## Converting an Angular Frequency to a Period

**Example:** A periodic signal has $\omega_0 = \frac{3\pi}{2}\text{ rad/s}$. Find $T_0$.

**Explanation**

The cue is that the angular frequency is already known in radians per second, and the target is the period in seconds. Use the inverse conversion rule $T_0 = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}T_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} \\[4pt]&= \frac{2\pi}{\frac{3\pi}{2}} \\[4pt]&= \frac{4}{3}\text{ s}\end{aligned}
$$

So the period is $T_0 = \dfrac{4}{3}\text{ s}$. The unit is seconds because the angular-frequency unit is inverted in the formula, so the result measures time for one full repeat.

<a id="interpreting-converted-period-and-angular-frequency-values"></a>
## Interpreting Converted Period and Angular Frequency Values

**Example:** A periodic signal has $T_0 = 2\text{ s}$ and $\omega_0 = \pi\text{ rad/s}$. Decide whether the pair is consistent and interpret each value.

**Explanation**

The cue is a proposed $T_0$/$\omega_0$ pair. Check consistency by converting one value with the period-frequency rule and comparing the result in the other units.

$$
\begin{aligned}\omega_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{T_0} = \frac{2\pi}{2\text{ s}} = \pi\text{ rad/s} \\[6pt]T_0 &= \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0} = \frac{2\pi}{\pi}\text{ s} = 2\text{ s}\end{aligned}
$$

The pair is consistent. $T_0 = 2\text{ s}$ means one full repeat takes 2 seconds, and $\omega_0 = \pi\text{ rad/s}$ means the phase advances by $\pi$ radians each second, not $\pi$ cycles per second.
