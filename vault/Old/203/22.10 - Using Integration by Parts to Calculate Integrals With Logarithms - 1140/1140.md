## Introduction

Suppose that we want to evaluate the integral

$$
\int x\ln{x}\, \textrm{d}x \,
$$

The method of integration by parts can help us solve this problem. Let's remind ourselves of the "by-parts" formula,

$$
\int u v' \textrm{d}x = uv - \int v u'\textrm{d}x
$$

We usually let $u = x$, but that would leave us with $v' = \ln(x)$ which is hard to integrate. So instead, we're going to choose our $u$ and $v'$ slightly differently, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \ln x & ⟹u^{′} = \frac{1}{x} \\ v^{′} = x & ⟹v = \frac{1}{2}x^{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now the formula of integration by parts gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x\ln x\,\textrm{d}x &= \int \underbrace{\ln x}_{u}\cdot \underbrace{x}_{v^{\prime}}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= \ln x \cdot \frac{1}{2}x^{2} - \int\frac{1}{2}x^{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= \frac{1}{2}x^{2}\ln x - \frac{1}{2}\int x\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= \frac{1}{2}x^{2}\ln x - \frac{1}{4}x^{2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

When we apply integration by parts to integrals containing logarithmic functions, we should *always* identify the logarithmic function with $u$, not with $v'$.

## Using Integration by Parts to Compute an Integral Containing a Natural Logarithm

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_{1}^{e} 4x\ln\left(x^3\right) \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

We will solve this problem using integration by parts. We start by defining $u$ and $v'$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \ln (x^{3}) & ⟹u^{′} = \frac{1}{x^{3}} \cdot 3x^{2} = \frac{3}{x} \\ v^{′} = 4x & ⟹v = \int 4x\,\textrm{d}x = 2x^{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the formula of integration by parts, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{1}^{e}4x\ln (x^{3})dx &= \int_{1}^{e}uv^{′}dx \\
&= \left.uv\right|_{1}^{e} - \int_{1}^{e}vu^{′}dx \\
&= \ln (x^{3}) \cdot \left.(2x^{2})\right|_{1}^{e} - \int_{1}^{e}(2x^{2}) \cdot \frac{3}{x}dx \\
&= [2(e)^{2}\ln (e^{3}) - 2(1)^{2}\ln (1^{3})] - 6\int_{1}^{e}xdx \\
&= 6e^{2} - \left.6(\frac{1}{2}x^{2})\right|_{1}^{e} \\
&= 6e^{2} - \left.3x^{2}\right|_{1}^{e} \\
&= 6e^{2} - 3(e^{2} - 1^{2}) \\
&= 3e^{2} + 3
\end{aligned}
$$

## Using Integration by Parts to Compute an Integral Containing an Arbitrary Logarithm

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{\log_4{x}}{x^3} \,\textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

We define

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \log_{4} x & ⟹u^{′} = \frac{1}{x\ln 4} \\ v^{′} = \frac{1}{x^{3}} & ⟹v = \int\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx =-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the formula of integration by parts, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{\log_{4} x}{x^{3}}dx &= \int uv^{′}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= uv - \int vu^{′}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= \log_{4} x(-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}) - \int(-\frac{1}{2x^{2}})\frac{1}{x\ln 4}dx \\
&=-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}\log_{4} x + \frac{1}{2\ln 4}\int\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx \\
&=-\frac{\ln x}{2x^{2}\ln 4} + \frac{1}{2\ln 4}(-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}) + C \\
&=-\frac{\ln x}{2x^{2}\ln 4} - \frac{1}{4x^{2}\ln 4} + C \\
&=-\frac{2\ln x + 1}{4x^{2}\ln 4} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that in the above calculation we used the change of base formula

$$
\log_4{x}=\dfrac{\ln{x}}{\ln{4}}
$$

## Using Integration by Parts to Compute an Integral Containing a Single Logarithmic Factor

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \ln{x}\,\textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

At first, it may not look like we have two factors. Actually, we do, but one of them is implicit. The trick is to write the integral as

$$
\int \ln{x}\,\textrm{d}x = \int \ln{x} \cdot 1 \cdot \textrm{d}x
$$

Using this trick, we can now use integration by parts. We let

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \ln x & ⟹u^{′} = \frac{1}{x} \\ v^{′} = 1 & ⟹v = x\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, using the by-parts formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \ln x\,\textrm{d}x &= x\ln x - \int x \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= x\ln x - \int 1\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= x\ln x - x + C \\
&= x(\ln x - 1) + C
\end{aligned}
$$
