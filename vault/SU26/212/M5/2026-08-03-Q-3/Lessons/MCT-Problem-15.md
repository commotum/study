## Introduction

For sound moving through still air, write the Doppler formula with observer motion in the numerator and source motion in the denominator:

$$
\boxed{
f_o=f_s\frac{v\pm v_o}{v\mp v_s}
}.
$$

The symbols have separate roles:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $f_s$ | frequency emitted by the source |
| $f_o$ | frequency detected by the observer |
| $v$ | speed of sound in the medium |
| $v_o$ | observer's speed relative to the medium |
| $v_s$ | source's speed relative to the medium |

Treat $v_o$ and $v_s$ as nonnegative speed magnitudes. The operation chosen beside each speed comes from the physical motion:

| Moving object | Motion | Frequency effect | Factor to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| observer | toward source | raises $f_o$ | numerator $v+v_o$ |
| observer | away from source | lowers $f_o$ | numerator $v-v_o$ |
| source | toward observer | raises $f_o$ | denominator $v-v_s$ |
| source | away from observer | lowers $f_o$ | denominator $v+v_s$ |

This is the sign logic in the first source segment (`WiTQxNaKAYA`, 00:05:02–00:15:51). The observer changes how quickly wavefronts are encountered, so $v_o$ belongs in the numerator. The source changes the spacing of the emitted wavefronts, so $v_s$ belongs in the denominator. Source and observer speeds cannot be exchanged even when their numerical values match.

Use this order on every case:

1. Identify which object is the source and which is the observer.
2. For each moving object, decide whether it approaches or separates from the other object.
3. Predict the direction of the shift before choosing signs: approaching raises frequency; separating lowers it.
4. Choose each numerator or denominator operation from the table.
5. Set the speed of any stationary object equal to zero.
6. Check that the calculated $f_o$ lies on the predicted side of $f_s$.

If neither the sound speed nor the air temperature is supplied, use

$$
v\approx343\ \mathrm{m/s}.
$$

When a Celsius temperature is given, the video's estimate is $v\approx331+0.6T$. The autogenerated transcript garbles the $0.6\ \mathrm{m/s}$-per-degree coefficient; the formula and its $20^\circ\mathrm C\mapsto343\ \mathrm{m/s}$ example fix the intended value.

The video also relates signs to left- and right-pointing arrows after placing the observer on one particular side of its drawing. That shortcut changes when the drawing is reversed. The toward/away prediction above does not.

## Source-Video Problem 1: Moving Source

In the first numerical source case (`WiTQxNaKAYA`, 00:15:54–00:21:06), an ambulance emits

$$
f_s=800\ \mathrm{Hz}
$$

and moves at $v_s=30\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The observer is stationary, so $v_o=0$, and $v=343\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

**Example:** Find the detected frequency when the ambulance moves (a) toward and (b) away from the observer.

**Explanation**

For motion toward the observer, predict $f_o>800\ \mathrm{Hz}$. A source approaching belongs in the smaller denominator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=800\frac{343+0}{343-30}\\
&=876.677\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{877\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For motion away, predict $f_o<800\ \mathrm{Hz}$. A receding source makes the denominator larger:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=800\frac{343+0}{343+30}\\
&=735.657\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{736\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The observer did not move in either part, so its numerator contribution is zero. Only the source term changes sign.

## Source-Video Problem 2: Moving Observer

The next source case (`WiTQxNaKAYA`, 00:21:08–00:25:54) keeps the ambulance stationary at

$$
f_s=1200\ \mathrm{Hz}
$$

while the observer moves at $v_o=25\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Here $v_s=0$.

**Example:** Find the frequency detected while the observer drives (a) toward and (b) away from the ambulance.

**Explanation**

Approaching raises the wavefront encounter rate, so add the observer speed in the numerator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=1200\frac{343+25}{343-0}\\
&=1287.464\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{1287\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Moving away lowers the encounter rate, so subtract the observer speed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=1200\frac{343-25}{343-0}\\
&=1112.536\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{1113\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Source correction.** During the away calculation, the narration briefly calls the negative term $v_s$ and immediately corrects it to $v_o$. The ambulance is stationary, so $v_s=0$ throughout; the moving observer supplies $343-25$ in the numerator.

## Source-Video Problem 3: Source and Observer Both Move

The final source-video case (`WiTQxNaKAYA`, 00:25:59–00:30:18) has

$$
f_s=900\ \mathrm{Hz},
\qquad
v_s=20\ \mathrm{m/s},
\qquad
v_o=25\ \mathrm{m/s}.
$$

The police car is the source. It moves west toward a driver, while the driver moves east toward the police car. Both motions are approaching motions, so both must raise the detected frequency: add $v_o$ in the numerator and subtract $v_s$ in the denominator.

| Role | Object | Motion | Doppler term |
|---|---|---|---|
| source | police car | toward observer | denominator $343-20$ |
| observer | driver | toward source | numerator $343+25$ |

**Example:** Find the frequency detected by the driver.

**Explanation**

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=900\frac{343+25}{343-20}\\
&=1025.387\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{1025\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The ratio $368/323$ is greater than $1$, so the result agrees with the prediction $f_o>900\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The east/west labels locate the objects, but toward/away decides the signs.

## Lecture Transfers: Bats as Observer and Source

The M5-3 lecture uses a bat first as an observer and then as a source. The role determines which part of the fraction changes.

**Example 1:** A bat flies toward a stationary singer at $v_o=35\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The singer emits $f_s=880\ \mathrm{Hz}$. What does the bat hear?

**Explanation**

The bat is an approaching observer, so its speed raises the numerator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=880\frac{343+35}{343}\\
&=969.796\ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}\\
&\approx\boxed{970\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Example 2:** A bat emits $25\ \mathrm{kHz}$. How must it move for a stationary human to detect $20\ \mathrm{kHz}$, and what source speed would be required?

**Explanation**

The detected frequency must be lower than the emitted frequency, so the bat source must fly away. Use the source-away denominator and solve before substituting:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_o
&=f_s\frac{v}{v+v_s},\\
f_o(v+v_s)
&=f_sv,\\
v_s
&=v\left(\frac{f_s}{f_o}-1\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Now substitute the two frequencies in the same unit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_s
&=343\left(\frac{25}{20}-1\right)\\
&=85.75\ \mathrm{m/s}\\
&\approx\boxed{86\ \mathrm{m/s}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The kilohertz unit cancels in the frequency ratio. The lecture notes convert this speed to about $190\ \mathrm{mph}$, far above an ordinary bat's flight speed, so the shift is mathematically possible but not physically plausible for a bat.

<a id="summary"></a>
## Summary

For a sound Doppler problem:

1. Label the source and observer before assigning either speed.
2. Predict whether each motion raises or lowers the detected frequency.
3. Put observer motion in the numerator: toward means $v+v_o$, away means $v-v_o$.
4. Put source motion in the denominator: toward means $v-v_s$, away means $v+v_s$.
5. Set a stationary object's speed to zero and use $v\approx343\ \mathrm{m/s}$ when no temperature is supplied.
6. Check the result against the prediction: approaching motion should raise $f_o$, while separating motion should lower it.

A moving observer changes the wavefront encounter rate; a moving source changes wavefront spacing. If a speed is unknown, choose its role and sign before rearranging the equation. Keeping those roles separate is more reliable than memorizing signs tied to a particular drawing.
