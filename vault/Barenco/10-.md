## What Deutsch–Hayden does and does not establish

Deutsch–Hayden shows that the Schrödinger and Heisenberg formulations encode the same quantum predictions, and that the Heisenberg descriptors allow the causal history of information to be represented locally.

It does **not** show that the exponentially large quantum description is the smallest possible description of the actual physical state.

Indeed, generic explicit Deutsch–Hayden descriptors still carry exponential mathematical content. Each descriptor is an operator on a $2^n$-dimensional Hilbert space. Their result concerns locality of evolution and information flow, not efficient compression into a fixed number of physical variables per qubit.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Schrödinger–Heisenberg equivalence is not a minimality theorem.}
}
$$

It does not rule out another representation in which:

- one actual state requires only $O(n)$ local variables;
- the variables evolve through local interactions;
- the ensemble of such trajectories reproduces the quantum distributions.

## The correct role of $e^{i\chi}|0\rangle$

Within standard quantum mechanics,

$$
e^{i\chi}|0\rangle
$$

cannot produce different subsequent behavior from $|0\rangle$, because for every ordinary unitary $U$,

$$
U(e^{i\chi}|0\rangle)
=
e^{i\chi}U|0\rangle,
$$

and for every ordinary measurement effect $E$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E\mid e^{i\chi}|0\rangle)
&=
\langle0|e^{-i\chi}E e^{i\chi}|0\rangle \\
&=
\langle0|E|0\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

The phase remains a common factor forever under linear quantum evolution.

Therefore, merely refusing to identify the conventional kets does not yet make $\chi$ physically effective. The new theory must give $\chi$ a new dynamical or measurement role.

The clean notation would be something like

$$
\lambda_\chi=(|0\rangle,\chi),
\qquad
\pi(\lambda_\chi)=[|0\rangle],
$$

rather than treating $e^{i\chi}|0\rangle$ as an ordinary quantum state with the ordinary rules.

For one qubit, standard normalized spinors form $S^3$, and conventional pure states are obtained by quotienting out the phase:

$$
S^3/U(1)\cong S^2.
$$

Your proposal retains the $U(1)$ coordinate as physical rather than treating it as gauge.

For several qubits, the change is even more substantial. Standard tensor composition gives

$$
\bigotimes_{j=1}^n e^{i\chi_j}|\psi_j\rangle
=
e^{i\sum_j\chi_j}
\bigotimes_{j=1}^n|\psi_j\rangle,
$$

so all the individual phases collapse into one overall phase. A phase-resolved theory must therefore replace or extend the ordinary tensor-product composition rule so that the local variables

$$
\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_n
$$

remain individually present and can participate in later local interactions.

A physically grounded version might regard each $\chi_j$ as phase relative to some local physical clock, oscillator, field, or internal reference—not as a bare absolute phase with no reference.

The architecture would allow:

$$

while probabilities arise from uncertainty or uncontrolled variability in

\omega_0\sim\mu_{0^n}.

The ensemble distribution would evolve deterministically by pushforward:

$$
\mu_t=(F_t)_*\mu_0.
$$

There would be no physical collapse of the ensemble. Upon learning an outcome, an observer might condition the ensemble description, while the actual system had followed one trajectory throughout.

That is closely analogous to classical statistical mechanics:

$$
\text{actual phase-space point }x_t
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{Liouville distribution }\mu_t(x).

Your proposal is that the quantum state may play a role more like the second object than the first, while the missing phase-resolved variables specify the first.

Nothing in Schrödinger–Heisenberg equivalence alone disproves that.

## Where the exponential information might go

There is an important distinction between:

$$
\boxed{\text{size of one actual microstate}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{size of the complete statistical description of all preparations and measurements.}}
$$

One actual sample from a complicated distribution can be small even when describing the entire distribution is enormous.

For example, an $n$-bit sample requires only $n$ bits, while an arbitrary probability distribution over the $2^n$ possible samples requires

$$
2^n-1
$$

independent probabilities.

Likewise, an actual phase-resolved state

$$
(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)
$$

could require only $O(n)$ variables, while its joint preparation distribution

$$
\mu(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)
$$

could contain exponential or even function-valued information.

This matters because the manifold of pure $n$-qubit quantum states has real dimension

$$
\dim\mathbb{CP}^{2^n-1}
=
2^{n+1}-2.
$$

By contrast, if each local physical state has a fixed dimension $d$, then one actual $n$-qubit microstate has dimension only

$$
nd.
$$

Consequently, a smooth one-to-one representation of every arbitrary quantum pure state by one fixed-dimensional local microstate is impossible once

$$
nd<2^{n+1}-2.
$$

But your proposal does **not** require such a one-to-one map. It can represent each operational quantum state by a different distribution over the $nd$-dimensional microstate space:

$$
\rho
\longleftrightarrow
\mu_\rho(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$

The space of probability distributions over a low-dimensional state space is itself infinite-dimensional, so it can in principle encode the exponentially large family of quantum states.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{an } O(n)\text{-sized actual state is mathematically compatible with} \\
\text{an exponentially rich family of ensemble distributions.}
\end{gathered}
}
$$

What has not automatically been achieved is an $O(n)$-sized description of the entire ensemble. The complexity may have moved into:

- the joint distribution $\mu_\rho$;
- the preparation history;
- the gate sequence;
- correlations among the local phases;
- the required precision of each continuous variable;
- the measurement response rule.

That distinction does not undermine the ontology. It only prevents us from equating “linear-size actual state” with “efficient classical calculation of every quantum probability.”

A single real number can set-theoretically encode exponentially many amplitudes in its digits. So a meaningful claim of linear scaling also needs physical regularity conditions: bounded local dimension, reasonable precision, continuous or computable update rules, and genuinely local access.

## The Bell issue is separate from the compression issue

The proposed $O(n)$ representation is not excluded because it is $O(n)$, and it is not excluded because it retains phases that standard quantum mechanics removes.

But once it supplies one actual outcome at each side of a Bell experiment, its outcome rule has to be examined separately.

If its Bell experiment has the form

$$
\omega\sim\mu,
$$

$$
A=A(\mathbf{a},\lambda_A),
\qquad
B=B(\mathbf{b},\lambda_B),
$$

with

$$
\mu(\omega\mid\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\mu(\omega),
$$

then the hidden phases are simply components of the complete physical state $\omega$. The resulting joint distribution has the form

$$
P(A,B\mid\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
=
\int
\mu(d\omega)\,
P_A(A\mid\mathbf{a},\omega)
P_B(B\mid\mathbf{b},\omega).
$$

That particular positive, setting-independent local pushforward obeys Bell inequalities regardless of whether $\omega$ contains:

- one phase per qubit;
- infinitely precise phases;
- electromagnetic fields;
- local clocks;
- the entire preparation history.

So the compression proposal and the Bell constraint must be kept distinct:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Bell does not rule out a linear-sized phase-resolved actual state.}
}
$$

It rules out one particular way of converting such states into unique local outcomes while retaining the other Bell assumptions.

A successful phase-resolved theory reproducing the Bell distributions would therefore have to show explicitly why its outcome-generation rule is not represented by the integral above. That could happen because the preparation measure, causal structure, outcome map, or notion of completeness differs from the ordinary form. But the failure would need to be located mathematically.
