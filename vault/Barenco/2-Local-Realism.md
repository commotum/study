# Local Realism

## Realism

Realism is the idea that there exists a world outside of our immediate subjective experience and that the state of this world determines the outcome of all observations.

This outside world can be called the real world, the external world, the objective world, or in Kant's terminology: the noumenal world [7,11]. In our terminology, we will call it: the physical world. It describes the world as it is rather than the world as it can be observed, or known through sensory experience.

According to realism, our subjective experience, our perceptions, our sense-data, are determined by the state of the external world. The portion of the real world that is observable or perceptible is called the perceptive world, or alternatively the observable world, or in Kant's terminology: the phenomenal world. We will use the same terminology as Kant here.

## Observability

To be perceptible does not mean to be perceived directly. If we scan a molecule with an atomic force microscope, the properties thus observed are perceptible even though we are not observing the molecule directly with our naked eyes. The only limit to what kind of measurement device may be used to determine what is perceptible are the laws of Nature, not merely the currently available technology.

Also, to be perceptible does not imply to be perceived right now by some observer. For instance, the far side of the Moon existed as part of the phenomenal world even before we had the technology that allowed us to observe it. Thus, we include as part of the perceptible world, not what is perceived now, but rather potential perceptions.

## The Projection

A physical state determines a phenomenal state through a projection $\pi$. For a closed system, this projection must be compatible with evolution: if the physical state evolves by $U$, then its phenomenal projection evolves by the corresponding phenomenal action.

This is well-defined because two physical states that give rise to the same phenomenal state cannot later give rise to different phenomenal states under the same transformation. If they did, an experiment beginning with $U$ could distinguish the original states. Thus:

$$
\text { Phenomenal }_2=U \cdot \text { Phenomenal }_1 .
$$

The same $\mathrm{Phenomenal}_2$ can therefore be reached in two ways: evolve $\mathrm{Physical}_1$ and then project, or project first and then evolve $\mathrm{Phenomenal}_1$.

This parallel process can be represented by the following commuting diagram:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\mathrm{Physical}_1 & \xrightarrow{\;\;U\;\;} & \mathrm{Physical}_2 \\[0.5em]
{\scriptstyle \pi}\Big\downarrow & & \Big\downarrow{\scriptstyle \pi} \\[0.05em]
\mathrm{Phenomenal}_1 & \xrightarrow{\;\;U\;\;} & \mathrm{Phenomenal}_2
\end{array}
$$

This commuting diagram states that **the evolution of the phenomenal consequences of the physical world are the phenomenal consequences of the evolution of the physical world.**

Mathematically $\pi$ is a homomorphism that verifies:

$$
\pi\left(U \star \text { Physical }_1\right)=U \cdot \pi\left(\text { Physical }_1\right) .
$$

The evolution of the phenomenal world is an epiphenomenon: Understanding how the physical world evolves and the relation between the physical world and the phenomenal world is sufficient to describe the evolution of the phenomenal world.
