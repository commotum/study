## Review of Oscillations

### 2. Oscillation vs. Wave
- An **oscillation** is repeated motion around an equilibrium position.
- A **wave** is a disturbance or oscillation that propagates through space.
- A wave can often be viewed as a system of many coupled oscillators.

---

### 3. Simple Pendulum Review
- For a simple pendulum under the small-angle approximation:
  $$ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} $$
- Its frequency is:
  $$ f=\frac{1}{T}
     =\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} $$
- Here:
  - $L$ is the string length
  - $g$ is gravitational acceleration

---

### 4. Effect of Pendulum Length
- From:
  $$ f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} $$
- A shorter pendulum has a higher frequency.
- A longer pendulum has a lower frequency.
- The pendulum mass does not appear in the frequency expression.

---

### 5. Restoring Force for a Pendulum
- Gravity provides the restoring force for a pendulum.
- When the pendulum is displaced from equilibrium, a component of gravity pulls it back toward the bottom position.

---

### 6. Stable Equilibrium
- An equilibrium is **stable** if a small displacement produces a restoring force toward equilibrium.
- Example:
  - a marble placed at the bottom of a bowl
- If displaced slightly, it oscillates around the equilibrium position.

---

### 7. Unstable Equilibrium
- An equilibrium is **unstable** if a small displacement causes the object to move farther away.
- Example:
  - a marble balanced on top of an overturned bowl
- A slight displacement causes the marble to roll away rather than return.

---

### 8. Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion
- Simple harmonic motion can be represented by a sine or cosine function.
- It can also be understood as the projection of uniform circular motion onto one axis.
- A point moving at constant speed around a circle produces sinusoidal motion when viewed from the side.

---

## Mass–Spring Oscillations

### 9. Mass–Spring Frequency
- For an ideal mass–spring oscillator:
  $$ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} $$
- Ordinary frequency:
  $$ f=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}
     =\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} $$
- Period:
  $$ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} $$
- Here:
  - $k$ is the spring constant
  - $m$ is the oscillating mass

---

### 10. Comparing Two Masses on Identical Springs
- Two identical springs carry different masses:
  - one mass is $100\ \mathrm{g}$
  - one mass is $50\ \mathrm{g}$
- Since:
  $$ f\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} $$
- The smaller mass has the higher frequency:
  $$ f_{50\ \mathrm{g}}>f_{100\ \mathrm{g}} $$

---

### 11. Physical Interpretation of Mass
- The mass is the inertial part of the oscillator.
- A larger mass is harder to accelerate back toward equilibrium.
- Therefore, increasing $m$ reduces the oscillation frequency.

---

### 12. Physical Interpretation of Spring Stiffness
- The spring constant controls the strength of the restoring force:
  $$ F_s=-kx $$
- A larger $k$ means a stronger restoring force for the same displacement.
- Therefore:
  $$ f\propto\sqrt{k} $$
- A stiffer spring produces a higher oscillation frequency.

---

### 13. Restoring Force for a Vertical Spring
- The spring force provides the restoring force around equilibrium.
- Gravity acts downward at all times and shifts the equilibrium position.
- At equilibrium:
  $$ kx_{\mathrm{eq}}=mg $$
- Once displacement is measured relative to equilibrium, the oscillation frequency remains:
  $$ \omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} $$

---

## Introduction to Waves

### 14. A Wave as Coupled Oscillators
- A mechanical wave can be viewed as many neighboring particles oscillating and interacting.
- Each particle moves around its own equilibrium position.
- The disturbance travels through the medium even though the individual particles do not travel with the disturbance.

---

### 15. Wave Pulse
- A single disturbance traveling through a medium is called a **wave pulse**.
- Example:
  - one upward or downward displacement sent along a string
- The pulse propagates while each part of the string moves only temporarily away from equilibrium.

---

### 16. Periodic Wave
- If the source oscillates repeatedly, it produces a periodic wave.
- The source frequency determines how frequently successive crests and troughs are generated.

---

## Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

### 17. Transverse Wave
- In a transverse wave, particle displacement is perpendicular to the propagation direction.
- Example:
  - wave traveling horizontally along a string
  - particles in the string move vertically
- Symbolically:
  $$ \text{particle motion}\perp\text{wave propagation} $$

---

### 18. Longitudinal Wave
- In a longitudinal wave, particle displacement is parallel to the propagation direction.
- Example:
  - sound wave traveling through air
- Air particles move back and forth in the same direction that the wave travels:
  $$ \text{particle motion}\parallel\text{wave propagation} $$

---

### 19. Compressions and Rarefactions
- A longitudinal sound wave contains alternating regions of:
  - compression, where particles are closer together
  - rarefaction, where particles are farther apart
- The particles oscillate locally while the pattern of compression and rarefaction travels through the medium.

---

### 20. Particle Motion vs. Wave Motion
- A particle in a transverse string wave does not move along the string with the wave.
- The particle remains at approximately the same horizontal position and oscillates vertically.
- The wave pattern moves horizontally through the string.

---

## Reflection and Superposition

### 21. Reflection at a Fixed End
- When a wave pulse reaches a fixed boundary, it reflects.
- The reflected pulse is inverted:
  - an upward pulse returns as a downward pulse
  - a downward pulse returns as an upward pulse

---

### 22. Superposition
- When two waves overlap, their displacements add:
  $$ y_{\mathrm{net}}=y_1+y_2 $$
- A right-moving wave and its reflected left-moving wave can overlap and interfere.

---

### 23. Standing-Wave Preview
- Repeated incident and reflected waves can create a standing-wave pattern.
- Standing waves will be studied later in connection with:
  - strings
  - musical instruments
  - normal modes

---

## Restoring Force and Wave Speed on a String

### 24. Restoring Force for a String Wave
- Tension provides the restoring force for particles in a string.
- If a portion of the string is displaced upward, the vertical components of tension pull it downward toward equilibrium.
- If displaced downward, the tension components pull it upward.

---

### 25. Linear Mass Density
- The linear mass density of a string is:
  $$ \mu=\frac{m_{\mathrm{string}}}{L} $$
- Units:
  $$ [\mu]=\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m} $$

---

### 26. Wave Speed on a String
- The wave speed is:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}} $$
- Here:
  - $F_T$ is the string tension
  - $\mu$ is the linear mass density

---

### 27. Effect of Tension
- Increasing the tension increases the wave speed:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}\propto\sqrt{F_T} $$
- A larger tension provides a stronger restoring force, allowing the disturbance to propagate more rapidly.

---

### 28. Effect of Linear Density
- Increasing the linear mass density decreases the wave speed:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}} $$
- A heavier or denser string has more inertia per unit length and responds more slowly.

---

### 29. String with a Hanging Mass
- A hanging mass can provide the string tension.
- If the mass is stationary and the pulley and string are ideal:
  $$ F_T\approx Mg $$
- Therefore:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{Mg}{\mu}} $$
- Increasing the hanging mass increases the tension and therefore increases the wave speed.

---

### 30. Frequency vs. Wave Speed
- The source controls the wave frequency.
- The medium determines the wave speed.
- Increasing frequency does not necessarily increase the wave speed in a fixed, nondispersive medium.

---

## Describing Waves in Space and Time

### 31. Displacement as a Function of Position
- A graph of:
  $$ y(x) $$
  at one fixed time is a snapshot of the entire wave.
- It shows the displacement of many particles at the same instant.

---

### 32. Displacement as a Function of Time
- A graph of:
  $$ y(t) $$
  at one fixed position follows one particle as it oscillates over time.
- It shows how that single point in the medium moves.

---

### 33. Two Views of the Same Wave
- **Spatial view**:
  $$ y \text{ vs. } x \quad \text{at fixed }t $$
- **Temporal view**:
  $$ y \text{ vs. } t \quad \text{at fixed }x $$
- Both describe the same wave but answer different questions.

---

### 34. Determining Particle Motion from a Wave Snapshot
- Suppose a transverse wave moves to the right.
- To predict a particle’s next motion:
  1. shift the entire wave shape slightly to the right
  2. compare the old and new displacement at the particle’s fixed position
- The particle itself does not shift to the right; only the wave profile does.

---

### 35. Example of a Right-Moving Wave
- If a particle lies on the leading side of a crest in a right-moving wave, the approaching profile determines whether it next moves up or down.
- The correct direction is found by translating the shape, not by following the slope as though the particle moved horizontally along the curve.

---

### 36. Particle Speed in a Sinusoidal Wave
- Each particle in a sinusoidal wave undergoes simple harmonic motion.
- At a crest or trough:
  $$ u=0 $$
- At equilibrium:
  $$ |u|=u_{\max} $$
- Therefore, particles move fastest while crossing the equilibrium line.

---

## Wavelength, Period, Frequency, and Speed

### 37. Wavelength
- Wavelength $\lambda$ is the distance between equivalent neighboring points on a wave:
  - crest to crest
  - trough to trough
  - compression to compression

---

### 38. Period
- The period $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation:
  $$ T=\frac{1}{f} $$

---

### 39. Wave-Speed Relation
- In one period, the wave travels one wavelength.
- Therefore:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=\frac{\lambda}{T} $$
- Since:
  $$ f=\frac{1}{T} $$
- We obtain:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=f\lambda $$

---

### 40. Frequency and Wavelength in a Fixed Medium
- If the wave speed is fixed by the medium:
  $$ \lambda=\frac{v}{f} $$
- Increasing frequency decreases wavelength.
- Decreasing frequency increases wavelength.
- The propagation speed remains the same if the medium is unchanged and nondispersive.

---

### 41. Sound-Wave Speed
- In a given medium under ordinary conditions, sound waves of different frequencies travel at approximately the same speed.
- Frequency changes:
  - pitch
  - wavelength
- It does not ordinarily change the sound speed in the same medium.

---

## Wave Dimensionality and Source Patterns

### 42. One-Dimensional Waves
- A wave on a string propagates primarily along one spatial dimension:
  $$ x $$
- The string particles may move in a perpendicular direction, but the disturbance travels along the string.

---

### 43. Two- and Three-Dimensional Waves
- Surface-water waves spread over an area.
- Sound waves from a localized source spread through three-dimensional space.
- Wavefront geometry becomes more complex in higher dimensions.

---

### 44. Monopole Source
- A monopole source oscillates symmetrically from one central region.
- It can produce approximately spherical wavefronts in three dimensions.
- An idealized pulsating source is an example.

---

### 45. Dipole Source
- A dipole source oscillates back and forth along one axis.
- Its radiation pattern is not spherically symmetric.
- The source direction affects how strongly the wave is emitted in different directions.

---

### 46. Quadrupole Source
- A quadrupole source has a more complex pattern involving multiple directions of motion.
- It produces a more complicated radiation pattern than either a monopole or dipole.

---

### 47. Membrane Oscillation Modes
- A drumhead or membrane can oscillate in several different normal modes.
- Different regions may move:
  - together
  - in opposite directions
  - with stationary nodal lines between them
- More complicated vibrations can be built from combinations of simpler modes.

---

### 48. Eardrum Motion
- The eardrum behaves as an oscillating membrane.
- Incoming sound produces complex membrane motion.
- Its shape at any instant may be a superposition of many vibrational modes.

---

## Mechanical and Electromagnetic Waves

### 49. Mechanical Waves
- Mechanical waves require a material medium.
- Examples:
  - waves on strings
  - sound waves
  - waves in membranes
- Neighboring particles interact through forces and transmit the disturbance.

---

### 50. Sound as a Mechanical Wave
- In a sound wave, molecules interact with neighboring molecules.
- Each molecule oscillates around an equilibrium position while pressure disturbances propagate through the material.

---

### 51. Electromagnetic Waves
- Light is an electromagnetic wave.
- It does not require a material medium and can propagate through vacuum.
- An electromagnetic wave contains:
  - an oscillating electric field
  - an oscillating magnetic field

---

### 52. Field Directions in an Electromagnetic Wave
- For an ideal electromagnetic wave:
  $$ \vec{E}\perp\vec{B} $$
- Both fields are also perpendicular to the propagation direction:
  $$ \vec{E}\perp\vec{v}_{\mathrm{wave}} $$
  $$ \vec{B}\perp\vec{v}_{\mathrm{wave}} $$

---

### 53. Main Physics Takeaways
- Pendulum frequency:
  $$ f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} $$
- Mass–spring frequency:
  $$ f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} $$
- Wave speed on a string:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=\sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}} $$
- General wave-speed relation:
  $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=f\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{T} $$
- A wave transports a disturbance while medium particles oscillate around equilibrium.
- Transverse and longitudinal waves differ in the direction of particle motion.
- Mechanical waves require a medium; electromagnetic waves can propagate through vacuum.

---

### 54. Main Conceptual Takeaways
1. Identify the restoring force responsible for each oscillation.
2. Distinguish particle motion from wave propagation.
3. Distinguish a spatial wave snapshot from a time history at one location.
4. Remember that the medium determines wave speed.
5. Use:
   $$ v_{\mathrm{wave}}=f\lambda $$
   to relate changes in frequency and wavelength.
6. Treat each point in a sinusoidal mechanical wave as a local simple harmonic oscillator.
