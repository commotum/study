## Introduction

Suppose that we want to find the finite area bounded between the curves $y=x^2$ and $y=1$.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/3d07dc99fba46a38c61d40c612a6c023.png>)

The area $A$ of the region is given by

$$
A = \int_{-1}^{1} \left[\, \left(\color{brown}{\textrm{upper function}}\right) - \left(\color{blue}{\textrm{lower function}}\right) \,\right] \,\textrm{d}x
$$

So in our case, the area is expressed as

$$
A = \int_{-1}^{1} \big[ ({\color{brown}{1}}) - ({\color{blue}{x^2}}) \big]\,\textrm{d}x = \int_{-1}^{1} (1-x^2)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

Now that the integral has been written down correctly, we can evaluate it using the usual techniques:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^{2})dx \\
&= [x - \frac{x^{3}}{3}]_{-1}^{1} \\
&= [1 - \frac{1}{3}] - [- 1 - ((-1)^{3})/(3)] \\
&= \frac{2}{3} - [- \frac{2}{3}] \\
&= \frac{4}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the area of the region is

$$
\dfrac{4}{3}
$$

Lastly, let's recap a few things:

- The limits of integration correspond to the $x$-values at the points of intersection of the two functions.
- If you're not sure which is the lower function and which is the upper, one way to find out is to draw a vertical line that *points upward* through the region, like so:

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/63d2918a98f1205f4612134313aa82b3.png>)

The vertical line always

- *enters* the region through the lower function $y=x^2$, and
- *leaves* through the upper function $y=1$.

## Expressing the Area Between Two Curves as an Integral Given the Limits of Integration

**Example:** Write down an integral that gives the area of the finite region bounded by the curves $y=x^2$ and $y=\sqrt x$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/0af8c0419c649a2986c7a4112399a532.png>)

**Explanation**

The diagram shows that the intersection points between the two graphs occur when $x=0$ and $x=1$. So these will be our limits of integration.

To determine which is the lower function and which is the upper, let's draw our vertical arrow.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/e2dad27370be8b6a9b82b1adc02d06c5.png>)

The vertical line

- *enters* the region through the lower function $y=x^2$, and
- *leaves* through the upper function $y=\sqrt x$.

Therefore, the integral that gives our area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{0}^{1}[(upper function) - (lower function)]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[(\sqrt{x}) - (x^{2})]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{x} - x^{2})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Expressing the Area Between Two Curves as an Integral by First Solving For the Limits of Integration

**Example:** What is the integral that gives the area of the finite region bounded by $y=x^2+x+5$ and $y=x+9$?

**Explanation**

First, let's draw a sketch of the two functions.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/36b16b895b0234f1f9e10a91977d6c66.png>)

*(Note: Figure not drawn to scale)*

Now, we want to determine the limits of integration. To do this, we equate the two functions and solve for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + x + 5 &= x + 9 \\
x^{2} - 4 &= 0 \\
(x - 2)(x + 2) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, we need to integrate from $x=-2$ to $x=2$.

To determine which is the lower function and which is the upper, let's draw our vertical arrow.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/c27aaed348fa7bebc01f25f85b3a720e.png>)

The vertical line

- *enters* the region through the lower function $y=x^2+x+5$, and
- *leaves* through the upper function $y=x+9$.

Therefore, the integral that gives our area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{-2}^{2}[(upper function) - (lower function)]dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}[(x + 9) - (x^{2} + x + 5)]dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}(4 - x^{2})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-area-between-two-curves-expressed-as-functions-of-x"></a>
## Calculating the Area Between Two Curves Expressed as Functions of X

**Example:** Find the area of the finite region enclosed between $f(x) =x^2-2x+2$ and $g(x) = 2$, shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/5c6f110a048606921590ea4e6403ede0.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we want to determine the limits of integration. To do this, we equate the two functions and solve for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} - 2x + 2 &= 2 \\
x^{2} - 2x &= 0 \\
x(x - 2) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, we need to integrate from $x=0$ to $x=2$.

To determine which is the lower function and which is the upper, we draw our vertical arrow.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/88219aab4954daef055e1fa24e9e55b6.png>)

The vertical line

- *enters* the region through the lower function $y=x^2-2x+2$, and
- *leaves* through the upper function $y=2$.

Therefore, we can find the area as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{0}^{2}[(upper function) - (lower function)]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}[(2) - (x^{2} - 2x + 2)]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}(-x^{2} + 2x)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}(2x - x^{2})dx \\
&= (x^{2} - \frac{1}{3}x^{3}) \mid _{0}^{2} \\
&= (2^{2} - \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2^{3}) - (0 - 0) \\
&= (4 - \frac{8}{3}) - 0 \\
&= \frac{4}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Justifying the Rule

Lastly, let's discuss why the area formula works.

$$
A = \int_{a}^{b}\left(\color{brown}{\textrm{upper function}}\right) - \left(\color{blue}{\textrm{lower function}}\right)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

Consider the area bounded by the two functions $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$. We'll assume that the two curves intersect at $x=\pm 1$, and for $x\in(-1,1)$ we have $f(x) > g(x)$, so $f(x)$ is the upper function.

We can approximate the area using rectangles in the following way. First, we will divide the interval $[-1,1]$ into small subintervals of equal length $\Delta{x}$. One such subinterval is shown in the picture below.

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/6e62e089f317066b0c0c58d98598fd43.png>)

The area $\Delta{A}$ bounded between the curves $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ can be approximated by the area of the rectangle shown below, with the sides $\Delta{x}$ (horizontal) and $f(x)-g(x)$ (vertical),

$$
\Delta{A} \approx \left(f(x)-g(x)\right)\Delta{x}
$$

![](<../Source/10.1.2. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of X/Images/9020afb615cd5628a69a5caa7d365d17.png>)

The sum over all the rectangles will approximate the total area $A$ that we want. This approximation becomes more accurate as the rectangles get narrower, i.e., $\Delta{x} \to 0$. And the limit of the sum, as $\Delta x \to 0$, is the required integral!

$$
A = ∫_{a}^{b}(f(x) - g(x))dx
$$
