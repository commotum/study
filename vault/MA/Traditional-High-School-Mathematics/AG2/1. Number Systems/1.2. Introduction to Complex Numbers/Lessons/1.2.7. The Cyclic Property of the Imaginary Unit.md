## Introduction

Let's compute the first few powers of $\textrm{i}$, where

$$
\textrm{i} = \sqrt{-1}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textrm{i}^{0} &= 1 \\
\textrm{i}^{1} &= \textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{2} &= -1 \\
\textrm{i}^{3} &= \textrm{i}^{2} \cdot \textrm{i} = (-1) \cdot \textrm{i} =-\textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{4} &= \textrm{i}^{2} \cdot \textrm{i}^{2} = (-1) \cdot (-1) = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

We can use these results to evaluate expressions containing powers of $\textrm{i}$. Let's take a look at some examples.

---

<a id="raising-i-to-a-small-positive-integer-power"></a>
## Raising i to a Small Positive Integer Power

**Example:** What is the value of $4\textrm{i}^3$?

**Explanation**

Let's recall the first few powers of $\textrm{i}$, where

$$
\textrm{i} = \sqrt{-1}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textrm{i}^{0} &= 1 \\
\textrm{i}^{1} &= \textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{2} &= -1 \\
\textrm{i}^{3} &= \textrm{i}^{2} \cdot \textrm{i} = (-1) \cdot \textrm{i} =-\textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{4} &= \textrm{i}^{2} \cdot \textrm{i}^{2} = (-1) \cdot (-1) = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
4\textrm{i}^{3} &= 4 \cdot (-\textrm{i}) =-4\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-cyclic-nature-of-powers-of-i"></a>
## The Cyclic Nature of Powers of i

Let's remind ourselves of the first few powers of $\textrm{i}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textrm{i}^{1} &= \textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{2} &= -1 \\
\textrm{i}^{3} &= -\textrm{i} \\
\textrm{i}^{4} &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

At the fourth power, we return to the value of $1$, which can also be written as

$$
\textrm{i}^0 = 1
$$

This means that if we continue to increase the powers, we would cycle through the same four values once again, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.2.7. The Cyclic Property of the Imaginary Unit/Images/fbffb9f09d2e7822b179e42e2c24ea85.png>)

So, for $\textrm{i}^5$ through $\textrm{i}^8$, the exact same pattern repeats. Remembering that

$$
\textrm{i}^4 = 1
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned} \textrm{i}^5&=\textrm{i}^4 \cdot \textrm{i} &= \textrm{i} \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^6&=\textrm{i}^4 \cdot \textrm{i}^2 &=-1 \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^7&=\textrm{i}^4 \cdot \textrm{i}^3 &= -\textrm{i} \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^8&=\textrm{i}^4 \cdot \textrm{i}^4 &= 1. \end{aligned}
$$

Similarly, for $\textrm{i}^9$ through to $\textrm{i}^{12}$, the pattern repeats again:

$$
\begin{aligned} \textrm{i}^9&=(\textrm{i}^4)^2 \cdot \textrm{i} &= \textrm{i} \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^{10}&=(\textrm{i}^4)^2 \cdot \textrm{i}^2 &=-1 \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^{11}&=(\textrm{i}^4)^2 \cdot \textrm{i}^3 &= -\textrm{i} \\[5pt] \textrm{i}^{12}&=(\textrm{i}^4)^2 \cdot \textrm{i}^4 &= 1 \end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, to simplify a higher integer power of $\textrm{i}$, we *divide the power by $4$ and raise $\textrm{i}$ to the remainder*.

Let's see this idea in action.

---

<a id="raising-i-to-a-positive-integer-power-using-the-cyclic-property"></a>
## Raising i to a Positive Integer Power Using the Cyclic Property

**Example:** Simplify $\textrm{i}^{15}$.

**Explanation**

To find the value of $\textrm{i}$ to some positive integer power $n$, we divide $n$ by $4$ and raise $\textrm{i}$ to the remainder.

In our case, we have $n=15$. Dividing the power by $4$, we get

$$
15\div 4 = 3\,\textrm{R} \color{blue}{3}
$$

So the remainder is $\color{blue}3$. Therefore,

$$
\textrm{i}^{15} = \textrm{i}^{\color{blue}{3}} = -\textrm{i}
$$

## Evaluating an Expression Involving Powers of i Using the Laws of Exponents

**Example:** If $a = \textrm{i}^9$ and $b = \textrm{i}^{7}$, then $ab =$

**Explanation**

Using the addition law for exponents, we have

$$
ab =\textrm{i}^9\cdot\textrm{i}^7 = \textrm{i}^{9+7} = \textrm{i}^{16}
$$

To find the value of $\textrm{i}$ raised to some positive integer power $n$, we divide $n$ by $4$ and raise $\textrm{i}$ to the remainder.

In our case, we have $n=16$. Dividing the power by $4$, we get

$$
16\div 4 = 4\,\textrm{R} \color{blue}{0}
$$

So the remainder is $\color{blue}0$. Therefore,

$$
\textrm{i}^{16} = \textrm{i}^{\color{blue}{0}} =1
$$

<a id="raising-i-to-a-negative-power"></a>
## Raising i to a Negative Power

**Example:** Find the value of $\textrm{i}^{-2}$.

**Explanation**

First, using the laws of exponents, we can write

$$
\textrm{i}^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{\textrm{i}^2}
$$

We now recall that

$$
\textrm{i}^2 = -1
$$

Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{1}{\textrm{i}^2} = \dfrac{1}{(-1)} = -1
$$
