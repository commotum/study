## Introduction

Suppose we have the following triangle, where

- Side $a$ is *opposite* angle $A$
- Side $b$ is *opposite* angle $B$
- Side $c$ is *opposite* angle $C$.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/2592bd04cc4acd402d5f9f067978365a.png>)

The **law of sines** states that

$$
\dfrac{a}{\sin{A}} = \dfrac{b}{\sin{B}} = \dfrac{c}{\sin{C}}
$$

We'll prove this result at the end of the lesson.

We can use the law of sines to find an unknown side of the triangle. Let's see an example.

---

<a id="determining-the-length-of-a-side-of-a-triangle-using-the-law-of-sines"></a>
## Determining the Length of a Side of a Triangle Using the Law of Sines

**Example:** Consider the triangle below

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/527d4f8b4eeff11fc584dbc20c11bebf.png>)

Calculate the length of the side $a$.

**Explanation**

First, let's label all of the vertices and sides of our triangle.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/189ec7d32f5066aee8c6e239e016984c.png>)

The law of sines states that

$$
\dfrac{a}{\sin{A}} = \dfrac{b}{\sin{B}} = \dfrac{c}{\sin{C}}
$$

In this case, we have

$$
m\angle{A} = 75^\circ,\quad m\angle{B}=45^\circ, \quad b=10
$$

and consequently

$$
\frac{a}{\sin{75^\circ}} = \frac{10}{\sin{45^\circ}}
$$

To solve for $a$, we multiply both sides by $\sin{75^\circ}$ to give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{a}{\sin 75^{∘}} &= \sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{10}{\sin 45^{∘}} \\
\sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{a}{\sin 75^{∘}} &= \sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{10}{\sin 45^{∘}} \\
a &= \sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{10}{\sin 45^{∘}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now,

$$
\sin{75^\circ}= 0.965\, 93
$$

and

$$
\sin{45^\circ}= 0.707\, 11
$$

both rounded to five decimal places to avoid rounding error. This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \sin 75^{∘} \cdot \frac{10}{\sin 45^{∘}} \\
&= 0.96593 \cdot \frac{10}{0.70711} \\
&= \frac{0.96593 \cdot 10}{0.70711} \\
&= \frac{9.6593}{0.70711} \\
&= 13.66
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to two decimal places.

## Using the Law of Sines to Calculate an Acute Angle

Let's again consider the following triangle:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/e62c98d65f8afeee6fc2362ff0befd9d.png>)

The **law of sines** states that

$$
\frac{a}{\sin{A}} = \frac{b}{\sin{B}} = \frac{c}{\sin{C}}
$$

We can flip the fractions *upside down* to get another formula

$$
\frac{\sin{A}}{a} = \frac{\sin{B}}{b} = \frac{\sin{C}}{c}
$$

We can use this to find an unknown angle of the triangle.

---

<a id="determining-the-measure-of-an-acute-angle-in-a-triangle-using-the-law-of-sines"></a>
## Determining the Measure of an Acute Angle in a Triangle Using the Law of Sines

**Example:** Calculate the angle $B$, rounding your answer to two decimal places.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/557b67eb9492ac67ef87c51671f2c3dd.png>)

**Explanation**

The law of sines states that

$$
\frac{\sin{B}}{b} = {\frac{\sin{A}}{a}}
$$

In this case, we have

$$
m\angle{A}=100^\circ,\quad a=15,\quad b=5
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin B}{5} &= \frac{\sin 100^{∘}}{15} \\
\sin B &= 5 \cdot \frac{\sin 100^{∘}}{15} \\
&= \frac{\sin 100^{∘}}{3} \\
&= \frac{0.98481}{3} \\
&= 0.32827
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to five decimal places.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠B &= \arcsin (0.32827) = 19.16^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to two decimal places.

## Calculating an Obtuse Angle Using the Law of Sines

Let's again consider the following triangle:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/6a7b50cc24fe3dbd5400b5518520b561.png>)

The **law of sines** states that

$$
\frac{\sin{A}}{a} = \frac{\sin{B}}{b} = \frac{\sin{C}}{c}
$$

We can use the law of sines to find an unknown acute angle of the triangle, but we must *be careful* when using the law of sines to calculate an **obtuse** angle.

The calculator will *always* give an acute angle. If we're trying to find an obtuse angle, we need to subtract our answer from $180^\circ$.

---

<a id="determining-the-measure-of-an-obtuse-angle-in-a-triangle-using-the-law-of-sines"></a>
## Determining the Measure of an Obtuse Angle in a Triangle Using the Law of Sines

**Example:** Given that $\angle A$ is obtuse, calculate its measure.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/79060b5edc6222197dab56960a1a751f.png>)

**Explanation**

The law of sines states that

$$
{\frac{\sin{A}}{a}} = {\frac{\sin{B}}{b}}
$$

In this case, we have

$$
a = 25,\quad m\angle{B}=20^{\circ}, \quad b=10
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin A}{25} &= \frac{\sin 20^{∘}}{10} \\
\sin A &= 25 \cdot \frac{\sin 20^{∘}}{10} \\
&= 2.5\sin 20^{∘} \\
&= 2.5 \cdot (0.34202) \\
&= 0.85505
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to five decimal places. This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠A &= \arcsin (0.85505) = 58.77^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to $2$ decimal places. But this is less than $90^\circ$. To get the true value of $A$, we subtract our answer from $180^\circ$:

$$
m\angle A = 180^\circ - 58.77^\circ = 121.23^{\circ}
$$

## The Case When Multiple Triangles are Possible

Even if we are given the measure of an angle and two sides of a triangle, it is sometimes possible for two different triangles to satisfy those criteria.

For example, suppose we are told that in $\triangle ABC$, we have

$$
m\angle A = 30^\circ
$$

$a=4$ and $b=5$.

There are two different triangles that satisfy these criteria.

- In one triangle, $\angle B$ is obtuse and $c$ is short:![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/629439566241b9cbb1f3de3d70237559.png>)
- In the other triangle, $\angle B$ is acute and $c$ is long:![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/d577627ea74754ef4be7e28321384de0.png>)

According to the law of sines, we have the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin A}{a} &= \frac{\sin B}{b} \\
\frac{\sin 30^{∘}}{4} &= \frac{\sin B}{5} \\
\sin B &= 5 \cdot \frac{\sin 30^{∘}}{4} \\
\sin B &= 5 \cdot \frac{0.5}{4} \\
\sin B &= 0.625
\end{aligned}
$$

One solution to the above equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \arcsin (0.625) \\
&= 38.68^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

This solution corresponds to the second triangle, where $\angle B$ is acute.

However, this is not the only solution. Another solution is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= 180^{∘} - \arcsin (0.625) \\
&= 180^{∘} - 38.68^{∘} \\
&= 141.32^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

This solution corresponds to the first triangle, where $\angle B$ is obtuse.

<a id="applying-the-law-of-sines-when-multiple-triangles-are-possible"></a>
## Applying the Law of Sines when Multiple Triangles are Possible

**Example:** In $\triangle ABC$, we have $m\angle B = 30^\circ$, $AC=5$ and $AB=8$. Which of the following statements *could* be true?

1. $\angle C$ is acute
2. $m\angle C = 23.13^{\circ}$ rounded to $2$ decimal places
3. $m\angle C = 53.13^\circ$ rounded to $2$ decimal places

**Explanation**

The following triangle illustrates the information given.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/16ce649a07abd8fe4c91ac408c99bd2a.png>)

The law of sines states that

$$
{\dfrac{\sin{C}}{c}} = {\dfrac{\sin{B}}{b}}
$$

In this case, we have

$$
c = 8, \quad m\angle{B} = 30^{\circ}, \quad b = 5
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin C}{8} &= \frac{\sin 30^{∘}}{5} \\
\sin C &= 8 \cdot \frac{\sin 30^{∘}}{5} \\
&= 8 \cdot \frac{0.5}{5} \\
&= 0.8
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠C &= \arcsin (0.8) = 53.13^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to $2$ decimal places.

In this case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠A &= 180^{∘} - m∠B - m∠C \\
&= 180^{∘} - 30^{∘} - 53.13^{∘} \\
&= 96.87^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

However, another solution to

$$
\sin{C} =0.8
$$

is given by

$$
m \angle C = 180^\circ - 53.13^{\circ} = 126.87^{\circ}
$$

In this case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠A &= 180^{∘} - m∠B - m∠C \\
&= 180^{∘} - 30^{∘} - 126.87^{∘} \\
&= 23.13^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, there are two possible triangles, with the following angle measures:

- $m\angle A = 96.87^\circ, \, \, m\angle B = 30^{\circ}, \,\, m\angle C = 53.13^\circ$

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/d3be7e75ccabdc8ed02ed76ba8598801.png>)

- $m\angle A = 23.13^\circ, \, m\angle B = 30^{\circ}, \, m\angle C = 126.87^\circ$

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/65e2a525d49b27087dd0c99aee17d267.png>)

In conclusion, the correct answer is "I and III only."

<a id="deriving-the-law-of-sines"></a>
## Deriving the Law of Sines

Let's now derive the law of sines.

Consider $\triangle ABC$ with sides of lengths $a,b$, and $c$, as shown below. Let's assume $\angle A$ is obtuse.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/ad12eb03c1b6844d65908c0b4ea12fdd.png>)

Let's add the height perpendicular to $\overline{BC}$, shown below, and let's assume that the length of this height equals $h$.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/c7e9547fe78f5287b077486e3035c0d6.png>)

Using elementary trigonometry, we have that

$$
\dfrac{h}{b} = \sin C,\qquad \dfrac{h}{c} = \sin B
$$

which gives

$$
h = b\sin C,\qquad h = c\sin B
$$

Equating the two expressions for $h$ gives

$$
b\sin C = c\sin B
$$

Dividing this equation by $\sin B\sin C$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
b\sin C &= c\sin B \\
\frac{b\sin C}{\sin B\sin C} &= \frac{c\sin B}{\sin B\sin C} \\
\frac{b\sin C}{\sin B\sin C} &= \frac{c\sin B}{\sin B\sin C} \\
\frac{b}{\sin B} &= \frac{c}{\sin C}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can also draw a height of length $h'$ perpendicular to the side $AB$.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. The Law of Sines/Images/f0caf8bcb9f04116f0cade04c958cef1.png>)

According to the diagram, we have

$$
\dfrac{h'}{a} = \sin B,\qquad \dfrac{h'}{b} = \sin (180^\circ - A)
$$

which gives

$$
h' = a\sin B,\qquad h' = b\sin (180^\circ - A)
$$

Equating the two expressions for $h'$ gives

$$
a\sin B = b\sin (180^\circ - A)
$$

It can be shown that

$$
\sin (180^\circ - A) = \sin A
$$

(this essentially states that the sine function is symmetric about

$$
A = 90^\circ
$$

which you'll explore in more detail in another lesson). Therefore,

$$
a\sin B = b\sin A
$$

Dividing this equation by $\sin A\sin B$ yields

$$
\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B}
$$

Finally, combining this with our previous result, we get

$$
\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}
$$

as required.

Here, we considered the case of an obtuse triangle, where one of the heights we considered lies outside the triangle. The law of sines also applies to acute triangles; the proof is similar.
