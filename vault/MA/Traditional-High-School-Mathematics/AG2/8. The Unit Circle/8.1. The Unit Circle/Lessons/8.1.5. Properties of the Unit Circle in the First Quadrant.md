## Introduction

Let's consider the unit circle with a central angle $\theta$ in the first quadrant, measured in the usual way.

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/dbd6f38c7eecfc5568af321347abd19c.png>)

Notice that

- the $x$-coordinate of $P$ coincides with the cosine of the angle $\theta$, and
- the $y$-coordinate coincides with the sine of the angle $\theta$.

So, we have

$$
x = \cos\theta, \qquad y = \sin\theta
$$

Consequently, if we know the angle $\theta$, we can easily work out the coordinates of the point $P$, and vice-versa. This works regardless of whether the angle $\theta$ is given in degrees or radians.

For now, we'll focus on points located in the first quadrant. Later, we will encounter points in all quadrants.

## Finding the Coordinates of a Point on the Unit Circle Given Its Central Angle

**Example:** The diagram above shows the unit circle. What are the coordinates of the point $P$?

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/aca7523739321562ee0129ca5f054028.png>)

**Explanation**

Any point $(x,y)$ on the unit circle is related to the central angle $\theta$ as follows:

$$
x = \cos\theta, \qquad y = \sin\theta
$$

We're given that

$$
\theta=45^\circ
$$

at the point $P$. Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \cos 45^{∘} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \\
y &= \sin 45^{∘} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the coordinates of $P$ are

$$
\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 2}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}\right)
$$

## A Proof of the Unit Circle Property

Let's consider the unit circle and a point $P(x,y)$ that lies on the circle.

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/587cfb0adcaf4e693d6671ed2d5e5b40.png>)

To prove that

$$
x=\cos\theta
$$

and

$$
y=\sin\theta
$$

let's examine the triangle that's formed by $\overline{OP}$ and the $x$-axis.

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/6c18116d1b3741038e4827a07b35d32d.png>)

Notice that:

- The hypotenuse has length $1$ since it is a radius of the unit circle.
- The adjacent and opposite sides have lengths $x$ and $y$, respectively.

Therefore, using the definitions of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$, we have

$$
\cos\theta = \dfrac{x}{1} = x, \qquad \sin\theta = \dfrac{y}{1} = y
$$

## Finding a Central Angle Given the Coordinates of a Point on the Unit Circle

**Example:** The diagram above shows the unit circle. Given that the $y$-coordinate of the point $P$ is $\dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}$, what is the measure of the angle $\theta$?

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/90b94aa4e9c9ade6748be507de6ecbca.png>)

**Explanation**

We're given that

$$
y=\dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}
$$

at the point $P$. Therefore, we have

$$
\sin\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}
$$

We compute the angle $\theta$ using the inverse sine:

$$
\theta = \arcsin\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\right) = 60^\circ
$$

## Finding a Reciprocal Trigonometric Ratio Given the Coordinates of a Point on the Unit Circle

**Example:** The point $P\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 8}{3},\dfrac{1}{3}\right)$ lies on the unit circle, as shown. What is $\sec\theta$?

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/203da6297658cc5402a019db17bfff22.png>)

**Explanation**

We're given that

$$
x=\dfrac{\sqrt 8}{3}
$$

at the point $P$. Therefore, we have

$$
\cos\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt 8}{3}
$$

Knowing that

$$
\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}
$$

we can calculate $\sec\theta$ as follows:

$$
\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 8}{3}\right)} =\dfrac{3}{\sqrt 8} = \dfrac{3\sqrt 8}{8}
$$

<a id="the-relationship-between-sine-cosine-and-tangent"></a>
## The Relationship Between Sine, Cosine, and Tangent

To find the tangent of an angle using the unit circle, we need to use the relationship

$$
\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}
$$

To see why this is true, let's take a look at the following triangle.

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/18194228cf1fb1c637db5880929e639f.png>)

For this particular triangle, we have

$$
\sin\theta = \dfrac{y}{z}, \qquad \cos\theta = \dfrac{x}{z}, \qquad \tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}
$$

When we take $\sin\theta$ and divide it by $\cos\theta$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin θ}{\cos θ} &= \frac{y}{z} \div \frac{x}{z} \\
&= \frac{y}{z} \cdot \frac{z}{x} \\
&= \frac{y \cdot z}{x \cdot z} \\
&= \frac{y \cdot z}{x \cdot z} \\
&= \frac{y}{x} \\
&= \tan θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Although this proof is only for acute angles, the identity is true for any angle, provided that $\tan\theta$ is well-defined.

Finally, since

$$
\cot\theta = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}
$$

it immediately follows that

$$
\cot\theta = \dfrac{1}{\left(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\right)} = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}
$$

## Finding the Tangent or Cotangent of a Central Angle Given a Point on the Unit Circle

**Example:** The point $P\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 7}{7},\dfrac{\sqrt{42}}{7}\right)$ lies on the unit circle, as shown. What is $\tan\theta$?

![](<../Source/8.1.5. Properties of the Unit Circle in the First Quadrant/Images/082f1d3f6dbbf27fc349ea605979ecf7.png>)

**Explanation**

We're given that

$$
x=\dfrac{\sqrt 7}{7}
$$

and

$$
y=\dfrac{\sqrt{42}}{7}
$$

at the point $P$. Therefore, we have

$$
\cos\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt 7}{7}, \qquad \sin\theta =\dfrac{\sqrt{42}}{7}
$$

Knowing that

$$
\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin \theta}{\cos\theta}
$$

we can calculate $\tan\theta$ as follows:

$$
\tan\theta = \dfrac{\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{42}}{7}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 7}{7}\right)} = \dfrac{\sqrt{42}}{\sqrt{7}} = \sqrt 6
$$
