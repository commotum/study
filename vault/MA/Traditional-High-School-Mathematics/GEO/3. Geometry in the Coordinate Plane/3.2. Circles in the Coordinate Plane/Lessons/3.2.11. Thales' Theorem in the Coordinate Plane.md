## Introduction

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/d3e083a3ba7a16f7262e78b6559dcfbc.png>)

Let's recall Thales' Theorem:

> *If the points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on the circumference of a circle and $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter of the circle, then $\angle ACB$ is a right angle.*

Note that the **converse** of Thales' theorem is also true:

> *If the points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on the circumference of a circle and $\angle ACB$ is a right angle, then $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter of the circle.*

In this lesson, we'll learn how to apply Thales' theorem in the coordinate plane.

## Finding a Circle's Center

**Example:** The points $A, B$ and $C$ lie on a circle and $m\angle{BAC}=90^\circ$. What are the coordinates of the circle's center?

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/2cc7da80e8ad6ec984d6cb3cba2e93f5.png>)

**Explanation**

Since

$$
m\angle{BAC}=90^\circ
$$

by Thales' theorem, $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter of the circle. Therefore, the circle's center is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$.

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/486f9579dc0f7c1b70da24c4d444aea0.png>)

Now, we use the midpoint formula:

$$
(\frac{x_{1} + x_{2}}{2}, \frac{y_{1} + y_{2}}{2}) = (\frac{-4 + 2}{2}, \frac{-1 + 5}{2}) = (-1, 2)
$$

Therefore, the center of the circle is $(-1,2)$.

## Finding the Length of a Chord Given the Diameter and the Length of Another Chord

**Example:** The points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on the above circle. The chord $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter of the circle and $BC= 2\sqrt{10}$. Given that $AB=2\sqrt{5}$, calculate the length of the chord $\overline{AC}$.

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/acfd18cfbbc99d158a241809df4bf6cd.png>)

**Explanation**

Since the points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on the circle, and $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter, it follows that $\triangle ABC$ is a right triangle.

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/5480073fbc1352b0b812c5ef2183fe55.png>)

So, using the Pythagorean theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
AC &= \sqrt{BC^{2} - AB^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(2\sqrt{10})^{2} - (2\sqrt{5})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{4(10) - 4(5)} \\
&= \sqrt{40 - 20} \\
&= \sqrt{20} \\
&= 2\sqrt{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Length of a Chord Given the Diameter and the Endpoints of Another Chord

**Example:** The points $A$ and $B$ with coordinates $(4, 8)$ and $(1,9)$, respectively, lie on the circumference of a circle of radius $4$. If a third point $C$ also lies on the circle and $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter, what is the value of $AC$?

**Explanation**

The radius of the circle is $r=4$, and since $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter of the circle, we have

$$
BC = 2r = 2(4) = 8
$$

Now, since $A$ lies on a circle and $\overline{BC}$ is a diameter, it follows that $\triangle ABC$ is a right triangle.

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/e59380e1092f2c8699d6bbc23d288688.png>)

Using the distance formula to calculate $AB$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB &= \sqrt{(4 - 1)^{2} + (8 - 9)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(3)^{2} + (-1)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the Pythagorean theorem gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
AC &= \sqrt{BC^{2} - AB^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{8^{2} - (\sqrt{10})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{64 - 10} \\
&= \sqrt{54} \\
&= 3\sqrt{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Endpoint of a Chord that Forms a Right Triangle with a Given Chord

**Example:** The points $A$, $B$, and $C$ lie on the circumference of a circle. The coordinates of $A$ and $B$ are $\left(-1,0\right)$ and $\left(2,3\right)$, respectively. If $\overline{AC}$ is a diameter of the circle and the coordinates of $C$ are $(k,0)$, then what is the value of $k$?

**Explanation**

The angle $\angle ABC$ is subtended by the diameter $\overline{AC}$, so its measure is $90^\circ$. So we have $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{AB} \perp \overset{\longleftrightarrow}{CB}$.

![](<../Source/3.2.11. Thales' Theorem in the Coordinate Plane/Images/a8380951d8dac0b3483c3d51bd35b424.png>)

Using the coordinates $A(-1,0)$ and $B(2,3)$, let's find the slope of the line $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{AB}$:

$$
m_{AB} = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \dfrac{3 - 0}{2 - (-1)} = 1
$$

Since $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{CB}$ is perpendicular to $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{AB}$, its slope is the negative reciprocal:

$$
m_{CB} = -\dfrac{1}{m_{BC}} = -1
$$

Since $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{CB}$ passes through the point $B(2,3)$ with slope

$$
m_{CB} = -1
$$

its equation in slope-point form must be

$$
\begin{aligned}
y - y_{1} &= m_{CB}(x - x_{1}) \\
y - 3 &= -1 \cdot (x - 2) \\
y - 3 &= - (x - 2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, the point $C(k,0)$ also lies on $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{CB}$, so its coordinates $(k,0)$ must satisfy the equation above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y - 3 &= - (x - 2) \\
0 - 3 &= - (k - 2) \\
- 3 &= -k + 2 \\
k &= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the answer is $k=5$.
