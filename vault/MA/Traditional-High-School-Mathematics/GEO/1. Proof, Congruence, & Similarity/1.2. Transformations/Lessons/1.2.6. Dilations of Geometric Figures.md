## Introduction

Consider the polygon $\mathcal{P}$ below. How can we construct a polygon with the same shape as $\mathcal{P}$ but whose side lengths are twice those of $\mathcal{P}$?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/6e0300c61f8ee4abf55058d40b3d51df.png>)

First, we pick a random point $O$ in the plane. Then, we draw three line segments that connect the point $O$ with the vertices of our polygon.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/68014d0474c773ec79e1bc3bca2fd413.png>)

Next, we increase the length of each segment by a factor of $2$. Notice that each segment should have $O$ as one of its endpoints.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/45c1e442529f64fd543b56c934cf6346.png>)

Finally, we connect the endpoints of the enlarged segments using edges, and we obtain a new polygon $\mathcal P'$.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/e5c494e7b06c4a6ed895b7dfc8450f61.png>)

The new polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ has the same shape as $\mathcal{P}$, but each side has a length that is twice as long as the corresponding side length of $\mathcal P$.

The transformation we just applied is called an **enlargement** with center $O$ and scale factor $k=2$.

For an enlargement, we must have $\mid k \mid > 1$. We can split this into two cases:

- If $k$ is positive, then the original polygon $\mathcal P$ and its image $\mathcal P'$ lie in the same direction from $O$.
- If $k$ is negative, then the original polygon $\mathcal P$ and its image $\mathcal P'$ lie in the opposite directions from $O$.

We will first explore the case where $k$ is positive. The cases when $k$ is negative will be dealt with toward the end of the lesson.

<a id="finding-the-scale-factor-of-an-enlargement"></a>
## Finding the Scale Factor of an Enlargement

**Example:** The polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ is the image of the polygon $\mathcal{P}$ under an enlargement with center $O$. What is the scale factor of the enlargement?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/b0c3ef0b53be9b02a08ec73fe5d84112.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/24c3574a799d7f2aa4121cc64c2cc090.png>)

Notice that, in the diagram above, we have that

$$
\dfrac{B'C'}{BC} = \dfrac52
$$

and the same proportion holds for the other corresponding sides too. As a result, the absolute value of the scale factor $k$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid k &= \dfrac52
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the points $B$ and $B'$ lie in the same direction from $O$, the scale factor $k$ is positive. Therefore,

$$
k=\dfrac52
$$

## Contractions

Consider the polygon $\mathcal{P}$ below. How can we construct a polygon with the same shape as $\mathcal{P}$ but whose side lengths are half those of $\mathcal{P}$?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/252bbf22836df2a76e265fe2eae74a7a.png>)

First, we pick a random point $O$ in the plane. Then, we draw three line segments that connect the point $O$ to the vertices of our polygon.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/5d5bedf4afd99328666dc79778fd7831.png>)

Notice that point $O$ is a common endpoint to all three segments.

Next, we scale the length of each line segment by a factor of

$$
\dfrac 12
$$

ensuring that $O$ is an endpoint of each of our new segments.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/91725c6a0121670c70e08aeb9d493c34.png>)

Finally, we connect the endpoints of the new segments to obtain a new polygon.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/143fcdc6638417fae72b022f78b4d32c.png>)

The new polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ has the same shape as $\mathcal{P}$, but its lengths are

$$
\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

times those of $\mathcal P$.

The transformation we just applied is called a **contraction** with center $O$ and scale factor

$$
k=\dfrac 12
$$

For a contraction, we must have $\mid k \mid < 1$. Again, we can split this into two cases:

- If $k$ is positive, then the original polygon $\mathcal P$ and its image $\mathcal P'$ lie in the same direction from $O$.
- If $k$ is negative, then the original polygon $\mathcal P$ and its image $\mathcal P'$ lie in the opposite directions from $O$.

Again, we'll first concern ourselves with positive $k$ only.

Finally, we use the term **dilation** to refer to both enlargements and contractions.

<a id="finding-the-scale-factor-of-a-contraction"></a>
## Finding the Scale Factor of a Contraction

**Example:** The polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ is the image of the polygon $\mathcal{P}$ under a dilation with center $O$. What is the scale factor of the dilation?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/4442ed93d84bbea5c7906e3ac1a90932.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/c886153bebbc3c136831cf67835fdde9.png>)

Notice that, in the diagram above, we have that

$$
\dfrac{R'S'}{RS} = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac 13
$$

and the same proportion holds for the other corresponding sides too. As a result, the absolute value of the scale factor $k$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid k &= \dfrac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the points $S$ and $S'$ lie in the same direction from $O$, the scale factor $k$ is positive. Therefore,

$$
k=\dfrac{1}{3}
$$

<a id="negative-scale-factors"></a>
## Negative Scale Factors

So far, we have only considered dilations where the scale factor $k > 0$. But what happens if the scale factor is negative?

For example, let's dilate the polygon $\mathcal P$ below using a scale factor $k=-2$.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/c17f9c46aefbfc2e6be5c2131f333e82.png>)

As usual, we draw three line segments that connect the point $O$ with the vertices of our polygon.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/ab0186a7c4c661dba54c5496b0c265c9.png>)

As always, our segments have one common endpoint, the point $O$.

Next, we flip the three line segments over the point $O$ and increase the length of each segment by a factor of

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid {-2} &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

keeping the point $O$ fixed.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/dd294214eb925b94386a6758a798904e.png>)

Finally, we connect the endpoints of the dilated segments by edges and obtain a new polygon, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/08db051b07415fa782d588c4fac84b8f.png>)

The new polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ has the same shape as $\mathcal{P}$ but its lengths are twice the size of $\mathcal P$ *and* it lies in the opposite direction from $O$.

Also, the new polygon has been "flipped over." Notice that

- the rightmost edge of $\mathcal{P}$ maps to the leftmost edge of $\mathcal{P'}$,
- the top edge of $\mathcal{P}$ maps to the bottom edge of $\mathcal{P'}$,

and so on.

<a id="finding-a-negative-scale-factor-for-a-dilation"></a>
## Finding a Negative Scale Factor for a Dilation

**Example:** The polygon $\mathcal{P}'$ is the image of the polygon $\mathcal{P}$ under a dilation with center $O$. What is the scale factor of the dilation?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/fefea295b373e7eb112cd4593e412968.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/455bfd6e6168ff9ba565e69ae0cb6c44.png>)

Notice that, in the diagram above, we have that

$$
\dfrac{Q'R'}{QR} = \dfrac{2}{4}= \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

and the same proportion holds for the other corresponding sides too. As a result, the absolute value of the scale factor $k$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid k &= \dfrac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since points $R$ and $R'$ lie in opposite directions from $O$, the scale factor $k$ is negative. Therefore,

$$
k=-\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

## Finding the Center of a Dilation

**Example:** One of the polygons below is the image of the other polygon under a dilation. Which of the points shown is the center of the dilation?

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/c72e3e5ceee98b23b8dc9a72f495230b.png>)

**Explanation**

First of all, notice that our figures have opposite orientations, which means that the scale factor of the dilation is negative.

The center of a dilation lies on any line which passes through a point and its image.

Therefore, the center of our dilation can be found as, for example, the point of intersection of the lines $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{BB'}$ and $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{CC'}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.2.6. Dilations of Geometric Figures/Images/bdf921696a0f8a3dcaea389bd2c38ade.png>)

Therefore, the center of the dilation is the point $Q$.
