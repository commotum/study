## Introduction

Remember that De Moivre's theorem provides a simple way to compute powers of complex numbers in polar form. It states that

$$
\left[ \cos(\theta) + \textrm{i}\sin(\theta) \right]^n = \cos(n\theta) + \textrm{i}\sin{(n\theta)}
$$

For example, let's use De Moivre's theorem to calculate $(1+\sqrt{3}\,\textrm{i})^3$.

Let

$$
z=1+\sqrt{3}\,\textrm{i}
$$

First, we express $z$ in polar form. Computing the magnitude and argument, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid z \mid &= \sqrt{1^{2} + (\sqrt{3})^{2}} = 2, \\
\arg (z) &= \arctan (\frac{\sqrt{3}}{1}) = \frac{π}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we can write $z$ in polar form, as follows:

$$
z = 2\left[ \cos \left(\frac{\pi}{3} \right) + \textrm{i}\sin \left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) \right]
$$

Finally, applying De Moivre's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1 + \sqrt{3}\textrm{i})^{3} &= z^{3} \\
&= [2[\cos (\frac{π}{3}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{3})]]^{3} \\
&= 2^{3}[\cos (\frac{π}{3}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{3})]^{3} \\
&= 8[\cos (3 \cdot \frac{π}{3}) + \textrm{i}\sin (3 \cdot \frac{π}{3})] \\
&= 8[\cos (π) + \textrm{i}\sin (π)] \\
&= 8(-1 + \textrm{i} \cdot 0) \\
&=-8
\end{aligned}
$$

## Raising a Complex Number in Polar Form to a Power Using De Moivre’s Theorem

**Example:** Express $z$ in the form $x+\textrm{i}y$, where
$z = \left[\cos \left(\frac{\pi}{6} \right) +\textrm{i}\sin \left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) \right]^5$.

**Explanation**

We use De Moivre's theorem,

$$
(\cos\theta + \textrm{i}\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + \textrm{i}\sin{(n\theta)}
$$

with $n=5$ and

$$
\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= [\cos (\frac{π}{6}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{6})]^{5} \\
&= \cos (5 \cdot \frac{π}{6}) + \textrm{i}\sin (5 \cdot \frac{π}{6}) \\
&= \cos (\frac{5π}{6}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{5π}{6}) \\
&=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Raising a Complex Number in Cartesian Form to a Power Using De Moivre's Theorem

**Example:** Use De Moivre's Theorem to express $(1+\textrm{i})^5$ in the form $x+\textrm{i}y$.

**Explanation**

Let

$$
z=1+\textrm{i}
$$

First, we express $z$ in polar form. Computing the magnitude and argument, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid z \mid &= \sqrt{1^{2} + 1^{2}} = 2^{1/2}, \\
\arg (z) &= \arctan (\frac{1}{1}) = \frac{π}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we can write $z$ in polar form, as follows:

$$
z = 2^{1/2}\left[ \cos \left(\frac{\pi}{4} \right) +\textrm{i}\sin \left(\frac{\pi}{4} \right) \right]
$$

Finally, applying De Moivre's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1 + \textrm{i})^{5} &= z^{5} \\
&= [2^{1/2}[\cos (\frac{π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4})]]^{5} \\
&= (2^{1/2})^{5}[\cos (\frac{π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4})]^{5} \\
&= 2^{5/2}[\cos (\frac{5π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{5π}{4})] \\
&= 2^{5/2}(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\textrm{i}) \\
&= 2^{5/2}(-2^{-1/2} - 2^{-1/2}\textrm{i}) \\
&=-2^{4/2} - 2^{4/2}\textrm{i} \\
&=-2^{2} - 2^{2}\textrm{i} \\
&=-4 - 4\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

## De Moivre's Theorem for Complex Numbers in CIS Notation

Remember that the

$$
\textrm{cis}\left(\theta\right)
$$

notation is a compact notation for expressing complex numbers:

$$
\textrm{cis}\left(\theta\right) = \cos(\theta) + \textrm{i} \sin(\theta)
$$

We can write De Moivre's theorem in

$$
\textrm{cis}\left(\theta\right)
$$

notation as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\cos (θ) + \textrm{i}\sin (θ)]^{n} &= \cos (nθ) + \textrm{i}\sin (nθ) \\
&= ⇓ \mid ⇓ \\
[cis(θ)]^{n} &= cis(nθ)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, to raise a complex number in the form

$$
z=r\textrm{cis}\left(\theta\right)
$$

to the power of $n$, we can use the identity

$$
\left[r\,\textrm{cis}\left(\theta\right)\right]^n = r^n\,\textrm{cis}\left(n\theta\right)
$$

<a id="raising-a-complex-number-in-cis-notation-to-a-power-using-de-moivres-theorem"></a>
## Raising a Complex Number In CIS Notation to a Power Using De Moivre's Theorem

**Example:** Simplify $\left[3\,\textrm{cis}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\right]^3$.

**Explanation**

We use the identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
[rcis(θ)]^{n} &= r^{n}cis(nθ)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $n=3$, $r=3$, and

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

Substituting in these values, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
[3cis(\frac{π}{4})]^{3} &= 3^{3}cis(3 \cdot \frac{π}{4}) = 27cis(\frac{3π}{4})
\end{aligned}
$$

Also, we know that

$$
r\,\textrm{cis}(\theta) = r \left[ \cos (\theta) +\textrm{i}\sin(\theta) \right]
$$

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
27cis(\frac{3π}{4}) &= 27[\cos (\frac{3π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{3π}{4})] \\
&= 27(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \textrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \\
&=-\frac{27\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{27\sqrt{2}}{2}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$
