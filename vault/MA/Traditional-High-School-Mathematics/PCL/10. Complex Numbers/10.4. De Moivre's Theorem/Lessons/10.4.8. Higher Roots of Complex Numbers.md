## Introduction

Any complex number $z$ can be written in polar form as

$$
z = r\left(\cos\theta+\textrm i\sin\theta\right)
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z)
$$

Recall that the $n$th roots of $z$ are given by

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right], \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

We've seen how to compute the square roots of complex numbers. In this lesson, we'll apply the same method to compute higher roots.

<a id="finding-a-formula-for-the-roots-of-a-number-with-unit-magnitude"></a>
## Finding a Formula for the Roots of a Number With Unit Magnitude

**Example:** The complex $6$th roots of the number $z=\cos\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)$ are given by

$z_k^{1/6} = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}\cdot{k}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}\cdot{k}\right)$,

where $k=0,\,1,\,2,\,3,\,4,\,5$. What are the values of $a$ and $b$?

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r=1, \qquad \theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}, \qquad n=6
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/6} &= \sqrt[6]{1}[\cos (\frac{\frac{3π}{2} + 2πk}{6}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{3π}{2} + 2πk}{6})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{π}{4} + π 3 \cdot k) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4} + π 3 \cdot k)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
a={\color{blue}4}
$$

and

$$
b={\color{red}\dfrac{\pi}{3}}
$$

## Finding a Formula for the Roots of a Number

**Example:** The complex $4$th roots of the number $z=256\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right]$ are given by

$z_k^{1/4} = \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a} \left[ \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{c}\cdot{k}\right)+ \textrm{i} \sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{c}\cdot{k}\right) \right]$,

where $k=0,\,1,\,2,\,3$. What are the values of $a$, $b$, and $c$?

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r=256, \qquad \theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad n=4
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/4} &= \sqrt[4]{256}[\cos (\frac{\frac{π}{2} + 2πk}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{π}{2} + 2πk}{4})] \\
&= 4[\cos (\frac{π}{8} + π 2 \cdot k) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{8} + π 2 \cdot k)]
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
a={\color{purple}4}
$$

$$
b={\color{blue}8}
$$

and

$$
c={\color{red}\dfrac{\pi}{2}}
$$

<a id="finding-the-roots-of-a-complex-number"></a>
## Finding the  Roots of a Complex Number

**Example:** The fourth roots of the number $z=\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)$ are as follows:
$z_{0}^{1/4} = \cos ϕ_{0} + \textrm{i}\sin ϕ_{0}; z_{1}^{1/4} = \cos ϕ_{1} + \textrm{i}\sin ϕ_{1}; z_{2}^{1/4} = \cos ϕ_{2} + \textrm{i}\sin ϕ_{2}; z_{3}^{1/4} = \cos ϕ_{3} + \textrm{i}\sin ϕ_{3}$
Find possible values of $\phi_0$, $\phi_1,\phi_2$ and $\phi_3$.

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r=1, \qquad \theta=\dfrac{\pi}{3}, \qquad n=4
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/4} &= \sqrt[3]{1}[\cos (\frac{\frac{π}{3} + 2π \cdot k}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{π}{3} + 2π \cdot k}{4})] \\
&= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{2π \cdot k}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{2π \cdot k}{4})] \\
&= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot k}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot k}{2})]
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, setting $k=0, 1, 2,3$ gives the required roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k = 0: z_{0}^{1/4} &= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 0}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 0}{2})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{π}{12}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12}) \\
k = 1: z_{1}^{1/4} &= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 1}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 1}{2})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{7π}{12}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{7π}{12}) \\
k = 2: z_{2}^{1/4} &= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 2}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 2}{2})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{13π}{12}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{13π}{12}) \\
k = 3: z_{3}^{1/4} &= [\cos (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 3}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{12} + \frac{π \cdot 3}{2})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{19π}{12}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{19π}{12})
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\phi_0 = \dfrac{\pi}{12}, \qquad \phi_1 = \dfrac{7\pi}{12}, \qquad \phi_2 = \dfrac{13\pi}{12}\qquad \phi_3 = \dfrac{19\pi}{12}
$$
