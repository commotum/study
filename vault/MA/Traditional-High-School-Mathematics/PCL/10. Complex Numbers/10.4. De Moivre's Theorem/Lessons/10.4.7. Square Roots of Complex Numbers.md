## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to calculate the roots of complex numbers. Let's start by considering the complex number

$$
z = \textrm i
$$

Our goal is to calculate

$$
z^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{\textrm i}
$$

where $n$ is a positive integer.

The first step is to write $z$ in polar form. Since

$$
\mid z \mid = 1
$$

and

$$
\arg(z) = \dfrac\pi2
$$

we have

$$
z = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) + \textrm i\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$

Now, here's a trick. We take advantage of the periodicity of sine and cosine and write $z$ as

$$
z = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\right) + \textrm i\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k\right)
$$

where $k$ is an integer.

Taking the $n$th root of the above expression and applying De Moivre's theorem, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/n} &= [\cos (\frac{π}{2} + 2πk) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{2} + 2πk)]^{1/n} \\
&= \cos [\frac{1}{n}(\frac{π}{2} + 2πk)] + \textrm{i}\sin [\frac{1}{n}(\frac{π}{2} + 2πk)] \\
&= \cos (\frac{π}{2n} + \frac{2πk}{n}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{2n} + \frac{2πk}{n})
\end{aligned}
$$

This formula allows us to compute the $n$th roots of $\textrm i$. We write $z_k^{1/n}$ because different values of $k$ will give different roots.

**Important**: Any nonzero complex number $z$ will have precisely $n$ *distinct* $n$th roots.

---

<a id="computing-the-square-roots-of-i"></a>
## Computing the Square Roots of i

We found that the $n$th roots of $z=\textrm i$ are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/n} &= \cos (\frac{π}{2n} + \frac{2πk}{n}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{2n} + \frac{2πk}{n})
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's use this formula to compute the square roots of $\textrm i$. To do this, we substitute $n=2$ into the above, and we get

$$
z^{1/2}_k = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4} + \pi k\right) + \textrm i\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4} + \pi k\right)
$$

Finally, setting $k=0, 1$ gives the square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k = 0: z_{0}^{1/2} &= \cos (\frac{π}{4} + π \cdot 0) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4} + π \cdot 0) \\
&= \cos (\frac{π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4}) \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \textrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\
k = 1: z_{1}^{1/2} &= \cos (\frac{π}{4} + π \cdot 1) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4} + π \cdot 1) \\
&= \cos (\frac{5π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{5π}{4}) \\
&=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \textrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can stop here because we have found two distinct roots. Note that substituting

$$
k=2,3,4\ldots
$$

will give roots equal to the ones found above.

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\sqrt{\textrm i} = \pm \left(\dfrac{\sqrt 2}{2} + \textrm i\dfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}\right)
$$

<a id="a-general-formula-for-the-roots-of-a-number"></a>
## A General Formula for the Roots of a Number

Any complex number $z$ can be written in polar form as

$$
z = r\left(\cos\theta+\textrm i\sin\theta\right)
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid
$$

and

$$
\theta = \arg(z)
$$

Using techniques similar to those we've just seen, we can show that the $n$th roots of $z$ are given by

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right], \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In this lesson, we will focus on computing the *square* roots of numbers. In a future lesson, we'll look at higher roots.

## Finding a Formula for the Square Roots of a Number With Unit Magnitude

**Example:** The complex square roots of the number $z = \cos\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{5}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{5}\right)$ are given by
$z_k^{1/2} = \cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}\cdot{k}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a}}+\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}\cdot{k}\right)$,

where $k=0,\,1$. What are the values of $a$ and $b$?

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r = 1, \qquad \theta = \dfrac{4\pi}{5}, \qquad n = 2
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/2} &= \sqrt{1}[\cos (\frac{\frac{4π}{5} + 2πk}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{4π}{5} + 2πk}{2})] \\
&= \cos (\frac{2π}{5} + π \cdot k) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{2π}{5} + π \cdot k)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
a={\color{blue}5}
$$

and

$$
b={\color{red}\pi}
$$

## Finding a Formula for the Square Roots of a Number

**Example:** The complex square roots of the number $z = 9\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)\right]$ are given by
$z_k^{1/2} = \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{a} \left[\cos\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}} + \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{c}\cdot{k}\right) + \textrm{i} \sin\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{b}} + \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{c}\cdot{k}\right) \right]$,

where $k = 0, \, 1$. What are the values of $a$, $b$, and $c$?

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right) + \textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta + 2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r = 9, \qquad \theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{3}, \qquad n = 2
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/2} &= \sqrt{9}[\cos (\frac{\frac{5π}{3} + 2πk}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{5π}{3} + 2πk}{2})] \\
&= 3[\cos (\frac{5π}{6} + π \cdot k) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{5π}{6} + π \cdot k)]
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
a = {\color{purple}3}
$$

$$
b = {\color{blue}6}
$$

and

$$
c = {\color{red}\pi}
$$

## Finding the Square Roots of a Complex Number

**Example:** Find the square roots of $z=9\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{5}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{5}\right)\right]$.

**Explanation**

Any nonzero complex number $z$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots. Using de Moivre's theorem, we can write down these roots as

$$
z_{k}^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi{k}}{n}\right)\right]
$$

where

$$
r = \mid z \mid, \qquad \theta = \arg(z), \qquad k=0, 1, 2,..., n-1
$$

In our case, we have

$$
r=9, \qquad \theta=\dfrac{\pi}{5}, \qquad n=2
$$

Substituting these values into the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{k}^{1/2} &= \sqrt{9}[\cos (\frac{\frac{π}{5} + 2π \cdot k}{2}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{\frac{π}{5} + 2π \cdot k}{2})] \\
&= 3[\cos (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot k) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot k)]
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, setting $k=0, 1$ gives the square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k = 0: z_{0}^{1/2} &= 3[\cos (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot 0) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot 0)] \\
&= 3[\cos (\frac{π}{10}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{10})] \\
k = 1: z_{1}^{1/2} &= 3[\cos (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot 1) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{10} + π \cdot 1)] \\
&= 3[\cos (\frac{11π}{10}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{11π}{10})]
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="de-moivres-theorem-for-non-integer-powers"></a>
## De Moivre’s Theorem for Non-Integer Powers

Let's recall De Moivre's theorem:

$$
(\cos\theta + \textrm i \sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + \textrm i \sin n\theta
$$

De Moivre's theorem is *not* valid when $n$ is not an integer. This is because the left-hand side is multi-valued (since every complex number has multiple roots), while the right-hand side is single-valued.

However, De Moivre's theorem can be adapted to take these multiple values into account for non-integer $n$ as follows:

$$
(\cos\theta + \textrm i \sin\theta)^n = \cos\left(n(\theta+2\pi k)\right) + \textrm i \sin \left(n(\theta+2\pi k)\right)
$$

where $k$ is an integer. In this instance, each possible value of $z^n$ on the left-hand side corresponds to some value(s) of $k$ on the right-hand side. It's this idea that allows us to use De Moivre's theorem to compute the roots of complex numbers.
