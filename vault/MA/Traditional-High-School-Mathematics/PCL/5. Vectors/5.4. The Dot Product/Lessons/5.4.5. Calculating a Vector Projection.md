## Introduction

The **vector projection** of a vector $\mathbf{a}$ onto another vector $\mathbf{b}$ is the portion that the vector $\mathbf{a}$ extends along $\mathbf{b}$.

For example, consider two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ shown below. Suppose we know that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{a} &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{b} &= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

and the angle between the vectors is

$$
\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

![](<../Source/5.4.5. Calculating a Vector Projection/Images/70c45a3fa72847dea43944c218fe8fcd.png>)

The starting point of the vector projection is $O$, and the ending point will be the point $P$ that lies on the line containing $\mathbf{b}$ with $AP \perp \mathbf{b}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/5.4.5. Calculating a Vector Projection/Images/c1f6a3e248a3fcd47b9077790ee3ebb8.png>)

The vector $\overrightarrow{OP}$ is called the **vector projection** of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$. It is denoted as $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a}$, and it is computed as

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= OP \cdot u \\
&= comp_{b}a \cdot u
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a} = \mid \,\mathbf{a}\, \mid \cos\theta
$$

is the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{u}$ is a unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{b}$.

First, we compute the scalar projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 2\cos (\frac{π}{4}) \\
&= \sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we compute the unit vector that is parallel to $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mid \,\mathbf{b}\, \mid} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{3} = \dfrac{1}{3} \mathbf{b}
$$

Therefore, the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= comp_{b}a \cdot u \\
&= \sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3}b \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}b
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-direction-of-the-projection-vector"></a>
## The Direction of the Projection Vector

When finding the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$, we sometimes find that the resulting projection vector points in the opposite direction to $\mathbf{b}$. This happens when the vector $\mathbf{a}$ extends in the direction opposite to that of $\mathbf{b}$.

![](<../Source/5.4.5. Calculating a Vector Projection/Images/1a9f17a8c9a9ad3438b1098867c60a0f.png>)

Consider two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, where it is known that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \,\mathbf{a}\, &= 2,\qquad \mid \,\mathbf{b}\,|=3,\qquad \theta = 135^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's calculate $\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a}$.

First, notice that the angle between $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ is obtuse. This means we expect the projection vector to point in the opposite direction to that of $\mathbf b$.

Next, we compute the scalar projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 2\cos (135^{∘}) \\
&= 2 \cdot (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \\
&=-\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the scalar projection is negative.

Then, we find the unit vector that is parallel to $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mid \,\mathbf{b}\, \mid} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{3} = \dfrac{1}{3} \mathbf{b}
$$

Therefore, the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= comp_{b}a \cdot u \\
&=-\sqrt{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3}b \\
&=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}b
\end{aligned}
$$

This vector points in the direction opposite to $\mathbf{b}$, as expected.

<a id="calculating-a-vector-projection-using-angle-and-magnitude"></a>
## Calculating a Vector Projection Using Angle and Magnitude

**Example:** Suppose that $\mid \,\mathbf{a}\,|=8, \mid \,\mathbf{b}\,|=4$ and that the angle between the vectors is $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$. Find the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ in terms of the vector $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

First, we find the scalar projection as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 8\cos (\frac{5π}{6}) \\
&= 8 \cdot (-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) \\
&=-4\sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we find the unit vector that is parallel to $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mid \,\mathbf{b}\, \mid} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{4} = \dfrac{1}{4} \mathbf{b}
$$

Finally, we compute the vector projection as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= comp_{b}a \cdot u \\
&=-4\sqrt{3} \cdot \frac{1}{4}b \\
&=-\sqrt{3}b
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="a-general-formula"></a>
## A General Formula

We have been using the following formula for the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a} =\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{u}
$$

However, we can write an alternative formula using the formula for the scalar projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b \mid}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above into the original formula for the vector projection, and also substituting

$$
\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\mathbf{b}}{\mid \, \mathbf{b} \, \mid}
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{\begin{vmatrix}b &} \cdot \frac{b}{\mid b\end{vmatrix}} \\
&= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b \mid ^{2}}b \\
&= \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}b
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, another general formula for the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}b
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-a-vector-projection-using-components"></a>
## Calculating a Vector Projection Using Components

**Example:** Let $\mathbf{a}=\langle 1,-3,5 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{b}=\langle 5,-4,0 \rangle$. Find the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the vector projection, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}b \\
&= \frac{a_{x}b_{x} + a_{y}b_{y} + a_{z}b_{z}}{b_{x}^{2} + b_{y}^{2} + b_{z}^{2}}b \\
&= (1 \cdot 5 + (-3) \cdot (-4) + 5 \cdot 0)/(5^{2} + (-4)^{2} + 0^{2})b \\
&= \frac{17}{41}⟨5,-4, 0⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{85}{41},-\frac{68}{41}, 0⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

## Projecting Perpendicular Vectors

Notice that if two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are perpendicular, then by projecting $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ (or $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$), we obtain the zero vector.

For example, the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto a perpendicular vector $\mathbf{b}$ looks as follows:

![](<../Source/5.4.5. Calculating a Vector Projection/Images/f3bb4c9d8854af35f141a1b60005492d.png>)

Furthermore, we have the following fact:

> *If $\mathbf{a}$ is a nonzero vector, its projection onto another nonzero vector $\mathbf{b}$ equals the zero vector if and only if the vectors are perpendicular.*

---

<a id="perpendicular-vectors"></a>
## Perpendicular Vectors

**Example:** Let $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 5 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$. Find the vector projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the vector projection, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}b \\
&= \frac{a_{x}b_{x} + a_{y}b_{y} + a_{z}b_{z}}{b_{x}^{2} + b_{y}^{2} + b_{z}^{2}}b \\
&= (1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot (-1))/(1^{2} + 5^{2} + (-1)^{2})b \\
&= (0)/(1^{2} + 5^{2} + (-1)^{2})b \\
&= ⟨0, 0, 0⟩
\end{aligned}
$$
