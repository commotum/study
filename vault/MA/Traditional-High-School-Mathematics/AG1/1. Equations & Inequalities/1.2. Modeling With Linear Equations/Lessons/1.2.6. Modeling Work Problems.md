## Introduction

If a person or machine works at a constant rate for some time, we can use the following formula to calculate how much output results from the work.

$$
\text{output} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}
$$

For example, suppose an employee washes cars at a rate of $3$ cars per hour, and they work for $2$ hours. Then, they wash a total of

$$
3 \cdot 2 = 6
$$

cars, which is the total output of the employee's work.

More generally, if an employee washes cars at a rate of $3$ cars per hour and works for $t$ hours, then they wash a total of $3t$ cars. The number $3t$ represents the total output of the employee's work.

It's important to remember that the units of rate and time must match for the formula to apply. In this case, the units of $\textrm{rate}$ and $\textrm{time}$ are "cars per hour" and "hours," respectively.

We can use these ideas to model situations where two people or machines work at different rates. Let's see an example.

## Modeling Work Problems

**Example:** Suppose Agnes can clean a car in $2$ hours, and her friend Sheldon can do the same in $3$ hours. How fast can they finish the job if they work together?

**Explanation**

We will use the following formula:

$$
\text{output} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}
$$

Let $t$ be the number of hours it takes for Agnes and Sheldon to finish the job together.

- Agnes can clean a car in $2$ hours, so she works at a rate of $\dfrac12$ cars per hour. So, Agnes can clean $\dfrac{1}{2}t$ cars in $t$ hours.
- Sheldon can clean a car in $3$ hours, so he works at a rate of $\dfrac13$ cars per hour. So, Sheldon can clean $\dfrac{1}{3}t$ cars in $t$ hours.

We can organize this information in a rate–time–output table:

| Workers | Rate (cars/h) | Time (h) | Output (cars) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Agnes | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $t$ | $\dfrac{t}{2}$ |
| Sheldon | $\dfrac{1}{3}$ | $t$ | $\dfrac{t}{3}$ |

Agnes and Sheldon together will clean one car. So, the sum of their outputs is $1$:

$$
\dfrac{t}{2} + \dfrac{t}{3} = 1
$$

We solve the above equation for $t$. To eliminate the fractions, we multiply by the least common multiple of $2$ and $3$, which is $6$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
6 \cdot (\frac{t}{2} + \frac{t}{3}) &= 6 \cdot 1 \\
\frac{6t}{2} + \frac{6t}{3} &= 6 \\
3t + 2t &= 6 \\
5t &= 6 \\
t &= \frac{6}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, working together, it takes them

$$
\dfrac{6}{5} = 1\dfrac15
$$

hours to clean one car.

## Problems Featuring Opposing Work

**Example:** Bryan is filling a tire with air. Bryan can usually fill a tire in $5$ minutes. However, his pump has a leak that can release all the air from a tire in $30$ minutes. How long will it take Bryan to fill the tire using the faulty pump?

**Explanation**

We will use the following formula:

$$
\text{output} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}
$$

Let $t$ be the number of minutes Bryan takes to fill the tire.

- Bryan can fill the tire in $5$ minutes, so he fills at a rate of $\dfrac{1}{5}$ tires per minute.
- The leak empties the tire at a rate of $\dfrac{1}{30}$ tires per minute, which we can think of as filling it at a rate of $-\dfrac{1}{30}$ tires per minute.

We can organize this information in a rate-time-output table:

| Work | Rate (tires/min) | Time (min) | Output (tires) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Filling | $\dfrac{1}{5}$ | $t$ | $\dfrac{t}{5}$ |
| Leaking | $-\dfrac{1}{30}$ | $t$ | $-\dfrac{t}{30}$ |

Working against the leak, Bryan fills a single tire. So, the sum of the outputs is $1$:

$$
\dfrac{t}{5} - \dfrac{t}{30} = 1
$$

We now solve this for $t$. To eliminate the fractions, we multiply by the least common multiple of $5$ and $30$, which is $30$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
30 \cdot (\frac{t}{5} - \frac{t}{30}) &= 30 \cdot 1 \\
\frac{30t}{5} - \frac{30t}{30} &= 30 \\
6t - t &= 30 \\
5t &= 30 \\
t &= 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, Bryan can fill the tire in $6$ minutes using the faulty pump.
