## Introduction

In many real-world situations, we want to model a total using two different quantities. When each quantity has a consistent rate or cost, we can write an equation of the form

$$
ax + by = c
$$

where $x$ and $y$ represent the two unknown quantities, and $c$ is the total.

For example, suppose a school buys two types of chairs: folding chairs that cost $\[math]30$ each and desk chairs that cost $\[/math]80$ each. Suppose the school spent $\[math]2,170$ on chairs. How can we model this situation?

Let's break the problem down into explicit steps:

**Step 1**: First, we identify the *unit rates*.

- Each folding chair costs $\[/math]30$, so the total cost for $f$ folding chairs is
$30f$.
- Each desk chair costs $\[math]80$, so the total cost for $d$ desk chairs is
$80d$.

**Step 2**: Add the contributions to get the total

The total cost of all the chairs is the sum of the two parts:

$$
30f + 80d
$$

We are told the school spent $\[/math]2,170$, so we can write this as

$$
30f + 80d = 2,170
$$

The key takeaways are:

- Multiply each quantity’s *unit rate* by the *number of items.*
- Add the expressions together.
- Set the total equal to the given value.

Let's see another example.

## Creating a Two-Variable Model Representing a Total

**Example:** A health clinic administers $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of a unit of one vaccine and $\dfrac{3}{10}$ of a unit of another vaccine per patient. Let $a$ be the number of patients receiving the first vaccine and $b$ the number receiving the second. If the clinic used $36$ units of vaccine in total, which equation models this situation?

**Explanation**

We are told that $a$ represents the number of patients receiving the first vaccine, and $b$ represents the number receiving the second.

- Each patient receiving the first vaccine uses $\dfrac{2}{5}$ of a unit, so the total amount used from that vaccine is $\dfrac{2}{5}a$.
- Each patient receiving the second vaccine uses $\dfrac{3}{10}$ of a unit, so the total amount used from that vaccine is $\dfrac{3}{10}b$.

To find the total amount of vaccine used, we add these two expressions together:

$$
\dfrac{2}{5}a + \dfrac{3}{10}b
$$

This total must equal the clinic’s total usage of $36$ units, so we write

$$
\dfrac{2}{5}a + \dfrac{3}{10}b = 36
$$

This is the equation that models the situation.

## Solving for a Variable

Suppose we have a linear model of the form

$$
ax + by = c
$$

where $x$ and $y$ are unknown quantities, and $c$ is the total.

We can often solve for one variable if we know the value of the other.

For example, suppose a theater collected $\[math]460$ in ticket sales. Adult tickets cost $\[/math]25$ and child tickets cost $\[math]10$. If the theater sold $8$ adult tickets, how many child tickets were sold?

The equation

$$
25a + 10c = 460
$$

models the total revenue, where $a$ is the number of adult tickets and $c$ is the number of child tickets.

We are told that $a = 8$. Substituting this into the equation gives

$$
25(8) + 10c = 460
$$

Now, we simplify the left-hand side. This gives

$$
200 + 10c = 460
$$

To solve for the unknown, we subtract $200$ from both sides:

$$
10c = 260
$$

Then, we divide both sides by $10{:}$

$$
c = 26
$$

Therefore, the theater sold $26$ child tickets.

The key takeaways for solving for a variable are:

- First, substitute the known value.
- Then, simplify the equation.
- Finally, solve for the unknown variable.

<a id="solving-for-a-variable-in-a-two-variable-model"></a>
## Solving for a Variable in a Two-Variable Model

**Example:** A smoothie shop uses $0.75$ pounds of strawberries and $1.2$ pounds of bananas in each batch. The equation $0.75s + 1.2b = 24$ models the total pounds of fruit used in $s$ batches with strawberries and $b$ batches with bananas. If $s = 8$, how many banana batches were made?

**Explanation**

We are told that $s = 8$. We will substitute this value into the equation and solve for $b$.

First, we start with the original equation:

$$
0.75s + 1.2b = 24
$$

Next, we substitute $s = 8$ and solve the resulting equation for $b{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.75(8) + 1.2b &= 24 \\
6 + 1.2b &= 24 \\
1.2b &= 24 - 6 \\
1.2b &= 18 \\
b &= \frac{18}{1.2} \\
b &= 15
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $15$ banana batches were made.
