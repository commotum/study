<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to translate constraints into systems of linear inequalities, represent them symbolically, and interpret their solutions. Let's start with an example.

Suppose a bakery manager is buying $x$ trays and $y$ boxes for a catering order. The manager needs to purchase at least $36$ items in total, but wants to spend no more than $\[math]180$. Each tray costs $\[/math]8$ and each box costs $\[math]2$.

To model this situation, we translate each condition into an inequality:

- The total number of items is equal to $x+y$. There must be at least $36$ items in total, so we have
$x + y \geq 36$.
- Since each tray costs $\[/math]8$ and each box costs $\[math]2$, their total cost is $8x + 2y$. We're told that it cannot exceed $\[/math]180$. So we have
$8x + 2y \leq 180$.

Therefore, putting it all together, the system of inequalities that models this situation is the following:

$$
{x + y \ge 36; 8x + 2y \le 180
$$

This system models all possible choices of trays and boxes that both satisfy the minimum item requirement and stay within the budget.

**Watch out!** We consider only non-negative pairs, as the number of items can't be negative.

---

<a id="checking-a-possible-solution-to-a-system"></a>
## Checking a Possible Solution to a System

For the system

$$
{x + y \ge 36; 8x + 2y \le 180
$$

every pair $(x,y)$ with

$$
x,y \geq 0
$$

that satisfies both inequalities represents a solution of the system. For example, consider the pair

$$
(x,y) = (10,40)
$$

Substituting into both inequalities, we obtain the following:

$$
{10 + 40 \ge 36; 8(10) + 2(40) \le 180
$$

Since both inequalities are satisfied, the point $(10,40)$ is a solution.
Thus, buying $10$ trays and $40$ boxes is a valid option for the manager in our original problem.

Now, consider

$$
(x,y) = (10,80)
$$

Substituting into both inequalities, we obtain the following:

$$
{10 + 80 \ge 36; 8(10) + 2(80) \le 180
$$

Since the second inequality does not hold, the point $(10,80)$ is *not* a solution.
Thus, buying $10$ trays and $80$ boxes is not a valid option for the manager in our original problem.

<a id="strict-vs-non-strict-inequalities"></a>
## Strict vs. Non-Strict Inequalities

In the bakery example from the beginning of the lesson, we wrote the system

$$
{x + y \ge 36; 8x + 2y \le 180
$$

because the problem said that the manager wanted

- to buy *at least $36$ items*, and
- to spend *no more than $\[math]180$.*

Both these phrases lead to *non-strict inequalities* (

$$
\geq
$$

,

$$
\leq
$$

).

But if the manager instead wants *more than $36$ items*, then the first inequality in the system becomes strict, as follows:

$$
{x + y>36; 8x + 2y \le 180
$$

The most common phrases and the corresponding inequality symbols are listed in the table below.

| **Phrasing** | **Symbol** |
| --- | ---: |
| at least, no less than | $\geq$ |
| at most, no more than | $\leq$ |
| more than, greater than | $\gt$ |
| less than, fewer than | $\lt$ |

<a id="modeling-a-situation-using-two-inequalities"></a>
## Modeling a Situation Using Two Inequalities

**Example:** A bookstore is purchasing boxes of paperbacks and hardcovers from a distributor. The delivery van can carry no more than $275$ pounds in a single trip. Each box of paperbacks weighs $4.2$ pounds, and each box of hardcovers weighs $7.5$ pounds. The store also wants to buy at least twice as many boxes of paperbacks as boxes of hardcovers. Let $p$ represent the number of paperback boxes and $h$ represent the number of hardcover boxes, where $p$ and $h$ are nonnegative integers. Write a system of inequalities that best models this situation.

**Explanation**

First, we translate each condition into an inequality, as follows:

- The total weight cannot exceed $275$ pounds. Since each paperback box weighs $4.2$ pounds and each hardcover box weighs $7.5$ pounds, we have
$4.2p + 7.5h \leq 275$.
- The store wants the number of paperback boxes to be at least twice the number of hardcover boxes, so we have
$p \geq 2h$.

Therefore, putting it all together, the system of inequalities that models this situation is the following:

$$
{4.2p + 7.5h \le 275; p \ge 2h
$$

## Modeling Using Two Inequalities and Checking a Possible Solution

**Example:** An art teacher is buying $t$ canvases and $u$ paintbrush sets for a school workshop. The teacher needs to purchase at least $28$ items in total, but wants to spend no more than $\[math]360$. Each canvas costs $\[/math]12$ and each paintbrush set costs $\[math]6$.

Write a system of inequalities that models this situation, and check whether the pair $(t,u)=(10,20)$ is valid for your system or not.

**Explanation**

First, we translate each part of the problem into an inequality:

- There must be at least $28$ items in total, so we have
$t + u \geq 28$.
- We're told that the total cost cannot exceed $\[math]360$. Since each canvas costs $\[/math]12$ and each paintbrush set costs $\[math]6$, we have
$12t + 6u \leq 360$.

Therefore, putting it all together, the system of inequalities that models this situation is the following:

$$
{t + u \ge 28; 12 t + 6 u \le 360
$$

To check if the pair

$$
(t,u)=(10,20)
$$

is a solution of the system, we substitute it into the inequalities:

$$
{10 + 20 \ge 28; 12(10) + 6(20) \le 360
$$

Since all inequalities are satisfied, the pair $\bbox[4pt, border: 1px solid gainsboro]{\text{is}}$ a solution to the system.

Thus, purchasing $10$ canvases and $20$ paintbrush sets $\bbox[4pt, border: 1px solid gainsboro]{\text{is}}$ a valid option for the teacher.

<a id="modeling-with-multiple-constraints"></a>
## Modeling With Multiple Constraints

Sometimes, a situation has *more than two conditions* that must be satisfied. Let’s return to the bakery manager example from the beginning of the lesson. The manager wanted

- to buy at least $36$ items, and
- to spend no more than $\[math]180$.

Recall that the system of inequalities that models this situation looked as follows:

$$
{x + y \ge 36; 8x + 2y \le 180
$$

In addition to these conditions, suppose the manager also decides to buy:

- more than $5$ trays, which can be written as
${\color{blue}x > 5}$.
- no more than $50$ boxes, which can be written as
${\color{red}y \leq 50}$.

Therefore, the full system is now the following:

$$
{x + y \ge 36; 8x + 2y \le 180; x > 5; y \le 50
$$

Let's see some more examples.

<a id="modeling-a-situation-using-multiple-inequalities"></a>
## Modeling a Situation Using Multiple Inequalities

**Example:** A logistics company is hiring $r$ drivers and $l$ loaders for a regional operation. The company must hire at least $24$ people in total, but wants to keep the total weekly pay at no more than $\[math]28{,}400$. Each driver will be paid $\[/math]1{,}050$ per week, each loader will be paid $\[math]650$ per week, and the company must hire at least $9$ drivers and at least $8$ loaders.

Write a system of inequalities that models this situation, and check whether the pair $(r,l)=(12,12)$ is valid for your system or not.

**Explanation**

First, we translate each condition into an inequality:

- We're told that the total weekly pay cannot exceed $\[math]28{,}400$. Since each driver costs $\[/math]1{,}050$ and each loader costs $\[math]650$, we have
$1{,}050r + 650l \leq 28{,}400$.
- The total number of people hired must be at least $24$, so we have
$r + l \geq 24$.
- The company must hire at least $9$ drivers, so we have
$r \geq 9$.
- The company must also hire at least $8$ loaders, so we have
$l \geq 8$.

Thus, putting it all together, the system of inequalities that models this situation is the following:

$$
{1,050 r + 650 l \le 28,400; r + l \ge 24; r \ge 9; l \ge 8
$$

To check if the pair

$$
(r,l)=(12,12)
$$

is a solution of the system, we substitute it into the inequalities:

$$
{1,050(12) + 650(12) \le 28,400; 12 + 12 \ge 24; 12 \ge 9; 12 \ge 8
$$

Since all inequalities are satisfied, the pair $\bbox[4pt, border: 1px solid gainsboro]{\text{is}}$ a solution to the system.

Thus, hiring $12$ drivers and $12$ loaders $\bbox[4pt, border: 1px solid gainsboro]{\text{is}}$ a valid option for the company.
