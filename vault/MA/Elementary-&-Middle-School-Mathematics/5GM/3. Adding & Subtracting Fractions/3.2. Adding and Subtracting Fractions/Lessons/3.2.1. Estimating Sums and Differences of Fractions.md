## Introduction

We can *estimate* fraction addition and subtraction by rounding each fraction to a **benchmark**. Usually, we use the following benchmarks:

$$
0,\qquad \dfrac{1}{2}, \qquad 1
$$

When comparing fractions with benchmarks, there are typically three cases:

- If the numerator is *much smaller* than the denominator, such as with $\dfrac{1}{6}$, we round the fraction to $0$.
- If the numerator is *about one half* of the denominator, such as with $\dfrac{3}{8}$, we round the fraction to $\dfrac 1 2$.
- If the numerator is *very close* to the denominator, such as with $\dfrac{5}{6}$, we round the fraction to $1$.

Let's check out some examples.

---

<a id="estimating-a-sum-of-fractions-using-benchmarks-of-zero-and-one-half"></a>
## Estimating a Sum of Fractions Using Benchmarks of Zero and One-Half

**Example:** Using the benchmarks $0$ and $\dfrac 1 2$, estimate the value of $\dfrac{7}{13} + \dfrac{1}{9}$.

**Explanation**

We round each fraction to its closest benchmark. Then, we add the benchmarks.

- The fraction $\dfrac{7}{13}$ is close to one-half because the numerator $(7)$ is approximately one-half of the denominator $(13)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{7}{13}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{2}}}$
- The fraction $\dfrac{1}{9}$ is close to zero because the numerator $(1)$ is much smaller than the denominator $(9)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{1}{9}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{0}}$

Next, we add the benchmarks:

$$
{\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{2}}} + {\color{blue}{0}} = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\dfrac{7}{13} + \dfrac{1}{9}\approx \dfrac12
$$

Remember that the symbol

$$
\approx
$$

means "is approximately equal to."

## Estimating a Sum of Fractions Using Benchmarks of Zero, One-Half, and One

**Example:** Using the benchmarks $0, \dfrac{1}{2}$, and $1$, estimate the value of $\dfrac{7}{8} + \dfrac{1}{15}$.

**Explanation**

We round each fraction to its closest benchmark. Then, we add the benchmarks.

- The fraction $\dfrac{7}{8}$ is close to one because the numerator $(7)$ is close to the denominator $(8)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{7}{8}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{1}}$
- The fraction $\dfrac{1}{15}$ is close to zero because the numerator $(1)$ is much smaller than the denominator $(15)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{1}{15}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{0}}$

Next, we add the benchmarks:

$$
{\color{blue}{1}} + {\color{blue}{0}} = 1
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\dfrac{7}{8} + \dfrac{1}{15} \approx \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}1}
$$

Remember that the symbol

$$
\approx
$$

means "is approximately equal to."

## Estimating a Difference of Fractions Using Benchmarks

**Example:** Using the benchmarks $0,\, \dfrac{1}{2}$, and $1$, estimate the value of $\dfrac{7}{12} - \dfrac{9}{16}$.

**Explanation**

We round each fraction to its closest benchmark. Then, we subtract the benchmarks.

- The fraction $\dfrac{7}{12}$ is close to one-half because the numerator $(7)$ is approximately one-half of the denominator $(12)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{7}{12}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{2}}}$

- The fraction $\dfrac{9}{16}$ is close to one-half because the numerator $(9)$ is approximately one-half of the denominator $(16)$. So, we round as follows:
$\dfrac{9}{16}\rightarrow {\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{2}}}$

Next, we subtract the benchmarks:

$$
{\color{blue}{\dfrac12}} - {\color{blue}{\dfrac12}} = 0
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\dfrac{7}{12} - \dfrac{9}{16} \approx 0
$$

Remember that the symbol

$$
\approx
$$

means "is approximately equal to."
