## Introduction

Suppose Bethany promised to work for

$$
9\,\dfrac23\,\rm{hr}
$$

this week in her mother's store. If she worked for

$$
2\,\dfrac13\,\rm{hr}
$$

in the first two days, how many hours does she have left to work over the remaining days?

To find the number of hours she has remaining, we need to calculate the following difference:

$$
9\,{\color{black}\dfrac23} - 2\,{\color{black}\dfrac13}
$$

To subtract mixed numbers with like denominators, we subtract

- whole numbers from whole numbers, and
- fractions from fractions.

Subtracting the whole numbers, we get

$$
9-2={\color{blue}7}
$$

Subtracting the fractions, we get

$$
\dfrac23 - \dfrac13 = \dfrac{2-1}3 = {\color{red}\dfrac13}
$$

Finally, we combine the resulting whole number and the fraction:

$$
9\,\dfrac23 - 2\,\dfrac13 = {\color{blue}7}\,{\color{red}\dfrac13}
$$

Therefore, this week, Bethany has to work a further

$$
7\,\dfrac13\,\rm{hr}
$$

.

## Subtracting Mixed Numbers in Context

**Example:** Jane and Ricky picked some raspberries. Jane picked $8\,\dfrac{7}{8}\,\textrm{kg}$ of raspberries, and Ricky picked $2\,\dfrac{5}{8}\,\textrm{kg}$ less than Jane. How many kilograms of raspberries did Ricky pick?

**Explanation**

To find the mass of the raspberries that Ricky picked, we need to calculate the difference

$$
8\,\dfrac{7}{8} - 2\,\dfrac{5}{8}
$$

To subtract mixed numbers with like denominators, we subtract

- whole numbers from whole numbers, and
- fractions from fractions.

With that in mind, let's subtract our mixed numbers.

- Subtracting the whole numbers, we get
$8 - 2 = {\color{red}6}$.
- Subtracting the fractions, we get
$\dfrac{7}{8} - \dfrac{5}{8} = \dfrac{7-5}{8} = \dfrac{2}{8}$.
We can simplify this fraction by dividing the numerator and denominator by $2{:}$
$\dfrac{2}{8} = \dfrac{2 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = {\color{blue}\dfrac{1}{4}}$

Finally, we combine the resulting whole number and the fraction:

$$
8\,\dfrac{7}{8} - 2\,\dfrac{5}{8} = {\color{red}6}\,{\color{blue}\dfrac{1}{4}}
$$

Therefore, Ricky picked

$$
6\,\dfrac{1}{4}\,\textrm{kg}
$$

of raspberries.

## Subtracting Mixed Numbers Using Improper Fractions

Sometimes, we must write each mixed number as an improper fraction before subtracting.

To demonstrate, let's return to the example of Bethany and her mother's store.

Suppose Bethany promised to work for

$$
8\,\dfrac25\,\rm{hr}
$$

next week in her mother's store. If she plans to work for

$$
3\,\dfrac45\,\rm{hr}
$$

on Monday, how many hours must she work over the remaining days next week?

To determine the number of hours Bethany must work over the remaining days, we need to calculate the following difference:

$$
8\,{\color{blue}\dfrac25} - 3\,{\color{red}\dfrac45}
$$

Now, notice that

$$
{\color{blue}\dfrac25} < {\color{red}\dfrac45}
$$

Therefore, we *cannot* subtract the fractional parts!

So instead, we'll write each mixed number as an improper fraction and then subtract them:

- Writing $8\,\dfrac25$ as an improper fraction, we have
$\dfrac{(8\times5)+2}5 = \dfrac{42}5$.
- Writing $3\,\dfrac45$ as an improper fraction, we have
$\dfrac{(3\times5)+4}5 = \dfrac{19}5$.

We can now carry out the subtraction in the usual way:

$$
\dfrac{42}5 - \dfrac{19}5 = \dfrac{23}5
$$

Finally, we convert it back to a mixed number:

$$
23 \div 5 = 4\,\textrm{R}3
$$

Therefore, Bethany has to work a further

$$
4\,\dfrac35\,\rm{hr}
$$

<a id="subtracting-mixed-numbers-using-improper-fractions-in-context"></a>
## Subtracting Mixed Numbers Using Improper Fractions in Context

**Example:** At the beginning of the day, a grocery store had $25\,\dfrac{1}{6}\,\textrm{kg}$ of sugar for sale. If $14\,\dfrac{5}{6}\,\textrm{kg}$ of sugar were sold during the day, how much sugar is left?

**Explanation**

To find the weight of the remaining sugar, we need to calculate the difference

$$
25\,\dfrac{1}{6} - 14\,\dfrac{5}{6}
$$

We cannot subtract the fractional parts because

$$
\dfrac{5}{6}
$$

is larger than

$$
\dfrac{1}{6}
$$

So, instead, we write each number as an improper fraction.

- Writing $25\,\dfrac{1}{6}$ as an improper fraction, we have
$\dfrac{(25 \times 6) + 1}{6} = \dfrac{151}{6}$.
- Writing $14\,\dfrac{5}{6}$ as an improper fraction, we have
$\dfrac{(14 \times 6) + 5}{6} = \dfrac{89}{6}$.

We can now carry out the subtraction:

$$
\dfrac{151}{6} - \dfrac{89}{6} = \dfrac{151 - 89}{6} = \dfrac{62}{6}
$$

We can simplify

$$
\dfrac{62}{6}
$$

by dividing the numerator and denominator by $2{:}$

$$
\dfrac{62}{6} = \dfrac{62 \div 2}{6 \div 2} = \dfrac{31}{3}
$$

Finally, we convert it back to a mixed number:

$$
31 \div 3 = 10\,\textrm{R}1
$$

Therefore, there is

$$
10\,\dfrac{1}{3}\,\textrm{kg}
$$

of sugar left.
