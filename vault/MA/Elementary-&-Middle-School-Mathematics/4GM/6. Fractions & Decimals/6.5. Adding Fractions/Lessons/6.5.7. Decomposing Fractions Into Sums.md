## Introduction

Let's consider the following decomposition of the fraction

$$
\dfrac67{:}
$$

$$
{\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{7}}} + \bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\phantom{\dfrac{a}{a}}} + {\color{red}\dfrac{2}{7}} = \dfrac{6}{7}
$$

To determine the missing fraction, we first decompose

$$
\dfrac67
$$

into the sum of $6$ instances of

$$
\dfrac17{:}
$$

$$
6
$$

Examining the known fractions on the left-hand side of the given equation, from left to right, we have the following:

- To get $\color{blue}\dfrac{1}{7}$, we need $1$ instance of $\dfrac{1}{7}$
- To get $\color{red}\dfrac{2}{7}$, we need $2$ instances of $\dfrac{1}{7}$

Let's add these to our diagram:

$$
6
$$

We have $3$ instances of

$$
\dfrac17
$$

remaining, and these give the fraction

$$
{\color{violet}{\dfrac37}}
$$

$$
6
$$

Therefore, we obtain the decomposition

$$
{\color{blue}\dfrac{1}{7}} + \bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\color{violet}\dfrac{3}{7}} + {\color{red}\dfrac{2}{7}} = \dfrac{6}{7}
$$

## Finding a Missing Fraction in a Decomposition

**Example:** What fraction gives a true statement when inserted into the blank space?

$\dfrac{2}{10} + \dfrac{4}{10} + \bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\phantom{\dfrac{a}{a}}}= \dfrac{9}{10}$

**Explanation**

First, recall that

$$
\dfrac{9}{10}
$$

can be viewed as the sum of $9$ instances of the fraction

$$
\dfrac{1}{10}{:}
$$

$$
9
$$

Examining the known fractions on the left-hand side of the given equation, from left to right, we have the following:

- $2$ instances of $\dfrac{1}{10}$ gives the fraction $\color{blue}\dfrac{2}{10}$
- $4$ instances of $\dfrac{1}{10}$ gives the fraction $\color{red}\dfrac{4}{10}$

Let's add these to our diagram:

$$
9
$$

We have $3$ instances of

$$
\dfrac1{10}
$$

remaining, and these give the fraction

$$
{\color{violet}{\dfrac3{10}}}
$$

$$
9
$$

This gives the decomposition

$$
{\color{blue}\dfrac{2}{10}} + {\color{red}\dfrac{4}{10}}+ \bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\color{violet}\dfrac{3}{10}} = \dfrac{9}{10}
$$

Therefore, the missing fraction is

$$
{\color{violet}{\dfrac3{10}}}
$$

## Properties of Fraction Decomposition

To check that a particular decomposition of a fraction is valid, we need to check the following:

- All denominators in the decomposition must equal the denominator of the fraction.
- The sum of numerators of the decomposition must equal the numerator of the fraction.

Let's see an example.

---

<a id="identifying-decompositions-of-a-given-fraction"></a>
## Identifying Decompositions of a Given Fraction

**Example:** Which of the following are decompositions of the fraction $\dfrac{7}{10}$?

1. $\dfrac{1}{10}+\dfrac{1}{10}+\dfrac{2}{10}+\dfrac{3}{10}$
2. $\dfrac{2}{10}+\dfrac{3}{10}+\dfrac{4}{10}$
3. $\dfrac{4}{11}+\dfrac{3}{11}$

**Explanation**

Let's examine each expression in turn.

- I is a decomposition of $\dfrac{7}{10}$. Indeed, each fraction in the expression has a denominator of $10$, and the sum of numerators is $7$.
$\dfrac{1}{10}+\dfrac{1}{10}+\dfrac{2}{10}+\dfrac{3}{10} = \dfrac{1+1+2+3}{10} = \dfrac{7}{10}$
- II is *not* a decomposition of $\dfrac{7}{10}$. Indeed, each fraction in the expression has a denominator of $10$, but the sum of numerators is $9$.
$\dfrac{2}{10}+\dfrac{3}{10}+\dfrac{4}{10} = \dfrac{2+3+4}{10} = \dfrac{9}{10}$
- III is *not* a decomposition of $\dfrac{7}{10}$. Notice that each fraction in the expression has a denominator of $11$. As a result, this cannot be a decomposition of a fraction with a denominator of $10$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only."

## Decompositions of Mixed Numbers

How can we find a fraction decomposition of the mixed number

$$
2 \, \dfrac{3}{7}
$$

First, recall that

$$
2 \, \dfrac{3}{7}
$$

can be viewed as the sum of its whole and fractional parts:

$$
2 \, \dfrac{3}{7} = 2 + \dfrac{3}{7}
$$

In turn, the whole number $2$ can be written as

$$
2 = 1 + 1 = \dfrac{7}{7} + \dfrac{7}{7}
$$

So, we have

$$
2 \, \dfrac{3}{7} = \underbrace{\dfrac{7}{7} + \dfrac{7}{7}}_{\large 2} + \dfrac{3}{7}
$$

which is a fraction decomposition of

$$
2\,\dfrac37
$$

Now, adding these fractions to make a mixed number, we get

$$
\dfrac{7}{7} + \dfrac{7}{7} + \dfrac{3}{7} = \dfrac{17}{7}
$$

Therefore, we can make more decompositions of

$$
2\,\dfrac37
$$

by adding together fractions whose denominator is $7$ and whose numerators add to $17$. For example,

$$
\dfrac{5}{7} + \dfrac{9}{7} + \dfrac{3}{7} = \dfrac{17}{7}
$$

<a id="finding-a-missing-fraction-in-a-mixed-number-decomposition"></a>
## Finding a Missing Fraction in a Mixed Number Decomposition

**Example:** $\bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\phantom{\dfrac{a}{a}}} + \dfrac{2}{4} + \dfrac{6}{4} = 3 \, \dfrac{1}{4}$

Which of the following fractions gives a true statement when inserted into the blank space?

1. $\dfrac{5}{4}$
2. $\dfrac{3}{4}$
3. $\dfrac{4}{6}$

**Explanation**

First, recall that

$$
3 \, \dfrac{1}{4}
$$

can be viewed as the sum of the whole and fractional parts:

$$
3 \, \dfrac{1}{4} = 3 + \dfrac{1}{4}
$$

In turn, the whole number $3$ can be written as

$$
3 = 1 + 1 + 1= \dfrac{4}{4} + \dfrac{4}{4} + \dfrac{4}{4}
$$

So, we have

$$
3 \, \dfrac{1}{4} = \underbrace{\dfrac{4}{4} + \dfrac{4}{4} + \dfrac{4}{4}}_{\large 3} + \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{4+4+4+1}{4} = \dfrac{\color{blue}13}{4}
$$

As a result, any decomposition of

$$
3 \, \dfrac{1}{4}
$$

must contain fractions

- whose denominator is $4$, and
- the sum of all numerators gives ${\color{blue}13}$.

Among the given options, the only fraction with these properties is

$$
\dfrac{5}{4}
$$

Indeed, its denominator is $4$ and

$$
\bbox[2pt, white, border: 1px solid black]{\dfrac{5}{4}} + \dfrac{2}{4} + \dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{5+2+6}{4} = \dfrac{\color{blue}13}{4}
$$
