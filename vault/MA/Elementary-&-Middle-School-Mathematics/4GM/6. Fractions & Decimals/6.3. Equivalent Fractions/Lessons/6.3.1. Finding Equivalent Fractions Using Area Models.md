## Introduction

Two fractions are **equivalent** (e-quiv-a-lent) if their area models have the same shape and have the same shaded area.

For example, let's take a look at the fractions below.

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/1e728852af45ccdf2721b0d6e9e62eab.png>)

Notice that

- both models have the *same* shape, and
- they have the *same* shaded area.

Therefore, these fractions are equivalent. This means they represent the same number, and we can put an "equals" sign between them.

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/406e6c48096ada815e3b427773a6b88b.png>)

## Writing an Equation Containing Equivalent Fractions

We have the following equivalent fractions:

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/5716b40192028a76900bcbc8c0d56c8e.png>)

Let's use this to write down an equation with numbers.

- For the shape on the left:There are $10$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $2$ parts are shaded. So, $\color{SteelBlue}\dfrac{2}{10}$ of the shape is shaded.
- For the shape on the right:There are $5$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $1$ part is shaded. So, $\color{Purple}\dfrac{1}{5}$ of the shape is shaded.

Since the two fractions are equivalent, they represent the same number, and we can write down the following equation:

$$
{\color{SteelBlue}\dfrac{2}{10}} = {\color{Purple}\dfrac{1}{5}}
$$

## Determining the Missing Value Given Two Equivalent Fraction Models

**Example:** What number is missing from the statement below?

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/2e48ba1c9b086c717289a0ca3706db16.png>)

$0$

**Explanation**

Let's compare the two shapes.

- For the shape on the left:There are $3$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $2$ parts are shaded. So, $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of the shape is shaded.
- For the shape on the right:There are $6$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $4$ parts are shaded. So, $\dfrac{4}{6}$ of the shape is shaded.

Both shaded regions have the same area. Therefore, the two fractions are equivalent, and we obtain the following:

$$
4
$$

Therefore, the missing value is $\color{blue}4$.

## Determining a Fraction Given Two Equivalent Fraction Models

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/c30ac6a8f4243a9f4bc91566290cb0b3.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's compare the two shapes.

- For the shape on the left:There are $10$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $8$ parts are shaded. So, $\dfrac{8}{10}$ of the shape is shaded.
- For the shape on the right:There are $5$ parts of the shape in total. Of them, $4$ parts are shaded. So, $\dfrac{4}{5}$ of the shape is shaded.

<a id="creating-equivalent-fractions-by-adding-more-parts"></a>
## Creating Equivalent Fractions by Adding More Parts

The following fraction model represents

$$
\dfrac12
$$

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/d40d6efc2d194790243853a7d9a5f8ab.png>)

We can generate equivalent fractions by splitting the shape into more equal parts.

For example, if we draw a horizontal line, we get the following model representing

$$
\dfrac24
$$

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/c5b951e11b2749f1e4402faef6be392c.png>)

We can continue to split the fraction into equal parts to generate more equivalent fractions.

For example, if we draw two more horizontal lines, we get a model representing

$$
\dfrac48
$$

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/686a5efee4b2a74a9cfe0d43fa1afbe9.png>)

Therefore, the following fractions are all equivalent:

$$
\dfrac12, \qquad \dfrac24, \qquad \dfrac48
$$

**Watch Out!** Whenever we split a fraction model into more equal parts to generate equivalent fractions, *all* parts must have the same area!

<a id="creating-equivalent-fractions-using-fewer-parts"></a>
## Creating Equivalent Fractions Using Fewer Parts

We can also create equivalent fractions by removing some parts, where possible.

For example, let's look at the following fraction, representing

$$
\dfrac24
$$

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/1841033cf1c017365c227d7decb2b446.png>)

If we *remove* the horizontal line, we'll generate an equivalent fraction representing

$$
\dfrac12
$$

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/529c11a4a1acb79ae3a764f6480c3742.png>)

Therefore, we conclude once again that the following fractions are equivalent:

$$
\dfrac12, \qquad \dfrac24
$$

<a id="determining-an-equivalent-fraction-given-a-fraction-model"></a>
## Determining an Equivalent Fraction Given a Fraction Model

**Example:** The fraction given below is equivalent to the fraction shown in the picture. What number is missing?

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/1beda2ef2e7e203f71dcb19c00b478ff.png>)

$0$

**Explanation**

There are $4$ equal parts in the given shape in total. Of them, $3$ parts are shaded. So,

$$
\dfrac{3}{4}
$$

of the shape is shaded.

Since we are looking for a fraction of the form $0$, we now draw another shape that has the same shaded area but is divided into $8$ equal parts. To do that, we subdivide each part into $2$ equal pieces:

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Finding Equivalent Fractions Using Area Models/Images/16fd3a447b5ffcdddfe8f6418107e25a.png>)

The shape on the right is divided into $8$ equal parts. Of them, $6$ parts are shaded. So,

$$
\dfrac{6}{8}
$$

of the shape is shaded.

Hence, we obtain
$6$

Therefore, the missing number is $\color{blue}6$.
