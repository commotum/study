## Introduction

Suppose that the base of a solid is the region enclosed between the curve

$$
y=\sqrt{9-x^2}
$$

and the $x$-axis from $x=0$ to $x=3$, as shown in the picture. Given that cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are equilateral triangles, how can we find the volume of the solid?

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/e6373a74d7bd8eed739190d7892ddfcb.png>)

A sketch with a few cross-sections is shown in the picture below.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/bf476f47590829bbc29c67feb74f2bd2.png>)

Consider the picture with only one cross-section that passes through $x \in (0,3)$:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/5e0db743bd42b54a116a707069a9d29f.png>)

If we project the triangular cross-section onto the $xy$-plane, we get the picture below:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/88517cfe27e1cfff2c4f1161ac55a68c.png>)

Then, the base of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \mid y|= \sqrt{9 - x^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Given a side $a$ of an equilateral triangle, we can show that the corresponding area is given by

$$
A = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} a^2
$$

So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^{2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(\sqrt{9 - x^{2}})^{2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(9 - x^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now write the volume of the solid, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}∫_{0}^{3}(9 - x^{2})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluating the integral above gives the required volume.

## A Summary of Useful Triangle Formulas

Recall that the area $A$ of a triangle is given by

$$
A = \dfrac12 \cdot \textrm{base} \cdot \textrm{height}
$$

We can use this formula to derive some further formulas for the areas of special triangles. We will make use of these formulas in this lesson.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/97f9e5fd4fedb01e1b3390e6422e0088.png>)

- For an equilateral triangle with side length $a$ (shown above), the area is given by the formula
$A = \dfrac{\sqrt 3}{4}a^2$.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/49759f78a5d518335ffdeadbc2392e9e.png>)

- For an isosceles right triangle with leg $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the areas can be found using the following formulas:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}b^{2} \\
A &= \frac{1}{4}c^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

We will give derivations of these formulas at the end of the lesson.

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by a Curve and the X-Axis

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region enclosed between the curve $y=-\sqrt{-x}$ and the $x$-axis from $x=-3$ to $x=-1$, as shown in the picture. Given that cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are isosceles right triangles with the hypotenuse lying on the $xy$-plane, calculate the volume of the solid.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/3d319e453c8bfd6d3f00cf1624fae1c5.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider a cross-section at some point $x$, where $x \in (-3,-1)$, and its projection on the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/5e3ac8e8215945e01b2ccf15af0e37ea.png>)

Then the base of the cross-section is

$$
c = \begin{vmatrix}y & = \left & -\sqrt{-x}\right\end{vmatrix} = \sqrt{-x}
$$

Given the hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle, the corresponding area can be found using the formula

$$
A =\dfrac{c^2}{4}
$$

So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= \frac{1}{4}c^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{-x})^{2} \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}x
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now calculate the volume of the solid, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{-3}^{-1}(-\frac{1}{4}x)dx \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}∫_{-3}^{-1}xdx \\
&=-\frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{x^{2}}{2} \mid _{-3}^{-1} \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}(((-1)^{2})/(2) - ((-3)^{2})/(2)) \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by Two Curves

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region enclosed between the curves $y=x^2-1$ and $y=-x^2+1$, as shown in the picture. If cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are equilateral triangles, which of the following gives the volume of the solid?

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/a3d03cdf7dba989352fd1ee6e4773035.png>)

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(x)=-x^2+1
$$

and $g(x)=x^2-1$.

First, we determine the limits of integration. To do that, we find the intersections of the two curves by setting the functions equal to one another and solving for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= g(x) \\
- x^{2} + 1 &= x^{2} - 1 \\
x^{2} &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the solutions are $x= \pm 1$.

Now, consider a cross-section at some point $x$, where $x \in (-1,1)$, and its projection on the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/4fe1ee3d82cd36e6035321bf8bd56c04.png>)

Then, the base of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= \mid f(x) - g(x) \mid \\
&= \mid (-x^{2} + 1) - (x^{2} - 1) \mid \\
&= 2 \mid 1 - x^{2} \mid
\end{aligned}
$$

Given a side $a$ of an equilateral triangle, the corresponding area can be found using the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2
$$

So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^{2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(2 \mid 1 - x^{2} \mid)^{2} \\
&= \sqrt{3}(1 - 2x^{2} + x^{4})
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now write the volume of the solid, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= \sqrt{3}∫_{-1}^{1}(1 - 2x^{2} + x^{4})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Cross-Sections Perpendicular to the y-Axis

Now, suppose that the base of a solid is the region enclosed between the curve

$$
x=\sqrt{2-y^2}
$$

and the $y$-axis for

$$
0 \leq y \leq \sqrt{2}
$$

as shown in the picture. If cross-sections perpendicular to the $y$-axis are isosceles right triangles with a leg lying on the $xy$-plane, can we find an integral that gives the volume of the solid?

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/5122440079eca62133cb1eab8bbddef4.png>)

A sketch with a few cross-sections is shown in the picture below.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/ce86d62e3caf63173b3f33bae2aefc3f.png>)

Consider the picture with only one cross-section that passes through $y \in (0,\sqrt{2})$:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/cc38229dac881b3336904988cb93dd73.png>)

If we project the triangular cross-section onto the $xy$-plane, we get the picture below:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/a87ec64e181ab1ee58d5f1953b9098f9.png>)

Then, the base of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= \mid x|= \sqrt{2 - y^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Given a leg $b$ of an isosceles right triangle, we can find the corresponding area using the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{b^2}{2}
$$

So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= \frac{1}{2}b^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{2 - y^{2}})^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(2 - y^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now write the volume of the solid as an integral, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{c}^{d}A(y)dy \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{\sqrt{2}}(2 - y^{2})dy
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluating the integral above gives the required volume.

## Computing the Volume  When the Cross Sections Are Perpendicular to the Y-Axis

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/63765298730171bb40daf703ab12bb49.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider a cross-section at some point $y$, where $y \in (0,\sqrt 2)$, and its projection on the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/7062053a6b5ff89ffa0fbe4aba9bf9ad.png>)

Then the base of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \mid x|= \mid \sqrt{4 - 2y^{2}}|= \sqrt{4 - 2y^{2}}

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by Two Curves

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region enclosed between the curve $x=\sqrt{y}$ and the line $x=y$, as shown in the picture. Given that cross-sections perpendicular to the $y$-axis are isosceles right triangles with a leg lying on the $xy$-plane, calculate the volume of the solid.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/10f13e47cd0e7dfa6866cc8cca319ccb.png>)

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(y)=\sqrt{y}
$$

and $g(y)=y$.

First, we determine the limits of integration. To do that, we find the intersections of the two curves by setting the functions equal to one another and solving for $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(y) &= g(y) \\
\sqrt{y} &= y \\
\sqrt{y} - y &= 0 \\
\sqrt{y}(1 - \sqrt{y}) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the intersections are $y=0$ and $y=1$.

Now, consider a cross-section at some point $y$, where $y \in (0,1)$, and its projection on the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/880b19428119d977c4622794f12aad41.png>)

Then the base of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= \mid f(y) - g(y) \mid \\
&= \mid \sqrt{y} - y \mid
\end{aligned}
$$

Given a leg $b$ of an isosceles right triangle, we can find the corresponding area using the formula

$$
A =\dfrac{b^2}{2}
$$

So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= \frac{1}{2}b^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \mid \sqrt{y} - y \mid ^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(y - 2y^{3/2} + y^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now calculate the volume of the solid, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{c}^{d}A(y)dy \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{1}(y - 2y^{3/2} + y^{2})dy \\
&= \frac{1}{2}[\frac{y^{2}}{2} - \frac{4}{5}y^{5/2} + \frac{y^{3}}{3}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{4}{5} + \frac{1}{3}) - 0 \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{30} \\
&= \frac{1}{60}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Derivations of the Triangle Formulas

Here, we derive the three useful triangle formulas that we have used throughout the lesson.

- For an equilateral triangle with side length $a$, the area $A$ is given by the formula
$A = \dfrac{\sqrt 3}{4}a^2$.
To see why this is true, consider the equilateral triangle below.![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/222802cd954b1b5c1aea22a6946dbd7b.png>)
For this triangle, we see that we have
$base|= a,; height|= a\sin 60^{∘} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a$.
Substituting this into the formula for the area of a triangle, we get
$A = \dfrac 1 2 \cdot a \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}a = \dfrac{\sqrt 3}{4}a^2$.

![](<../Source/2.2.3. Volumes of Solids with Triangular Cross Sections/Images/ae246b8f0527e99ee09ff5c9a4500a6a.png>)

- For an isosceles right triangle with leg $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the area $A$ of the triangle is
$A = \dfrac12 b^2$.
This is a straightforward application of the formula for the area of a triangle. Clearly, we have $\textrm{base} = \textrm{height} = b$, and therefore, the formula follows immediately.We also have the formula
$A = \dfrac14 c^2$.
To see why this is true, recall that, for an isosceles right triangle, the base $b$ and hypotenuse $c$ are related by the equation $c = \sqrt 2 b$. Now, substituting for $b$ into the formula $A=\dfrac12 b^2$, we get
$A = \dfrac12\left(\dfrac{c}{\sqrt 2}\right)^2 = \dfrac14 c^2$.
