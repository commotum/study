## Introduction

Let's consider the curve

$$
r = 1+\cos\theta
$$

for

$$
0\leq \theta < 2\pi
$$

shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/86544ecf2e7f40b4e329c273feca654d.png>)

Using previously discussed techniques, it's straightforward to show that at point $C$ where

$$
\theta = \pi
$$

we have

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)} = \dfrac00
$$

To see what's happening at this point, let's zoom in and observe the behavior of the curve.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/8273a3da61da754360f4c96968036fe5.png>)

If we look carefully, we notice that the tangent lines flatten out as we approach the point $C$ from either direction.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/156e2051a24c2eb1481a3cea6fbfe2fd.png>)

Therefore, as we take the *limit* of the tangent lines as we approach $C$, it would appear that they become horizontal.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/1eac80c01ce5c6cd03e8241f7b41a2fe.png>)

When the limit of the tangent lines becomes horizontal like this, we say that the curve has a horizontal tangent at $C$.

## Using Limits to Find Horizontal Tangents

So, it seems that the curve

$$
r = 1+\cos\theta
$$

has a horizontal tangent at

$$
\theta = \pi
$$

However, to be sure of this, we need to check that the tangent lines flatten out as we approach

$$
\theta = \pi
$$

In other words, we need to check that

$$
\lim\limits_{\theta \to \pi} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = 0
$$

Let's now verify that this is true:

Using techniques discussed in previous lessons, it's straightforward to show that

$$
x'(\theta)=-\sin\theta(2\cos\theta+1), \qquad y'(\theta)=2\cos^2\theta +\cos\theta - 1
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(θ → π)\frac{dy}{dx} &= lim_(θ → π)((2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1)/(-\sin θ(2\cos θ + 1))) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π)((2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1)/(\sin θ(2\cos θ + 1))) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π)(\frac{2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1}{2\sin θ\cos θ + \sin θ}) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π)(\frac{2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1}{\sin 2θ + \sin θ})
\end{aligned}
$$

We already know that substituting

$$
\theta = \pi
$$

gives the indeterminate form

$$
\dfrac00
$$

so evaluating this limit by direct substitution won't work. However, we can evaluate this limit using L'Hopital's rule.

Applying L'Hopital's rule, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(θ → π)\frac{dy}{dx} &= -lim_(θ → π)(\frac{2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1}{\sin 2θ + \sin θ}) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π)(((2\cos^{2} θ + \cos θ - 1)^{′})/((\sin 2θ + \sin θ)^{′})) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π)(\frac{-4\cos θ\sin θ - \sin θ}{2\cos 2θ + \cos θ}) \\
&= lim_(θ → π)(\frac{4\cos θ\sin θ + \sin θ}{2\cos 2θ + \cos θ}) \\
&= \frac{4\cos π\sin π + \sin π}{2\cos 2π + \cos π} \\
&= (4 \cdot (-1) \cdot (0) + 0)/(2 \cdot 1 + (-1)) \\
&= \frac{0}{1} \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, since

$$
\lim\limits_{\theta \to \pi} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = 0
$$

we conclude that our curve does indeed have a horizontal tangent at

$$
\theta = \pi
$$

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/bdc58015b7128566b18ccd016a2a70d6.png>)

<a id="finding-horizontal-tangents-in-non-differentiable-cases"></a>
## Finding Horizontal Tangents in Non-Differentiable Cases

**Example:** Consider the polar curve $r=2(1+\sin\theta)$ for $[MATH: \theta \in \left[0, 2\pi\right).]$ Given that

$x'(\theta) = 2\cos2\theta - 2\sin\theta, \qquad y'(\theta) = 2\cos\theta+2\sin2\theta$,
and $x'\left(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right) = 0$, which of the following statements are true?

1. $y'\left(\dfrac{3\pi}2\right) = 0$
2. $\lim\limits_{\theta \to 3\pi/2} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x}=0$
3. The curve has a horizontal tangent at $\theta = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

*Hint: Use L'Hopital's rule to evaluate the limit in part II.*

**Explanation**

When a polar curve is differentiable at a point $\theta_0$, there is a horizontal tangent at $\theta_0$ if

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta_0)}{x'(\theta_0)} = 0
$$

which is equivalent to

$$
y'(\theta_0) = 0 \qquad \textrm{and}\qquad x'(\theta_0) \neq 0
$$

However, if the curve is continuous at $\theta_0$ yet the derivative $y'(x)$ takes the indeterminate form

$$
\dfrac00
$$

at this point, we say that the curve has a horizontal tangent at this point if

$$
\lim\limits_{\theta \to \theta_0} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = 0
$$

In this case, we're told that

$$
x'\left(\dfrac{3\pi}2\right)=0
$$

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is true. Substituting $\theta = \dfrac{3\pi}2$ into the expression for $y'(\theta)$, we get
$y^{′}(\frac{3π}{2})|= 2\cos (\frac{3π}{2}) + 2\sin (2 \cdot \frac{3π}{2}); = 2\cos (\frac{3π}{2}) + 2\sin (3π); = 0 + 0; = 0$.
- Statement II is false. Computing the limit as $\theta\to \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ using L'Hopital's rule, we obtain
$lim_(θ → 3π/2)\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → 3π/2)(y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)); = lim_(θ → 3π/2)(\frac{2\cos θ + 2\sin 2θ}{2\cos 2θ - 2\sin θ}); = lim_(θ → 3π/2)(\frac{\cos θ + \sin 2θ}{\cos 2θ - \sin θ}); = lim_(θ → 3π/2)(((\cos θ + \sin 2θ)^{′})/((\cos 2θ - \sin θ)^{′})); = lim_(θ → 3π/2)(\frac{-\sin θ + 2\cos 2θ}{-2\sin 2θ - \cos θ}); = (-\sin (\frac{3π}{2}) + 2\cos (2 \cdot \frac{3π}{2}))/(-2\sin (2 \cdot \frac{3π}{2}) - \cos (\frac{3π}{2})); = (-\sin (\frac{3π}{2}) + 2\cos (3π))/(-2\sin (3π) - \cos (\frac{3π}{2})); = (1 + 2 \cdot (-1))/(-2 \cdot 0 - 0); = \frac{1 - 2}{0}; = \frac{-1}{0}; \ne 0$.

- Statement III is false. Since the limit in part II does *not* equal to $0$, there curve does *not* have a horizontal tangent at $\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$. In fact, it is a vertical tangent.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/9358e7cfaeed727c4002fc75656e3ce7.png>)

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only."

**Note**: We'll discuss vertical tangents in more detail later in this lesson.

r = 1-\sin\theta

$$

$$

shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/35737169372c37e2c269354cb423dbd9.png>)

Using previously discussed techniques, it's straightforward to show that at point $C$ where

$$

To see what's happening, let's zoom in and observe the behavior of the curve at this point.

## Using Limits to Find Vertical Tangents

So, it seems that the curve

$$
r = 1-\sin\theta
$$

has a vertical tangent at

$$
\theta = \dfrac\pi 2
$$

However, to be sure of this, we need to check that the tangent lines' slopes grow to infinity as we approach

$$
\theta = \dfrac \pi 2
$$

In other words, we need to check that

$$
\lim\limits_{\theta \to (\pi/2)^{-}} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \pm\infty \qquad\textrm{and}\qquad \lim\limits_{\theta \to (\pi/2)^{+}} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \pm\infty
$$

Let's now verify that this is true:

Using techniques discussed in previous lessons, it's straightforward to show that

$$
x'(\theta)=-\sin\theta-\cos(2\theta), \qquad y'(\theta)=\cos\theta - \sin2\theta
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(θ → π/2)\frac{dy}{dx} &= lim_(θ → π/2)((\cos θ - \sin 2θ)/(-\sin θ - \cos (2θ))) \\
&=-lim_(θ → π/2)((\cos θ - \sin (2θ))/(\sin θ + \cos (2θ)))
\end{aligned}
$$

We already know that substituting

$$
\theta = \dfrac\pi 2
$$

gives the indeterminate form

$$
\dfrac00
$$

so evaluating this limit by direct substitution won't work. However, we can evaluate this limit using L'Hopital's rule.

- Applying L'Hopital's rule to evaluate the left-sided limit, we get
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})\frac{dy}{dx}|=-lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})((\cos θ - \sin (2θ)))/(\sin θ + \cos (2θ))); =-lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})(((\cos θ - \sin (2θ))^{′})/((\sin θ + \cos (2θ))^{′})); =-lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})((-\sin θ - 2\cos (2θ))/(\cos θ - 2\sin (2θ))); = lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})((\sin θ + 2\cos (2θ))/(\cos θ - 2\sin (2θ)))$.
Evaluating the limit in the numerator, we get
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})(\sin θ + 2\cos (2θ))|= \sin (\frac{π}{2}) + 2\cos (2 \cdot \frac{π}{2}); = \sin (\frac{π}{2}) + 2\cos (π); = 1 - 2; =-1$.
For the limit in the denominator, it can be shown that
$\lim\limits_{\theta \to (\pi/2)^-} \left(\cos\theta - 2\sin(2\theta)\right) = 0^-$,
where the notation $0^-$ means that the denominator approaches zero from the negative numbers. Therefore, we have
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})((\sin θ + 2\cos (2θ))/(\cos θ - 2\sin (2θ))); = \frac{-1}{0^{-}}; = ∞$.

- Using similar methods, it can be shown that the right-sided limit is
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})((\sin θ + 2\cos (2θ))/(\cos θ - 2\sin (2θ))); = \frac{-1}{0^{+}}; = - ∞$.

Since the limits of our derivative are both infinite, we conclude that there is a vertical tangent to the curve at

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/3115827a82bef56fc86690040ec9d891.png>)

<a id="finding-vertical-tangents-in-non-differentiable-cases"></a>
## Finding Vertical Tangents in Non-Differentiable Cases

**Example:** Consider the polar curve $r=\cot^2\theta$ for $\theta \in \left(0, \pi\right)$. Given that

$x'(\theta) = -\cot\theta(\cos\theta + 2\cot\theta\csc\theta), \qquad y'(\theta) = -\cot\theta\csc\theta(1+\sin^2\theta)$,
and $y'\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$, which of the following statements are true?

1. $x'\left(\dfrac\pi2\right) = 0$
2. $\lim\limits_{\theta \to (\pi/2)^{+}} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x}=-\infty$
3. The curve has a vertical tangent at $\theta = \dfrac\pi 2$

*Hint: You may wish to make use of the following results.*
$\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ → 0^{+}asθ → (\frac{π}{2})^{-}; \cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ → 0^{-}asθ → (\frac{π}{2})^{+}$

*It is not necessary to use L'Hopital's rule to evaluate the limit in part II.*

**Explanation**

For a given polar curve, there is a vertical tangent at

$$
\theta = \theta_0
$$

if

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta_0)}{x'(\theta_0)}
$$

is infinite. This means that

$$
y'(\theta_0) \neq 0 \qquad \textrm{and}\qquad x'(\theta_0) = 0
$$

However, if the curve is continuous at $\theta_0$ yet the derivative takes the indeterminate form

$$
\dfrac00
$$

at this point, we say that the curve has a vertical tangent at this point if

$$
\lim\limits_{\theta \to \theta_0^{-}} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \pm\infty \qquad\textrm{and}\qquad \lim\limits_{\theta \to \theta_0^{+}} \dfrac{\textrm d y}{\textrm d x} = \pm\infty
$$

In this case, we're told that

$$
y'\left(\dfrac\pi2\right)=0
$$

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is true. Substituting $\theta = \dfrac\pi2$ into the expression for $x'(\theta)$, we get
$x^{′}(\frac{π}{2})|=-\cot (\frac{π}{2})(\cos (\frac{π}{2}) + 2\cot (\frac{π}{2})\csc (\frac{π}{2})); = 0 \cdot (0 + 2 \cdot 0 \cdot 1); = 0$.
- Statement II is true. Computing the limit as $\theta\to\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^+$, we obtain
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})(y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)); = lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})((-\cot θ\csc θ(1 + \sin^{2} θ))/(-\cot θ(\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ))); = lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})((-\cotθ\csc θ(1 + \sin^{2} θ))/(-\cotθ(\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ))); = lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})((\csc θ(1 + \sin^{2} θ))/(\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ)); = lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})(\frac{\csc θ + \sin θ}{\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ})$.
Evaluating the limit in the numerator, we get
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})(\csc θ + \sin θ)|= \csc (\frac{π}{2}) + \sin (\frac{π}{2}); = 1 + 1; = 2$.
Therefore, using the second hint, we have
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → (π/2)^{+})(\frac{\csc θ + \sin θ}{\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ}); = \frac{2}{0^{-}}; = - ∞$.
- Statement III is true. Using the calculations from part I, and the first hint, we have
$lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})\frac{dy}{dx}|= lim_(θ → (π/2)^{-})(\frac{\csc θ + \sin θ}{\cos θ + 2\cot θ\csc θ}); = \frac{2}{0^{+}}; = ∞$.

Since the limits of our derivative are both infinite, there is a vertical tangent to the curve at

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

![](<../Source/3.4.4. Horizontal and Vertical Tangents to Polar Curves in Non-Differentiable Cases/Images/689c3c6e56f383819dad78a86732ed85.png>)

Therefore, the correct answer is "I, II, and III."
