## Introduction

Suppose we want to find the area of the region bounded by the polar curve

$$
r = 3\cos\theta
$$

and the rays

$$
\theta_1 = 0
$$

and

$$
\theta_2 = \dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

as shown in blue in the picture below. Can we calculate this area using polar coordinates?

![](<../Source/3.4.6. Finding the Area of a Polar Region/Images/0ae57c37a8bcd46584a48b6258ff7d47.png>)

In general, the area of the region bounded by a polar curve $r=r(\theta)$ and the rays

$$
\theta=\theta_1
$$

and

$$
\theta=\theta_2
$$

is given by the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} r^2 \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

Therefore, the area of the half-circle above can be obtained as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{π/2}(3\cos θ)^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{9}{2}∫_{0}^{π/2}\cos^{2} θdθ \\
&= \frac{9}{2}∫_{0}^{π/2}\frac{1}{2}(1 + \cos (2θ))dθ \\
&= \frac{9}{4}(θ \mid _{0}^{π/2} + \frac{1}{2}\sin (2θ) \mid _{0}^{π/2}) \\
&= \frac{9}{4}(\frac{π}{2} + 0) \\
&= \frac{9π}{8}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Area Bounded by a Petal of a Polar Rose

**Example:** Find the area bounded by the polar curve $r = \cos(3\theta)$ and the lines $\theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$.

![](<../Source/3.4.6. Finding the Area of a Polar Region/Images/bb7cc7dd162b6f0a389d6dc6e05c9319.png>)

**Explanation**

The area bounded by a polar curve is given by

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} r^2(\theta) \, \textrm{d}\theta \,
$$

When

$$
r(\theta) = \cos(3\theta)
$$

$$
\theta_1=-\dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

and

$$
\theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

this gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}r^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}\cos^{2} (3θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}(1 + \cos (6θ))dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{4}(θ \mid _{- π/6}^{π/6} + \frac{1}{6}\sin (6θ) \mid _{- π/6}^{π/6}) \\
&= \frac{1}{4}((\frac{π}{6} - (-\frac{π}{6})) + \frac{1}{6}(\sin π - \sin (- π))) \\
&= \frac{1}{4}(\frac{π}{3} + 0) \\
&= \frac{π}{12}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Area Bounded by the Loop of a Limaçon

**Example:** Find the area bounded by the polar curve $r = 1+2\cos\theta$ for $\dfrac{2\pi}{3} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{4\pi}{3}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.6. Finding the Area of a Polar Region/Images/6471920e9e3f1a0eef69f5be705a6e6c.png>)

**Explanation**

The area bounded by a polar curve is given by

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} r^2(\theta) \, \textrm{d}\theta \,
$$

When

$$
r(\theta) = 1+2\cos(\theta)
$$

$$
\theta_1=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}
$$

and

$$
\theta_2=\dfrac{4\pi}{3}
$$

this gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}r^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{2π/3}^{4π/3}(1 + 2\cos (θ))^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{2π/3}^{4π/3}(1 + 4\cos θ + 4\cos^{2} θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(θ \mid _{2π/3}^{4π/3} + 4\sin θ\begin{vmatrix}_{2π/3}^{4π/3} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}∫_{2π/3}^{4π/3}(1 + \cos (2θ))dθ) \\ = \frac{1}{2}(θ\begin{vmatrix}_{2π/3}^{4π/3} + 4\sin θ & _{2π/3}^{4π/3} + 2θ & _{2π/3}^{4π/3} + \sin (2θ)\end{vmatrix}_{2π/3}^{4π/3}) \\ = \frac{1}{2}(3θ \mid _{2π/3}^{4π/3} + 4\sin θ \mid _{2π/3}^{4π/3} + \sin (2θ)\end{vmatrix}_{2π/3}^{4π/3}) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(2π - 4\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{3}) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(2π - 3\sqrt{3})
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding Areas Bounded by Other Polar Curves

**Example:** Find the area bounded by the polar curve $r = 2\sin\theta + 2\cos\theta$ for $\dfrac{\pi}{2} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{3\pi}{4}$, as shown above.

![](<../Source/3.4.6. Finding the Area of a Polar Region/Images/0cf3efb2050d3ceed8f1d4dba42a466e.png>)

**Explanation**

The area bounded by a polar curve is given by

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} r^2(\theta) \, \textrm{d}\theta \,
$$

When

$$
r(\theta) = 2\sin\theta + 2\cos\theta
$$

$$
\theta_1=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

and

$$
\theta_2=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}
$$

this gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}r^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/2}^{3π/4}(2\sin θ + 2\cos θ)^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/2}^{3π/4}(4\sin^{2} θ + 8\sin θ\cos θ + 4\cos^{2} θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/2}^{3π/4}(4 + 8\sin θ\cos θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{4}{2}∫_{π/2}^{3π/4}(1 + \sin (2θ))dθ \\
&= 2(θ\begin{vmatrix}_{π/2}^{3π/4} - (\cos (2θ))/(2) & _{π/2}^{3π/4}) \\ = 2θ & _{π/2}^{3π/4} - \cos (2θ)\end{vmatrix}_{π/2}^{3π/4} \\
&= 2(\frac{3π}{4} - \frac{π}{2}) - (\cos (\frac{3π}{2}) - \cos (π)) \\
&= \frac{π}{2} - 1 \\
&= \frac{π - 2}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Intuition Behind the Formula

We've been using the following formula for the area of the region bounded by a polar curve $r=r(\theta)$ and the rays

$$
\theta=\theta_1
$$

and

$$
\theta=\theta_2{:}
$$

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} r^2 \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

But where does this formula come from?

To see where the formula comes from, we can think about breaking up the area bounded by a polar curve into infinitesimally small sectors of circles.

As shown in the graph below, each sector has a radius of $r$ and spans an infinitesimally small angle, $\textrm d \theta$.

![](<../Source/3.4.6. Finding the Area of a Polar Region/Images/f54818e0e1def6c498e34fd18ddf6925.png>)

The ratio of the area of a sector to the full area of the circle must be the same as the ratio of the angle of the sector to a full revolution of the circle. So, if the area of a sector is $\textrm dA$, then we can set up and solve the following proportion:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm dA}{\pi r^2} = \dfrac{\textrm d\theta}{2\pi} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \textrm dA = \dfrac{1}{2} r^2 \textrm d\theta
$$

Finally, to find the area under the curve, we can integrate the areas of all the sectors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\frac{1}{2}r^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}r^{2}dθ
\end{aligned}
$$
