## Introduction

Suppose that $A$ is an $n\times n$ symmetric matrix over $\mathbb{R}$. The **spectral theorem** states the following:

- The eigenvalues of $A$ are all real.
- The eigenvectors corresponding to *different* eigenvalues are orthogonal.
- The dimension of the eigenspace for each eigenvalue equals the multiplicity of that eigenvalue as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
- $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable.

---

<a id="identifying-true-statements-regarding-the-spectral-theorem"></a>
## Identifying True Statements Regarding the Spectral Theorem

**Example:** Given that $A$ is a real symmetric matrix, which of the following statements are true?

1. $A$ must have all real eigenvalues
2. $A$ is diagonalizable
3. If $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are two eigenvectors, then $\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v}$

**Explanation**

Given that $A$ is an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix over $\mathbb{R}$, the spectral theorem states the following:

- The eigenvalues of $A$ are all real.
- Eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
- $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable.

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is true. Indeed, the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real.
- Statement II is true. Indeed, any symmetric matrix with real entries is orthogonally diagonalizable. Hence, it is diagonalizable.
- Statement III is false. According to the theorem, eigenvectors corresponding to *different* eigenvalues must be orthogonal, but two eigenvectors corresponding to the *same* eigenvalue may not be orthogonal.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

<a id="the-spectral-decomposition-of-a-symmetric-matrix"></a>
## The Spectral Decomposition of a Symmetric Matrix

We can write an $n\times n$ symmetric matrix $A$ in terms of a sum of products of its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Specifically, if $\mathbf{u}_i$ denotes an orthonormal eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ for

$$
i=1,\ldots, n
$$

, then

$$
A=\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T
$$

Expressing a symmetric matrix $A$ in this way is called the **spectral decomposition** of $A$.

To demonstrate, let's find the spectral decomposition of the $2\times 2$ symmetric matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}
$$

The eigenvalues of $A$ are

$$
\lambda_1 = 6
$$

and

$$
\lambda_2 = -4
$$

and the corresponding eigenvectors are

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

The spectral decomposition of $A$ is given by

$$
A =\sum_{i=1}^2 \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T = \lambda_1 \mathbf u_1 \mathbf u_1^T + \lambda_2 \mathbf u_2 \mathbf u_2^T
$$

where $\mathbf u_1$ and $\mathbf u_2$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, respectively.

First, let's normalize our eigenvectors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1} &= \frac{v_{1}}{‖v_{1}‖} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \\
u_{2} &= \frac{v_{2}}{‖v_{2}‖} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the spectral decomposition of $A$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= λ_{1}u_{1}u_{1}^{T} + λ_{2}u_{2}u_{2}^{T} \\
&= 6 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3\end{bmatrix} + (-4) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 6 \cdot \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3\end{bmatrix} + (-4) \cdot \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 6_(⏟)_(λ_{1}) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{10} & \frac{3}{10} \\ \frac{3}{10} & \frac{9}{10}\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(u_{1}u_{1}^{T}) + (-4)_(⏟)_(λ_{2}) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{9}{10} & -\frac{3}{10} \\ -\frac{3}{10} & \frac{1}{10}\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(u_{2}u_{2}^{T})
\end{aligned}
$$

In this decomposition, the matrix $\mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T$ is the orthogonal projection matrix that maps any $\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^2$ onto the subspace spanned by $\mathbf u_i$.

<a id="finding-the-spectral-decomposition-of-a-symmetric-matrix"></a>
## Finding the Spectral Decomposition of a Symmetric Matrix

**Example:** Suppose that

$\qquad$ $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

are eigenvectors of a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix $A$ that correspond to the eigenvalues $\lambda_1=6$ and $\lambda_2=-7$, respectively. Find the matrix $A$.

**Explanation**

The spectral decomposition of a $2\times 2$ symmetric matrix $A$ is given by

$$
A = \lambda_1 \mathbf u_1\cdot \mathbf u_1^T + \lambda_2 \mathbf u_2\cdot \mathbf u_2^T
$$

where $\mathbf u_1$ and $\mathbf u_2$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, respectively.

First, let's normalize our eigenvectors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1} &= \frac{v_{1}}{‖v_{1}‖} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \\
u_{2} &= \frac{v_{2}}{‖v_{2}‖} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= λ_{1}u_{1}u_{1}^{T} + λ_{2}u_{2}u_{2}^{T} \\
&= 6 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3\end{bmatrix} + (-7) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 6 \cdot \frac{1}{13}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3\end{bmatrix} + (-7) \cdot \frac{1}{13}\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{13}(6 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3\end{bmatrix} + (-7) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 2\end{bmatrix}) \\
&= \frac{1}{13}(6 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 & 6 \\ 6 & 9\end{bmatrix} + (-7) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}9 & -6 \\ -6 & 4\end{bmatrix}) \\
&= \frac{1}{13}\begin{bmatrix}-39 & 78 \\ 78 & 26\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-3 & 6 \\ 6 & 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding a Projection Matrix That Orthogonally Projects a Vector to the Subspace Spanned by an Eigenvector

**Example:** Find the orthogonal projection matrix that maps $\mathbf x\in \mathbb R^2$ onto the subspace spanned by an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -2 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

The spectral decomposition of a $2\times 2$ symmetric matrix $A$ is given by

$$
A = \lambda_1 \mathbf u_1\cdot \mathbf u_1^T + \lambda_2 \mathbf u_2\cdot \mathbf u_2^T
$$

where $\mathbf u_1$ and $\mathbf u_2$ are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, respectively.

Moreover, $\mathbf u_i\cdot \mathbf u_i^T$ is the projection matrix corresponding to $\mathbf u_i$.

First, we need to find an eigenvector that corresponds to $\lambda=-1$. Computing $A-(-1)I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - (-1)I &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -2 & 0\end{bmatrix} - (-1)\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have a system of linear equations with the augmented matrix $M$ which we reduce to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & -2 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + \frac{1}{2}R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}4 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has one pivot column (the $1$st one). Thus, $x_2$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1=\dfrac12x_2
$$

Hence, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, setting $x_2=2$, we get the eigenvector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

where

$$
\| \mathbf{v} \| = \sqrt{5}
$$

Dividing $\mathbf{v}$ by its norm, we obtain the normalized eigenvector:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally, the required orthogonal projection matrix is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
u \cdot u^{T} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
