## Introduction

The **singular value decomposition** (or SVD) of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is a factorization of the form

$$
A=U \Sigma V^T
$$

where

- $\Sigma$ is an $m \times n$ matrix with the singular values of $A$ (ordered from largest to smallest) on the main diagonal and zeroes outside the main diagonal,
- $U$ is an orthogonal $m \times m$ matrix, and
- $V$ is an orthogonal $n \times n$ matrix.

The columns of $U$ are called the **left singular vectors** of $A$, and the columns of $V$ are called the **right singular vectors** of $A$.

For example, the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ -3 & -1\end{bmatrix}$ has the following singular value decomposition:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

The singular value decomposition of a matrix $A$ can be thought of as a type of diagonalization that works for *any* $m\times n$ matrix! It's also one of linear algebra's most useful forms of matrix factorization with applications in statistics, image compression, machine learning, and more.

## Finding the Second and Third Matrices of a Singular Value Decomposition

Suppose that $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, and

$$
A= U\Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$. Let's describe how to find the matrices $V$ and $\Sigma$ of this singular value decomposition.

Notice that if we compute $A^TA$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{T}A &= (UΣV^{T})^{T}(UΣV^{T}) \\
&= (VΣ^{T}U^{T})(UΣV^{T}) \\
&= VΣ^{T}(U^{T}U)ΣV^{T} \\
&= VΣ^{T}(I_{n})ΣV^{T} \\
&= V(Σ^{T}Σ)V^{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

From here, we note that $A^T \! A$ is always symmetric, and that $\Sigma^T \! \Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix with the squares of the singular values on the main diagonal. As a result,

$$
A^TA = V\left(\Sigma^T \Sigma\right) V^T
$$

is an orthogonal diagonalization of the symmetric matrix $A^T \! A$. So, to find the matrix $V$ in our SVD, we need to orthogonally diagonalize $A^T A$.

Let's demonstrate how this works in practice by computing the matrices $\Sigma$ and $V$ in the SVD of the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

We proceed as follows:

- Computing $A^T \! A$, we obtain
$A^{T}A|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ -3 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}4 & -6 \\ -6 & 13\end{bmatrix}$.
- Solving the characteristic equation $\mid A^T \! A - \lambda I \mid = 0$, we get the eigenvalues of $A^T \! A$:
${\color{blue}{\lambda_1 = 16}}, \qquad {\color{red}{\lambda_2 = 1}}$
So, the corresponding singular values are
${\color{blue}\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\lambda_1} = 4}, \qquad {\color{red}\sigma_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_2} = 1}$.

We now find a unit eigenvector basis of $A^T \! A$. These unit eigenvectors will be the columns of our matrix $V$ in the orthogonal diagonalization of $A^T \! A$.

- Computing $(A^T \! A - {\color{blue}\lambda_1} I)$, we obtain
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & -6 \\ -6 & 13\end{bmatrix}$
Seeking a unit solution of $(A^T\! A - 16I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$
- Computing $(A^T \! A - {\color{red}\lambda_2} I)$, we obtain
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & -6 \\ -6 & 13\end{bmatrix}$
Seeking a unit solution of $(A^T\! A - I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, we construct the matrices $\Sigma$ and $V$:
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

## Finding the Columns of the Third Matrix Given Part of a Singular Value Decomposition

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}17 & 8 \\ 8 & 17\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrices shown above. Given that $A=U \Sigma V^T$ is a singular value decomposition of $A$, what is the value of $\begin{vmatrix}v_{11} & + & v_{21}\end{vmatrix}$?

**Explanation**

If

$$
A=U \Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$, the columns of $V$ must be the right singular vectors of $A$, i.e., the unit eigenvectors of the matrix $A^T \! A$.

From the matrix $\Sigma$, we deduce that the singular values are $\sigma_1=5$ and $\sigma_2=3$. So, the first column of $V$ corresponds to the singular value $\sigma_1=5$.

Hence, we need to find a unit eigenvector of $A^T \!A$ corresponding to

$$
\lambda_1 = \sigma_1^2=25
$$

Computing $A^T \! A - 25 I$, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}17 & 8 \\ 8 & 17\end{bmatrix}
$$

Seeking a non-zero solution of

$$
(A^T\!A-25I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
$$

gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}$ by its norm, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}v_{11} & + & v_{21} & = \left & \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right & + \left & \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right\end{vmatrix} = \sqrt{2}
$$

## Finding the First Matrix of a Singular Value Decomposition

Suppose that $A$ is an $2\times 2$ matrix, and

$$
A= U\Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$. Let's now describe how to compute the matrix $U$ of the SVD using the matrices $\Sigma$ and $V$.

Recall that $V$ is orthogonal, meaning that

$$
V^T \! V = I
$$

Consequently, multiplying

$$
A = U\Sigma V^T
$$

by $V$ on the right, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= UΣV^{T} \\
AV &= UΣ(V^{T}V) \\
AV &= UΣ
\end{aligned}
$$

In expanded form, our final equation looks as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A[ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= v_{1} \mid v_{2} \\
\mid \mid \mid ] &= [ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= u_{1} \mid u_{2} \\
&= \mid \mid \mid ]\begin{bmatrix}σ_{1} & 0 \\ 0 & σ_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= [ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= Av_{1} \mid Av_{2} \\
\mid \mid \mid ] &= [ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= σ_{1}u_{1} \mid σ_{2}u_{2} \\
&= \mid \mid \mid ]
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i
$$

for $i=1,2$. Finally, provided that

$$
\sigma_i \ne 0
$$

we obtain

$$
\mathbf{u}_i = \dfrac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i
$$

We can use this to find the columns of the matrix $\mathbf u$. Let's see an example.

<a id="calculating-the-first-matrix-a-worked-example"></a>
## Calculating the First Matrix - A Worked Example

Consider the matrices $A$, $\Sigma$, and $V$ shown below.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Suppose we're given that

$$
A=U \Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$. Let's use this information to find the matrix $U$.

From the matrix $\Sigma$, we deduce that the singular values are

$$
\color{blue}\sigma_1=9
$$

and

$$
\color{red}\sigma_2=1
$$

Earlier, we saw that the columns of $\mathbf u$ are given by

$$
\mathbf{u}_i = \dfrac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i
$$

where $\mathbf u_i$ and $\mathbf v_i$ are the $i$th columns of the matrices $U$ and $V$, respectively. Therefore, we compute the columns of $\mathbf u$ as follows:

- First, we compute the vector $\mathbf u_1$:
$u_{1}|= \frac{1}{σ_{1}}Av_{1}; = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix}3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{27}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{9}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$
- Then, we compute the vector $\mathbf u_2$:
$u_{2}|= \frac{1}{σ_{2}}Av_{2}; = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix}3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$

As a result, the matrix $U$ in our singular value decomposition of $A$ is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} & \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Determining the Columns of the First Matrix Given Part of a Singular Value Decomposition

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}6 & -5 \\ 0 & 6\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrices $A$, $\Sigma$, and $V$ shown above. Given that $A=U \Sigma V^T$ is a singular value decomposition of $A$, what is the second column of $U$?

**Explanation**

Recall that if

$$
A=U \Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$, and

$$
\sigma_i \neq 0
$$

then the $i$th column of $U$ can be found as

$$
\mathbf{u}_i = \dfrac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i
$$

where $\mathbf v_i$ is the $i$th column of the matrix $V$.

From the matrix $\Sigma$, we deduce that the singular values are $\sigma_1=9$ and $\sigma_2=4$. Therefore, the second column of $U$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{2} &= \frac{1}{σ_{2}}Av_{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}6 & -5 \\ 0 & 6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{13}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{13}}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{8}{\sqrt{13}} \\ \frac{12}{\sqrt{13}}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{\sqrt{13}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{13}}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## An Algorithm for Finding a Singular Value Decomposition

We can now fully describe the algorithm for computing the singular value decomposition of $A$.

- **Step 1.** Compute $A^T \! A$.
- **Step 2.** Find the singular values. This allows us to construct the second matrix $\Sigma$.
- **Step 3.** Find the right singular vectors of $A$ (i.e., the unit eigenvectors of $A^T \! A$). This allows us to construct the third matrix $V$.
- **Step 4.** Find the left singular vectors of $A$ using the singular values, right singular vectors, and the formula
$\mathbf{u}_i = \dfrac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i$,
where $\mathbf u_i$ and $\mathbf v_i$ are the $i$th columns of the matrices $U$ and $V$, respectively. This allows us to construct the matrix $U$.

Consider the matrices $A$ and $\Sigma$ shown below. Given that

$$
A=U \Sigma V^T
$$

is a singular value decomposition of $A$, let's find the matrices $U$ and $V$.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}5 & -8 \\ 8 & 7\end{bmatrix}
$$

We find the singular value decomposition of $A$ using our algorithm.

- **Step 1.** Compute $A^T \! A$:
$A^{T}A|= \begin{bmatrix}89 & 16 \\ 16 & 113\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 2.** Find the singular values: From the matrix $\Sigma$, we deduce that $\color{blue}\sigma_1=11$ and $\color{red}\sigma_2=9$.
- **Step 3.** Find the right singular vectors of $A$ (i.e., the unit eigenvectors of $A^T \! A$):Seeking a unit solution of $(A^T \! A - 121I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ gives
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$Seeking a unit solution of $(A^T \! A - 81I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ gives
$\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 4.** Find the left singular vectors of $A$: Since we know the singular values and right singular vectors, we can compute the columns of $U$ as follows:
$u_{1} = \frac{1}{σ_{1}}Av_{1} = \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}, u_{2} = \frac{1}{σ_{2}}Av_{2} = \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, we construct the matrices $U$ and $V$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} & -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="the-singular-value-decomposition-of-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## The Singular Value Decomposition of a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrices $A$ and $\Sigma$ shown above. Given that $A=U \Sigma V^T$ is a singular value decomposition of $A$, find the matrices $U$ and $V$.

**Explanation**

Let's find the singular value decomposition of $A$ using the usual algorithm.

- **Step 1.** Compute $A^T \! A$:
$A^{T}A|= \begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}-3 & 0 \\ 8 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}9 & -24 \\ -24 & 73\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 2.** Find the singular values: From the matrix $\Sigma$, we deduce that $\sigma_1=9$ and $\sigma_2=1$.
- **Step 3.** Find the right singular vectors of $A$ (i.e., the unit eigenvectors of $A^T \! A$):For $\lambda_1=\sigma_1^2=81$, we have
$A^{T}A - 81I|= \begin{bmatrix}9 & -24 \\ -24 & 73\end{bmatrix} - 81\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-72 & -24 \\ -24 & -8\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A^T \! A - 81I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ gives a unit eigenvector
$\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$For $\lambda_2=\sigma_2^2=1$, we have
$A^{T}A - I|= \begin{bmatrix}9 & -24 \\ -24 & 73\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}8 & -24 \\ -24 & 72\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A^T \! A - I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ gives a unit eigenvector
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 4.** Find the left singular vectors of $A$: Since we know the singular values and right singular vectors, we can compute the columns of $U$ as follows:First, we compute $\mathbf u_1$:
$u_{1}|= \frac{1}{σ_{1}}Av_{1}; = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \frac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{27}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{9}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$Then, we compute $\mathbf u_2$:
$u_{2}|= \frac{1}{σ_{2}}Av_{2}; = \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 8 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, we construct the matrices $U$ and $V$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}} & \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Geometric Interpretation of the Singular Value Decomposition

Let

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

be a singular value decomposition of a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$ with singular values $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$.

Due to the decomposition, the action of $A$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the same as the action of the sequence $V^T \to \Sigma \to U$.

As shown below, let's consider both equivalent actions on a unit circle (with the two standard basis vectors).

![](<../Source/7.4.5. Singular Value Decomposition of 2x2 Matrices/Images/3fe2ac4e26c91f3c13bb1a8fde8ff2c6.png>)

- Since $V^T$ is an orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrix, it represents a transformation of the plane that preserves distances between vectors. This could be a rotation, a reflection, or a combination of these two, which is sometimes called a **rotoreflection.**
- Since $\Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix, it represents a scaling along the $x$- and $y$-axes by $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, respectively.
- Since $U$ is again an orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrix, it represents a rotation, a reflection, or a rotoreflection.

To summarize, we have the following theorem:

> *Any linear transformation on the plane can be decomposed into an orthogonal transformation (rotation, reflection, or rotoreflection), a scaling, and another orthogonal transformation (rotation, reflection, or rotoreflection).*
