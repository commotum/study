## Introduction

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A function

$$
f: \Bbb R^n \to \Bbb R
$$

is a **linear form** (or *linear functional*) if there is a row-vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a_{1} & a_{2} & ⋯ & a_{n}\end{bmatrix}
$$

such that for any $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= ax \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}a_{1} & a_{2} & ⋯ & a_{n}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= a_{1}x_{1} + a_{2}x_{2} + … + a_{n}x_{n}
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, let $f(\mathbf{x})$ be the linear form defined on $\Bbb R^2$ by

$$
f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a} \mathbf{x}
$$

where $\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2\end{bmatrix}$

Suppose we want to evaluate $f(\mathbf{x})$ at $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ Then, we simply substitute $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ and obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= ax \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 3 \cdot 1 + (-2) \cdot (-1) \\
&= 3 + 2 \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating a Linear Form

**Example:** The linear form $f(\mathbf{x})$ is defined as $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{a} \mathbf{x}$, where $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ Evaluate the function when $\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Substituting $\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$ we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= ax \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 & 4\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 3 \cdot (-5) + 0 \cdot 8 + 4 \cdot 5 \\
&=-15 + 0 + 20 \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="bilinear-forms"></a>
## Bilinear Forms

Let $n$ be a positive integer. A function $B: \Bbb R^n\times \Bbb R^n \to \Bbb R$ is said to be a **bilinear form** if there is an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ such that

$$
B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A\mathbf{y}, \qquad \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \Bbb R^n
$$

Suppose, for example, that $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ To evaluate the bilinear form

$$
B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{y}
$$

when $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ we substitute these vectors in the above expression as follows

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(x, y) &= x^{T}Ay \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}7 & 10\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

To write down our bilinear form $B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ above in terms of vector components

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

we proceed as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(x, y) &= x^{T}Ay \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} + 2y_{2} \\ 3y_{1} + 4y_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_{1}(y_{1} + 2y_{2}) + x_{2}(3y_{1} + 4y_{2}) \\
&= x_{1}y_{1} + 2x_{1}y_{2} + 3x_{2}y_{1} + 4x_{2}y_{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-bilinear-form"></a>
## Evaluating a Bilinear Form

**Example:** Consider the the bilinear form $B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$, given by

$B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})= 2 x_1y_2 - x_2y_2 +3 x_2y_3-x_3y_1$

Evaluate $B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ when $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We are given that

$\qquad$ $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} \\ y_{2} \\ y_{3}\end{bmatrix}$

Substituting these into the bilinear expression, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(x, y) &= 2x_{1}y_{2} - x_{2}y_{2} + 3x_{2}y_{3} - x_{3}y_{1} \\
&= 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot (-2) \\
&= 4 - 2 + 3 - 2 \\
&= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="writing-down-a-bilinear-form-given-its-matrix"></a>
## Writing Down a Bilinear Form Given Its Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ 3 & -2\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrix $A$ above. Write down a bilinear form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{y}$ in terms of the components of $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

**Method 1**

Each entry $a_{ij}$ of $A$ corresponds to the term $a_{ij}x_i y_j$ in the expression of the bilinear form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{T}Ay &= 2x_{1}y_{1} + (-1)x_{1}y_{2} + 3x_{2}y_{1} + (-2)x_{2}y_{2} \\
&= 2x_{1}y_{1} - x_{1}y_{2} + 3x_{2}y_{1} - 2x_{2}y_{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2**

To write down a bilinear form in terms of vector components, we compute the following expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{T}Ay &= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ 3 & -2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2y_{1} - y_{2} \\ 3y_{1} - 2y_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_{1}(2y_{1} - y_{2}) + x_{2}(3y_{1} - 2y_{2}) \\
&= 2x_{1}y_{1} - x_{1}y_{2} + 3x_{2}y_{1} - 2x_{2}y_{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-matrix-of-a-bilinear-form"></a>
## Finding the Matrix of a Bilinear Form

**Example:** The matrix of the bilinear form $B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = 2x_1y_1 + 2x_2y_1 - x_3 y_2+2x_3y_3$ is given below. What is the value of $a+b+c$?
$\begin{bmatrix}a & 0 & 0 \\ b & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c & 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Each term $a_{ij}x_i y_j$ in the expression of the bilinear form corresponds to the entry $a_{ij}$ of the matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(x, y) &= 2x_{1}y_{1} + 2x_{2}y_{1} - x_{3}y_{2} + 2x_{3}y_{3} \\
&= 2x_{1}y_{1} + 0x_{1}y_{2} + 0x_{1}y_{3} \\
&= + 2x_{2}y_{1} + 0x_{2}y_{2} + 0x_{2}y_{3} \\
&= + 0x_{3}y_{1} + (-1)x_{3}y_{2} + 2x_{3}y_{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, the corresponding matrix of the bilinear form is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, $a=2$, $b=2$, and $c=-1$. Finally,

$$
a+b+c=2+2+(-1) = 3
$$

<a id="properties-of-bilinear-forms"></a>
## Properties of Bilinear Forms

Suppose $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix. Then, it readily follows that the function $B: \Bbb R^n\times \Bbb R^n \to \Bbb R$ defined as

$$
B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{y}, \qquad \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \Bbb R^n
$$

is **linear in each argument** separately. That's the reason why we call them *bilinear*.

- For $\mathbf{y}$ fixed, all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \Bbb R^n$, and any real constant $c$, linearity in the first argument means the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(u + v, y) &= B(u, y) + B(v, y) \\
B(cu, y) &= cB(u, y)
\end{aligned}
$$

- For $\mathbf{x}$ fixed, all $\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{z} \in \Bbb R^n$, and any real constant $c$, linearity in the second argument means the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(x, w + z) &= B(x, w) + B(x, z) \\
B(x, cw) &= cB(x, w)
\end{aligned}
$$
