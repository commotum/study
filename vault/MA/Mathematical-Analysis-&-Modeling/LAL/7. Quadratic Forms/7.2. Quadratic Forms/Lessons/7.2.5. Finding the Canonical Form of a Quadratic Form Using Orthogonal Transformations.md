## Introduction

Consider a quadratic form

$$
\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}
$$

where $A$ is a symmetric matrix. As we know, any symmetric matrix can be orthogonally diagonalized as follows:

$$
D = P^T \! AP
$$

where $P$ is an orthogonal matrix and $D$ is a diagonal matrix that contains eigenvalues of $A$ on the main diagonal.

Now, substituting the change of variable

$$
\mathbf{x} = P \mathbf{y}
$$

into our quadratic form, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{T}Ax &= (Py)^{T}A(Py) \\
&= y^{T}(P^{T}AP)y \\
&= y^{T}Dy
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives us a canonical form.

As a result, using the orthogonal diagonalization of $A$, we can find the canonical form of $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}$.

For example, let's find the canonical form of

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})=x_1^2+4x_1x_2+x_2^2
$$

First, we write down the matrix of our quadratic form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

To find the coefficients of the canonical form, we need to calculate the eigenvalues of $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}1 - λ & 2 \\ 2 & 1 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(1 - λ)(1 - λ) - 4 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 2λ - 3 &= 0 \\
(λ + 1)(λ - 3) &= 0 \\
λ &= 3,-1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, writing the eigenvalues in descending order, we obtain the diagonal matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

The corresponding quadratic form after a change of variables is

$$
3y_1^2 - y_2^2
$$

## Finding the Canonical Form of a Quadratic Function by Finding Eigenvalues

**Example:** Using the method of orthogonal transformations, find the canonical form of $Q(\mathbf{x})=x_1^2+x_2^2+2x_3^2+4x_1x_2$.

**Explanation**

First, we write down the matrix of our quadratic form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every quadratic form can be reduced to its canonical (diagonal) form using orthogonal transformations.

To find the coefficients of the canonical form, we need to calculate the eigenvalues of $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}1 - λ & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 - λ & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)\begin{vmatrix}1 - λ & 2 \\ 2 & 1 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)((1 - λ)(1 - λ) - 4) &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)(λ^{2} - 2λ - 3) &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)(λ - 3)(λ + 1) &= 0 \\
λ &= 3, 2,-1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, writing the eigenvalues in descending order, we obtain the diagonal matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

The corresponding quadratic form after a change of variables is

$$
3y_1^2 + 2y_2^2 - y_3^2
$$

## Reducing a Quadratic Function Over the 2D-Space to the Canonical Form Using Orthogonal Transformations

**Example:** Given the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})=2x_1^2-2x_1x_2+2x_2^2$, find its canonical (diagonal) form $\mathbf{y}^T \! D \mathbf{y}$ and an orthogonal matrix $P$ such that $\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{y}$ defines the corresponding change of variables.

**Explanation**

First, we write down the matrix of our quadratic form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every quadratic form can be reduced to its canonical (diagonal) form using orthogonal transformations.

To find the coefficients of the canonical form, we need to calculate the eigenvalues of $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}2 - λ & -1 \\ -1 & 2 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 4λ + 3 &= 0 \\
λ &= 3, 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we need to find an eigenvector basis of $A$.

- For $\lambda_1=3$, we have
$A - 3I|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-3I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_1$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_1 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_2=1$, we have
$A - I|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_2$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_2 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, we construct the diagonal matrix $D$ (writing the eigenvalues in descending order) and the orthogonal matrix $P$ in the usual way, and this gives us the following:
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

## Reducing a Quadratic Function Over the 3D-Space to the Canonical Form Using Orthogonal Transformations

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}$

Consider the matrices given above.
Find an orthogonal matrix $P$ such that $\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{y}$ defines the corresponding change of variables that reduces the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}$ to the canonical (diagonal) form $\mathbf{y}^T \! D \mathbf{y}$.

**Explanation**

Every quadratic form can be reduced to its canonical (diagonal) form using orthogonal transformations.

From the diagonal matrix $D$, we deduce that the eigenvalues of the matrix $A$ are

$\qquad$

$$
\lambda_1=4, \quad
$$

$$
\lambda_2=2, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad\lambda_3=1
$$

Now, we need to find an eigenvector basis of $A$.

- For $\lambda_1=4$, we have
$A - 4I|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3\end{bmatrix} - 4\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-4I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_1$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_1 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_2=2$, we have
$A - 2I|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3\end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_2$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_2 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_3=1$, we have
$A - I|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_3$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_3 \| = 1$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, we construct the orthogonal matrix $P$ in the usual way, and this gives us the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 1 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$
