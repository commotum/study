## Introduction

Let's now consider Jordan canonical forms of $3 \times 3$ matrices.

Let $A$ be a $3\times 3$ matrix. The simplest case is when $A$ has three distinct eigenvalues, say $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, and $\lambda_3$. In this case, $A$ must be diagonalizable. Therefore, the Jordan canonical form of $A$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

For example, consider the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}7 & 1 & -4 \\ 8 & 0 & 2 \\ 4 & -4 & 9\end{bmatrix}$
This matrix has eigenvalues

$$
\lambda_1=8
$$

$$
\lambda_2=5
$$

and

$$
\lambda_3=3
$$

Therefore, its Jordan canonical form is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Remember that a Jordan canonical form of a matrix is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks.

## Finding a Jordan Canonical Form of a 3x3 Matrix With Three Distinct Eigenvalues

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}8 & 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 8 & 8 \\ -4 & -2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$

The matrix above has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=8$, $\lambda_2=7$, and $\lambda_3=4$. Find a Jordan canonical form of the matrix.

**Explanation**

Notice that we are given a $3 \times 3$ matrix that has precisely $3$ distinct eigenvalues.

Therefore, $A$ is diagonalizable, and its Jordan canonical form is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

A Jordan canonical form is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks. So, another Jordan canonical form of $A$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 8\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Jordan Canonical Forms of 3x3 Matrices That Have Two Distinct Eigenvalues

Let's now consider the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 6 & 1 \\ 6 & -7 & 9\end{bmatrix}
$$

The characteristic equation of $A$ is given by

$$
(\lambda-8)^2(\lambda - 3) = 0
$$

Therefore, this matrix has only two distinct eigenvalues, namely,

- $\lambda=8$ of algebraic multiplicity $2$, and
- $\lambda=3$ of algebraic multiplicity $1$.

How can we find its Jordan canonical form?

In this example, we have one $1 \times 1$ Jordan block $J_1(3)$ corresponding to $\lambda=3$.
However, there are two possibilities for the Jordan blocks corresponding to

$$
\lambda=8{:}
$$

- *Case I.* We may get the single $2 \times 2$ Jordan block $J_2(8)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=8$ form the following chain:
$\mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A-8I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A-8I} \mathbf{0}$

- *Case II.* We may get a composition of two $1 \times 1$ Jordan blocks, namely $J_1(8) {\:\oplus\:} J_1(8)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=8$ form the following chains:
$v_{1}→^(A - 8 I)0; v_{2}→^(A - 8 I)0$

To figure out which of the cases we obtain in this situation, let's find the number of linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda=8$.

Computing $A-8I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 8I &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 6 & 1 \\ 6 & -7 & 9\end{bmatrix} - 8\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 6 & -7 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we reduce this matrix to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 8I &= \begin{bmatrix}-4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 6 & -7 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{1}↔R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & 3 & 1 \\ 6 & -7 & 1\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + 4R_{1} \\
&= R_{3}:=R_{3} + (-6)R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 5 \\ 0 & 5 & -5\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{3}:=R_{3} + R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\text{nullity}(A-8I)=1
$$

there will be only one eigenvector that corresponds to the eigenvalue $\lambda=8$, say, $\mathbf{v}_1$. So, the generalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=8$ form the following chain:

$$
\mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A-8I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A-8I} \mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore, we have *case I*, and $A$ has the following Jordan canonical form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 8 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

As always, the Jordan canonical form is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks.

## Finding a Jordan Canonical Form of a 3x3 Matrix With Two Distinct Eigenvalues

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 8 & 8 \\ 4 & 7 & 8 \\ -4 & -8 & -9\end{bmatrix}$

The matrix above has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=-1$, and $\lambda_3=3$. Find a Jordan canonical form of the matrix.

**Explanation**

Notice that we are given a $3 \times 3$ matrix with the following eigenvalues:

- $\lambda=-1$ of algebraic multiplicity $2$
- $\lambda=3$ of algebraic multiplicity $1$

As a result, we have one $1 \times 1$ Jordan block $J_1(3)$ corresponding to $\lambda=3$.
However, there are two possibilities for the Jordan blocks corresponding to

$$
\lambda=-1{:}
$$

- *Case I.* We may get the single $2 \times 2$ Jordan block $J_2(-1)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ form the following chain:
$\mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A+I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A+I} \mathbf{0}$
- *Case II.* We may get a composition of two $1 \times 1$ Jordan blocks, namely $J_1(-1) {\:\oplus\:} J_1(-1)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ form the following chains:
$v_{1}→^(A + I)0; v_{2}→^(A + I)0$

To figure out which of the cases we obtain in this situation, let's find the number of linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda=-1$.

Computing $A+I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A + I &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 8 & 8 \\ 4 & 7 & 8 \\ -4 & -8 & -9\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 8 & 8 \\ 4 & 8 & 8 \\ -4 & -8 & -8\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we reduce this matrix to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A + I &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 8 & 8 \\ 4 & 8 & 8 \\ -4 & -8 & -8\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + (-1)R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}4 & 8 & 8 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -4 & -8 & -8\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{3}:=R_{3} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}4 & 8 & 8 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\text{nullity}(A+I)=2
$$

there will be two eigenvectors that correspond to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$, say, $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$. So, the generalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ form the following chains:

$$
\mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A+I} \mathbf{0} \[5pt] \mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A+I} \mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore, we have *case II*, and $A$ has the following Jordan canonical form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Note:** The Jordan canonical form is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks.

## Jordan Canonical Forms of 3x3 Matrices That Have Only One Eigenvalue

Now, suppose we are given a non-scalar $3 \times 3$ matrix that has only one eigenvalue $\lambda$ of algebraic multiplicity $3$.

Then, there are two possibilities for the Jordan blocks corresponding to $\lambda{:}$

- *Case I.* We may have the single $3 \times 3$ Jordan block $J_3(\lambda)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf v_3$ corresponding to $\lambda$ form the following chain:
$\mathbf{v}_3 \xrightarrow{A-\lambda I} \mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A-\lambda I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A-\lambda I} \mathbf{0}$
- *Case II.* We may have a composition of one $2 \times 2$ and one $1 \times 1$ Jordan block, say $J_2(\lambda) {\:\oplus\:} J_1(\lambda)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf v_3$ corresponding to $\lambda$ form the following chains:
$v_{2}→^(A - λ I)v_{1}→^(A - λ I)0; v_{3}→^(A - λ I)0$

To illustrate this situation, let's consider the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 & 3 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ -2 & 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$
which has eigenvalues

$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=-1
$$

To determine which of the above cases applies to this example, we now find the number of linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda=-1$.

Computing

$$
A-(-1)I=A+I
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A + I &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 & 3 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ -2 & 2 & -3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3 & -3 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 2 & -2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we reduce this matrix to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A + I &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -3 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 2 & -2\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{1}↔R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 1 \\ 3 & -3 & 3 \\ -2 & 2 & -2\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + (-3)R_{1} \\
&= R_{3}:=R_{3} + 2R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the matrix above, there is only one pivot column (the $1$st one). So, the rank of $A+I$ is $1$. Using the rank-nullity theorem, we have that

$$
\begin{aligned}
nullity(A - I) &= 3 - rank(A - I) \\
&= 3 - 1 \\
&= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Recall that the nullity of $A-I$ gives the number of eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda=-1$. Therefore, there must be two such eigenvectors, say, $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_3$.
So, the generalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ form the following chains:

$$
v_{2}→^(A + I)v_{1}→^(A + I)0; v_{3}→^(A + I)0
$$

Therefore, we have *case II*, and $A$ has the following Jordan canonical decomposition:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

which, as we know, is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks.

## Finding a Jordan Canonical Form of a 3x3 Matrix With Only One Eigenvalue

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}4 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

The matrix above has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=2$. Find a Jordan canonical form of the matrix.

**Explanation**

Notice that we are given a non-scalar $3 \times 3$ matrix that has the eigenvalue $\lambda=2$ of algebraic multiplicity $3$.

As a result, there are two possibilities for the Jordan blocks corresponding to

$$
\lambda=2{:}
$$

- *Case I.* We may get the single $3 \times 3$ Jordan block $J_3(2)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf v_3$ corresponding to $\lambda=2$ form the following chain:
$\mathbf{v}_3 \xrightarrow{A-2I} \mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A-2I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A-2I} \mathbf{0}$
- *Case II.* We may get a composition of one $2 \times 2$ and one $1 \times 1$ Jordan block, say $J_2(2) {\:\oplus\:} J_1(2)$. This happens when the generalized eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf v_3$ corresponding to $\lambda=2$ form the following chains:
$v_{2}→^(A - 2 I)v_{1}→^(A - 2 I)0; v_{3}→^(A - 2 I)0$

To figure out which of the cases we obtain in this situation, let's find the number of linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda=2$.

Computing $A-2I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 2I &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we reduce this matrix to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 2I &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{1}↔R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 3 \\ -1 & 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + (-2)R_{1} \\
&= R_{3}:=R_{3} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\text{nullity}(A-2I)=1
$$

there will be only one eigenvector that corresponds to the eigenvalue $\lambda=2$, say, $\mathbf{v}_1$. So, the generalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=2$ form the following chain:

$$
\mathbf{v}_3 \xrightarrow{A-2 I} \mathbf{v}_2 \xrightarrow{A-2 I} \mathbf{v}_1 \xrightarrow{A-2 I} \mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore, we have *case I*, and $A$ has the following Jordan canonical form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Note:** The Jordan canonical form is unique up to the order of the Jordan blocks.

<a id="overview-of-all-possible-jordan-canonical-forms-of-3x3-matrices"></a>
## Overview of All Possible Jordan Canonical Forms of 3x3 Matrices

In general, there are $6$ possible Jordan canonical forms $J$ for $3\times 3$ matrices up to permutations of its Jordan blocks.

- If $A$ has *three* distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, and $\lambda_3$, then $A$ is diagonalizable, and its corresponding Jordan canonical form $J$ is simply the diagonal form of the matrix. In this case, $J$ is a composition of three $1 \times 1$ distinct Jordan blocks:
$\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ_{3}\end{bmatrix}$
- If $A$ has *two* distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, where $\lambda_1$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$, then there are two possible Jordan canonical forms for $A$:If there are two linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda_1$, then $J$ is the composition of two $1\times1$ Jordan blocks corresponding to $\lambda_1$ and a $1 \times 1$ Jordan block corresponding to $\lambda_2{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ_{2}\end{bmatrix}$If there is only one linearly independent eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, then $J$ is the composition of a $2 \times 2$ Jordan block corresponding to $\lambda_1$ and a $1 \times 1$ Jordan block corresponding to $\lambda_2{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ_{2}\end{bmatrix}$
- If $A$ has only *one* eigenvalue $\lambda_1= \lambda_2=\lambda_3= \lambda$, then there are three possible Jordan canonical forms for $A$:If there are three linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda$, then $A$ must be a scalar matrix (meaning that its already diagonalized), and $J=A$ is a composition of three identical $1 \times 1$ Jordan blocks:
$\begin{bmatrix}λ & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & λ & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ\end{bmatrix}$If there are only two linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda$, then $J$ is the composition of a $2 \times 2$ Jordan block corresponding to $\lambda$ and a $1 \times 1$ Jordan block also corresponding to $\lambda$:
$\begin{bmatrix}λ & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & λ & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & λ\end{bmatrix}$Finally, if there is just one linearly independent eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$, then $J$ is a single $3 \times 3$ Jordan block corresponding to $\lambda$:
$\begin{bmatrix}λ & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & λ & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & λ\end{bmatrix}$
