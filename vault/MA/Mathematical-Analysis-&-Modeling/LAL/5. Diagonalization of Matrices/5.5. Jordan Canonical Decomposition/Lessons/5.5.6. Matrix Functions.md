## Introduction

We can extend a scalar function $f(x)$ to a square matrix argument $A$. This function $f(A)$ is called a **matrix function**, and maps a square matrix to another square matrix of the same size.

Given a square matrix $A$ and a scalar function $f$ with Maclaurin series

$$
f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k x^k
$$

we can define $f(A)$ by replacing $x$ with $A$, with addition and multiplication performed in the matrix sense:

$$
f(A) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k A^k
$$

For example, recall that the Maclaurin series of

$$
f(x) = e^x
$$

is

$$
e^x = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \dfrac{x^k}{k!}
$$

Then, for a square matrix $A$, we define the matrix exponential as

$$
e^A = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \dfrac{1}{k!}A^k
$$

In the next slide, we'll see how to apply matrix functions to square matrices.

## Function of a Diagonal Matrix

Suppose $D$ is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{2} & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & λ_{n}\end{bmatrix}$

For a scalar function $f$ with a Maclaurin series expansion, $f(D)$ is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $f(\lambda_1), \ldots, f(\lambda_n){:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}f(λ_{1}) & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & f(λ_{2}) & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & f(λ_{n})\end{bmatrix}$

For example, given the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
and the function

$$
f(x) = e^x
$$

we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}f(2) & 0 \\ 0 & f(1)\end{bmatrix}
$$

This rule works because raising a diagonal matrix to a power is equivalent to raising each diagonal entry to the same power.

Indeed, if

$$
\displaystyle f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k x^k
$$

is the Maclaurin series of $f$, then, by the definition of $f(D)$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(D) &= ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}D^{k} \\
&= ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1} & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{2} & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & λ_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{k} \\
&= ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}\begin{bmatrix}λ_{1}^{k} & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & λ_{2}^{k} & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & λ_{n}^{k}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}λ_{1}^{k} & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}λ_{2}^{k} & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}λ_{n}^{k}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}f(λ_{1}) & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & f(λ_{2}) & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & 0 & ⋯ & f(λ_{n})\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Value of a Function of a Diagonal Matrix

**Example:** Given that
$\begin{bmatrix}100 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 10\end{bmatrix}$
find $\log D$.

**Explanation**

Given a function $f$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ with diagonal entries $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}f(λ_{1}) & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & f(λ_{n})\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log D &= \begin{bmatrix}\log (100) & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \log (1) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \log (10)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Function of a Jordan Block

Suppose $J_n(\lambda)$ is an $n\times n$ Jordan block where the main diagonal entries are all $\lambda{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}λ & 1 & 0 & ⋯ & 0 \\ 0 & λ & 1 & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & 0 & λ & 1 \\ 0 & ⋯ & ⋯ & 0 & λ\end{bmatrix}$

For a scalar function $f$ analytic at $\lambda$ (i.e., has a Taylor series expansion at $\lambda$), $f(J_n(\lambda))$ is the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & (f^{″}(λ))/(2!) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 1)}(λ))/((n - 1)!) \\ 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 2)}(λ))/((n - 2)!) \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) \\ 0 & ⋯ & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ)\end{bmatrix}$

To demonstrate, let's find $f(J_3(2))$ for

$$
f(x) = \ln x
$$

Since $J_3(2)$ is a $3\times 3$ matrix, we will need the first $3-1=2$ derivatives of $f{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \ln x \\
f^{′}(x) &= \frac{1}{x} \\
f^{″}(x) &= -\frac{1}{x^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
Missing \end{bmatrix}
$$

Now that we can compute the value of a function of a Jordan block, we can also find the value of a function of a Jordan matrix.

---

<a id="function-of-a-jordan-matrix"></a>
## Function of a Jordan Matrix

Consider a Jordan matrix formed by the direct sum of blocks $J_{n_1}(\lambda_1) {\textstyle\:\oplus\:} \cdots {\textstyle\:\oplus\:} J_{n_k}(\lambda_k)$ and a function $f$. By applying the rule for diagonal matrices to this block diagonal matrix, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f (J n 1 (λ 1) ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ J n k (λ k)) &= f(\begin{bmatrix}J_{n_{1}}(λ_{1}) & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & J_{n_{k}}(λ_{k})\end{bmatrix}) \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}f(J_{n_{1}}(λ_{1})) & ⋯ & 0 \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & f(J_{n_{k}}(λ_{k}))\end{bmatrix} \\
&= f (J n 1 (λ 1)) ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ f (J n k (λ k))
\end{aligned}
$$

This allows us to compute functions of any matrix in Jordan Normal Form by applying the function to each Jordan block individually.

For example, suppose

$$
J = J_2(2) \oplus J_1(3)
$$

Then, applying a function $f$, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}f(J_{2}(2)) & 0 \\ 0 & f(J_{1}(3))\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="computing-the-value-of-a-function-of-a-jordan-block"></a>
## Computing the Value of a Function of a Jordan Block

**Example:** Find $f\left(J_4(1) \right)$ for $f(x)=2^x$.

**Explanation**

Given a function $f$ and a Jordan block $J_n(\lambda)$, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & (f^{″}(λ))/(2!) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 1)}(λ))/((n - 1)!) \\ 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 2)}(λ))/((n - 2)!) \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) \\ 0 & ⋯ & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ)\end{bmatrix}$

In this case, we have

$$
f(x) = 2^{x}
$$

$n=4$, and $\lambda=1$.

Since $n=4$, we need to compute the first $4-1=3$ derivatives of $f{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= 2^{x} \\
f^{′}(x) &= 2^{x}\ln 2 \\
f^{″}(x) &= 2^{x}(\ln 2)^{2} \\
f^{‴}(x) &= 2^{x}(\ln 2)^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(J_{4}(1)) &= \begin{bmatrix}f(1) & f^{′}(1) & \frac{1}{2!}f^{″}(1) & \frac{1}{3!}f^{‴}(1) \\ 0 & f(1) & f^{′}(1) & \frac{1}{2!}f^{″}(1) \\ 0 & 0 & f(1) & f^{′}(1) \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & f(1)\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2^{1} & 2^{1}(\ln 2) & \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^{1}(\ln 2)^{2} & \frac{1}{6} \cdot 2^{1}(\ln 2)^{3} \\ 0 & 2^{1} & 2^{1}(\ln 2) & \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^{1}(\ln 2)^{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 2^{1} & 2^{1}(\ln 2) \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 2^{1}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 2\ln 2 & (\ln 2)^{2} & \frac{1}{3}(\ln 2)^{3} \\ 0 & 2 & 2\ln 2 & (\ln 2)^{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 2\ln 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Value of a Function of a Jordan Matrix

**Example:** Let $J_n\left(\lambda\right)$ denote the $n \times n$ Jordan block matrix with $\lambda$ on the main diagonal. Then, find
$e^{2\left(J_2(1) {\textstyle\:\oplus\:} J_2(3)\right)}$.

**Explanation**

Given a function $f$ and a Jordan block $J_n(\lambda)$, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & (f^{″}(λ))/(2!) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 1)}(λ))/((n - 1)!) \\ 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) & ⋯ & (f^{(n - 2)}(λ))/((n - 2)!) \\ ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ 0 & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ) & f^{′}(λ) \\ 0 & ⋯ & ⋯ & 0 & f(λ)\end{bmatrix}$

First, note that

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{2(J_{2}(1)⊕J_{2}(3))} &= e^{2(\begin{bmatrix}J_{2}(1) & 0 \\ 0 & J_{2}(3)\end{bmatrix})} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}e^{2(J_{2}(1))} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2(J_{2}(3))}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= e^{2(J_{2}(1))}⊕e^{2(J_{2}(3))}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we compute the function of each Jordan block separately.

- For $J_2(1)$, we have $n=2$ and $\lambda=1$. Since $n=2$, we need to compute the first derivative of $f$ only:
$f(x)|= e^{2x}; f^{′}(x)|= 2e^{2x}$
So, we get
$f(J_{2}(1))|= \begin{bmatrix}f(1) & f^{′}(1) \\ 0 & f(1)\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}e^{2 \cdot 1} & 2e^{2 \cdot 1} \\ 0 & e^{2 \cdot 1}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}e^{2} & 2e^{2} \\ 0 & e^{2}\end{bmatrix}$.
- For $J_2(3)$, we have $n=2$ and $\lambda=3$. Since $n=2$, we need the first derivative of $f$ only, which we have already computed. So, we get
$f(J_{2}(3))|= \begin{bmatrix}f(3) & f^{′}(3) \\ 0 & f(3)\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}e^{2 \cdot 3} & 2e^{2 \cdot 3} \\ 0 & e^{2 \cdot 3}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}e^{6} & 2e^{6} \\ 0 & e^{6}\end{bmatrix}$.

Therefore, we conclude that
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{2} & 2e^{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & e^{6} & 2e^{6} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & e^{6}\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="function-of-a-jordan-decomposition"></a>
## Function of a Jordan Decomposition

Suppose $A$ and $S$ are matrices, where $S$ is invertible.
For a scalar function $f$ with a Maclaurin series expansion, we have

$$
f(SAS^{-1}) = Sf(A)S^{-1}
$$

Indeed, given the Maclaurin series

$$
\displaystyle f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_kx^k
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(SAS^{-1}) &= ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}(SAS^{-1})^{k} \\
&= ∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}SA^{k}S^{-1} \\
&= S(∑_(k = 0)^(∞)c_{k}A^{k})S^{-1} \\
&= Sf(A)S^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

So far, we have seen how to evaluate functions for diagonal matrices and Jordan blocks. With the formula above, we will now be able to

- evaluate $f(B)$ for diagonalizable $B$ using a diagonalization $B=PDP^{-1}$, and
- evaluate $f(B)$ for non-diagonalizable $B$ using a Jordan decomposition $B=PJP^{-1}$.

Let's see how with an example.

---

<a id="computing-the-value-of-a-function-of-a-jordan-decomposition"></a>
## Computing the Value of a Function of a Jordan Decomposition

**Example:** Consider the following matrices:

$\begin{bmatrix}-4 & 1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix}$

Given that $V = PJP^{-1}$, find $\sin\left(\pi V\right)$.

**Explanation**

Given a function $f$ and matrices $A$ and $S$, where $S$ is invertible, we have

$$
f(SAS^{-1}) = S \cdot f(A) \cdot S^{-1}
$$

For

$$
f(x) = \sin(\pi x)
$$

and the given matrix $V$, we have

$$
\sin(\pi V) = \sin\left(\pi{PJP^{-1}}\right) = P \cdot (\sin(\pi J)) \cdot P^{-1}
$$

Now, since $J$ is a Jordan block with $-3$s on the main diagonal, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}f(-3) & f^{′}(-3) \\ 0 & f(-3)\end{bmatrix}
$$

The derivative of $f$ is

$$
f'(x) = \pi \cos \pi x
$$

So, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}f(-3) & f^{′}(-3) \\ 0 & f(-3)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Also, the inverse of $P$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin (πV) &= P \cdot (\sin (πJ)) \cdot P^{-1} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 & - π \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}π & - π \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}π & - π \\ π & - π\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
