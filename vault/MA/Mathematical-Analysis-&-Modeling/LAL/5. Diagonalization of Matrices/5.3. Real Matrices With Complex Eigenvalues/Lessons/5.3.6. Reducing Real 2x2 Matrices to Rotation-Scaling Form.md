## Introduction

Rotation-scaling matrices are involved in an important theorem regarding complex eigenvalues.

To sketch out the key idea, let's consider the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-5 & -10 \\ 4 & 7\end{bmatrix}
$$

which has the complex eigenvalue

$$
\lambda=1-2\textrm{i}={\color{blue}1}+({\color{red}-2})\textrm{i}
$$

and the corresponding complex eigenvector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 - \textrm{i} \\ -1 + \textrm{i}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, we construct a rotation-scaling matrix $C$ using the real and imaginary parts of the eigenvalue $\lambda$ as follows:
$\begin{bmatrix}Re(λ) & Im(λ) \\ -Im(λ) & Re(λ)\end{bmatrix}$
We also construct a matrix $P$ whose columns are the real and imaginary parts of the corresponding eigenvector:
$[\mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid]$

The **rotation-scaling theorem** states that $A$ can be factored as follows:

$$
A=PCP^{-1}
$$

Let's check that this is true in this case:

$$
\begin{aligned}
PCP^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 & -5 \\ 1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-5 & -10 \\ 4 & 7\end{bmatrix} \\
&= A✓
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-rotation-scaling-theorem"></a>
## The Rotation-Scaling Theorem

Let's now formally state the rotation-scaling theorem:

> *Let $A$ be a $2 \times 2$ matrix with real entries. If $\lambda$ is a complex (not real) eigenvalue of $A$, and $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$, then*
> $A = PCP^{-1}$
> *where*
> $[\mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid]$

Note the following:

- When two matrices $A$ and $C$ are related via the equation $A = PCP^{-1}$, we say that they are **similar.**
- The rotation-scaling theorem tells us that every $2 \!\times\! 2$ real matrix with complex (non-real) eigenvalues is similar to a rotation-scaling matrix.
- We call the right-hand side of $A = PCP^{-1}$ the **rotation-scaling form** of $A$. We can also refer to this as the **rotation-scaling decomposition** of $A$.
- The matrix $P$ *must* be constructed from the eigenvector that corresponds to the eigenvalue defined by $C$.

## Finding the Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Matrix in Rotation-Scaling Form

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrix $P$ and the rotation-scaling matrix $C$ shown above. Given that $A=PCP^{-1}$, what are the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of $A$?

**Explanation**

Recall that for any $2 \times 2$ real matrix $A$ with complex eigenvalues, we have $A=PCP^{-1}$, where

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

and $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the complex eigenvalue $\lambda$.

- From the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ - (-1) & 3\end{bmatrix}$ we deduce that $\textrm{Re}(\lambda)={\color{blue}3}$ and $\textrm{Im}(\lambda) = {\color{red}-1}$. So, our eigenvalue is
$\lambda = {\color{blue}3}+({\color{red}-1})\text{i} = 3-\text{i}$.
- From the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ we deduce that our corresponding eigenvector is
$\begin{bmatrix}1 + (-1)\textrm{i} \\ 1 + 0\textrm{i}\end{bmatrix}$

Finally, the second eigenvalue of $A$ must be the complex conjugate of $\lambda$, and the second eigenvector must be the complex conjugate of $\mathbf{v}$.

Therefore, our eigenvalues and eigenvectors are

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 ± \textrm{i} \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Converting a Matrix to Rotation-Scaling Form Given Multiple Parts of the Decomposition

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 + 2\textrm{i}\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the vector $\mathbf{v}$ and the rotation-scaling matrix $C$ shown above. Given that $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=-1-4\textrm{i}$, find a matrix $P$ such that $A=PCP^{-1}$.

**Explanation**

Recall that for any $2 \times 2$ real matrix $A$ with complex eigenvalues, we have $A=PCP^{-1}$, where

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

and $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the complex eigenvalue $\lambda$.

First, notice that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Re(λ) & Im(λ) \\ -Im(λ) & Re(λ)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, we can find the real and imaginary parts of our eigenvector:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 + 2\textrm{i}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(Re(v)) + \textrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(Im(v))
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can now construct $P$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= [ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= Re(v) \mid Im(v) \\
&= \mid \mid \mid ] = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="converting-a-matrix-to-rotation-scaling-form-using-the-complex-conjugate"></a>
## Converting a Matrix to Rotation-Scaling Form Using the Complex Conjugate

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}-2\textrm{i} \\ 7\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the vector $\mathbf{v}$ and the rotation-scaling matrix $C$ shown above. Given that $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda=2+4\textrm{i}$, find a matrix $P$ such that $A=PCP^{-1}$.

**Explanation**

Recall that for any $2 \times 2$ real matrix $A$ with complex eigenvalues, we have $A=PCP^{-1}$, where

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

and $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the complex eigenvalue $\lambda$.

First, notice that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Re(λ) & Im(λ) \\ -Im(λ) & Re(λ)\end{bmatrix}
$$

while

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Re(λ^(―)) & Im(λ^(―)) \\ -Im(λ^(―)) & Re(λ^(―))\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, our rotation-scaling matrix $C$ corresponds to the eigenvalue

$$
\overline{\lambda}=2-4\textrm{i}
$$

the complex conjugate of $\lambda$. Hence, to construct the matrix $P$, we use the eigenvector $\overline{\mathbf{v}}$, the complex conjugate of $\mathbf{v}$.

Next, we find the real and imaginary parts of our eigenvector:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v^(―) &= \begin{bmatrix}2\textrm{i} \\ 7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(Re(v^(―))) + \textrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(Im(v^(―)))
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can now construct $P$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= [ \mid \mid \mid \\
&= Re(v^(―)) \mid Im(v^(―)) \\
&= \mid \mid \mid ] = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 2 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="converting-a-matrix-to-rotation-scaling-form-given-only-one-eigenvalue"></a>
## Converting a Matrix to Rotation-Scaling Form Given Only One Eigenvalue

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1 & -5 \\ 9 & -5\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrices shown above.
Given that $A$ has the complex eigenvalue $\lambda=-2+6\textrm{i}$, and that $A=PCP^{-1}$ where $C$ is a rotation-scaling matrix, find the matrix $P$.

**Explanation**

Recall that for any $2 \times 2$ real matrix $A$ with complex eigenvalues, we have $A=PCP^{-1}$, where

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

and $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to the complex eigenvalue $\lambda$.

Since we are given the eigenvalue

$$
\lambda=-2+6\textrm{i}
$$

we can immediately construct the rotation-scaling matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 6 \\ -6 & -2\end{bmatrix}
$$

To compute the eigenvectors of a matrix, we need to find non-zero solutions to the matrix equation

$$
(A - \lambda I) \textbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
$$

In our case, we have

$$
\,\lambda_1 =-2+6\textrm{i}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - λI &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & -5 \\ 9 & -5\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}-2 + 6\textrm{i} & 0 \\ 0 & -2 + 6\textrm{i}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 - 6\textrm{i} & -5 \\ 9 & -3 - 6\textrm{i}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the homogeneous system of linear equations

$$
{(3 - 6\textrm{i})x_{1} - 5x_{2} = 0; 9x_{1} + (-3 - 6\textrm{i})x_{2} = 0
$$

Since the matrix $A-\lambda I$ is singular, the system above must have a non-zero solution. This means that one equation of the system is a multiple of another. As a result, we can drop one of the rows and take one of the variables as the free variable. For example, from the second equation, we get

$$
x_1=\dfrac 1 3(1+2\textrm{i})x_2
$$

Hence, the general solution is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3}(1 + 2\textrm{i})x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Setting $x_2=3$, we get the eigenvector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 + 2\textrm{i} \\ 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, we can construct the matrix $P$ as follows:

$$
P = [ \mid \mid \mid; Re(v) \mid Im(v); \mid \mid \mid ] = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

## The Geometric Interpretation of Rotation-Scaling Form

Suppose $A$ is a real $2\!\times\! 2$ matrix. To interpret the rotation-scaling form $A=PCP^{-1}$, we should bear in mind the following:

- The matrix $C$ rotates vectors $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^2$ and then scales them.
- We can think of $P$ as representing the following change of variable:
$\mathbf x = P\mathbf u \qquad \Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mathbf u = P^{-1}\mathbf x$

Therefore, $A$ can be thought of as a change of variable from $\mathbf x$ to $\mathbf u$, followed by a rotation and scaling before returning to the original variable.

![](<../Source/5.3.6. Reducing Real 2x2 Matrices to Rotation-Scaling Form/Images/cbd4b95334c42000dbf0d4385179aec7.png>)

For example, let's consider the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{3} + 1 & -2 \\ 1 & \sqrt{3} - 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

This is a real matrix with complex eigenvalues and is thus similar to a rotation-scaling matrix.

Using the techniques described in this lesson, we can show that the rotation-scaling decomposition of $A$ is

$\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
The matrix $C$ can be written explicitly as a constant multiple of a rotation matrix as follows:
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Thus, the matrix $C$ represents the following:

- a rotation of $\arccos\left(\dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\right) = \dfrac\pi 6$ counterclockwise about $O$, and
- an enlargement of scale factor ${\color{blue}{2}}$.
