## Introduction

Given a matrix $A$, an **eigenvector** of $A$ is a *non-zero* vector $\mathbf{v}$ such that

$$
A \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

for some scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. The scalar $\lambda$ is called the **eigenvalue** corresponding to the eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$.

For example, consider the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1\end{bmatrix}$
and the vector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}$ Computing the product $A \cdot \mathbf{v}$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= (-2) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&=-2v
\end{aligned}
$$

We've discovered that

$$
A {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}} = {\color{red}-2} {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}}
$$

Therefore,

- the vector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}$ is an *eigenvector* of $A$, and
- the scalar $\color{red}\lambda=-2$ is the *eigenvalue* corresponding to the eigenvector ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}}$.

**Caution:** Remember that an eigenvector must be a *non-zero* vector. This is very important.

<a id="finding-the-eigenvalue-that-corresponds-to-a-given-eigenvector"></a>
## Finding the Eigenvalue that Corresponds to a Given Eigenvector

**Example:** Determine if $1; 1$ is an eigenvector of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ If so, then what is the corresponding eigenvalue?

**Explanation**

The eigenvectors of $A$ are the non-zero vectors $\mathbf{v}$ such that

$$
A\mathbf{v}=\lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

We have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 4 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 4v
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
A\mathbf{v}=4 \mathbf{v}
$$

we conclude that $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ and the corresponding eigenvalue is $\lambda=4$.

<a id="the-geometric-interpretation-of-eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors"></a>
## The Geometric Interpretation of Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues can be interpreted geometrically.

First, recall that if the matrix $A$ is the standard matrix of some linear transformation $\mathbf A$, then the product $A\mathbf x$ gives the image of $\mathbf x$ under the action of $\mathbf A$. Under the action of $\mathbf A$, the image of $\mathbf x$ can appear quite different to the original vector $\mathbf x$.

However, for an eigenvector $\mathbf v$ with corresponding eigenvalue $\lambda$, we get

$$
A\mathbf v = \lambda \mathbf v
$$

In other words, when the matrix $A$ is applied to an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$, the image of $\mathbf v$ is a scaled version of the original vector $\mathbf v$, and the scale factor is $\lambda$.

To demonstrate, consider once again the following matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

We've already seen that $A$ has an eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}$ with corresponding eigenvalue

$$
{\color{black}\lambda=-2}
$$

So we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Plotting the vector $\mathbf v$ and its image $-2\mathbf v$ in the plane, we get the following picture.

![](<../Source/5.1.1. The Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a 2x2 Matrix/Images/9d8976848db07d0cdf8392b21f15e54d.png>)

In general, when we apply a matrix $A$ to an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$, the resulting vector

$$
A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
$$

always lies on the same line as $\mathbf{v}$.

If $\mid \lambda \mid > 1$, then the matrix stretches the vector. For $\mid \lambda \mid < 1$, the matrix compresses the vector. In addition, a negative eigenvalue $\lambda < 0$ means that $\mathbf v$ is flipped in the opposite direction as well as scaled.

<a id="determining-the-value-of-an-unknown-given-an-eigenvalue-and-eigenvector-of-a-matrix"></a>
## Determining the Value of an Unknown Given an Eigenvalue and Eigenvector of a Matrix

**Example:** An eigenvector of $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ corresponding to an eigenvalue $\lambda=6c$ is $1; 2c$ Determine the value of $c$.

**Explanation**

Remember that the eigenvectors of $A$ are the non-zero vectors $\mathbf{v}$ such that

$$
A\mathbf{v}=\lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av &= λv \\
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2c\end{bmatrix} &= 6c\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2c\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}2 + 2c \\ 1 + 4c\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}6c \\ 12c^{2}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us the following system of linear equations:

$$
{2 + 2c = 6c; 1 + 4c = 12c^{2}
$$

Solving for $c$ in the first equation, we get

$$
2+2c=6c \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad c = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

Now, substituting

$$
c=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

into the second equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 + 4c &= 12c^{2} \\
1 + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} &= 12(\frac{1}{2})^{2} \\
3 &= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

which is a true statement.

Therefore, the solutions of the system is

$$
c=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

.

<a id="identifying-true-statements-about-eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors"></a>
## Identifying True Statements About Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

**Example:** Which of the following statements are true?

1. $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 4 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ that corresponds to the eigenvalue $2$.
2. $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ that corresponds to the eigenvalue $0$.
3. If $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of a matrix $A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda = 5$, then $2\mathbf{v}$ is also an eigenvector of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda = 5$.

**Explanation**

Remember that the eigenvectors of $A$ are the non-zero vectors $\mathbf{v}$ such that

$$
A\mathbf{v}=\lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

With that in mind, let's examine the statements one by one.

- Statement I is true. We have
$Av|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 4 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 8\end{bmatrix}; = 2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}; = 2v$.
Since $A\mathbf{v}=2 \mathbf{v}$, we conclude that $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ and the corresponding eigenvalue is $\lambda=2$.
- Statement II is false. An eigenvector must be a *non-zero* vector.
- Statement III is true. We have
$A(2v) = 2Av = 2(5v) = 5(2v)$.
Since, $A(2\mathbf{v})=5(2 \mathbf{v})$, and $2 \mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$, this implies that $2 \mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ and the corresponding eigenvalue is $\lambda = 5$.

Therefore, the correct answer is, "I and III only."

**Note:** Statement III is true in the general case, too. In general, any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector, and the corresponding eigenvalue is the same.
