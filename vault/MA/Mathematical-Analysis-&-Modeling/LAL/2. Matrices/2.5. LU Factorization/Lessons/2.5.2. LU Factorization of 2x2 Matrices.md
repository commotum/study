## Introduction

In the same way that we can factor numbers or polynomials, we can factor a matrix by writing it as a product of two or more matrices. In fact, there is one factorization technique that works for *all* matrices, called **LU factorization** (or **LU decomposition**).

LU factorization involves writing a matrix $A$ as a product of two matrices $L$ and $U$, where

- $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ ∗ & 1\end{bmatrix}$ is a *unit lower triangular matrix* (a lower triangular matrix that has only $1$'s on the main diagonal), and
- $\begin{bmatrix}∗ & ∗ \\ 0 & ∗\end{bmatrix}$ is an *upper triangular matrix*.

For example, we can write the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 6 & -6\end{bmatrix}$ in LU factored form as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 6 & -6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(L) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(U)
$$

You may be wondering how we arrived at this factorization. Shortly, we'll discuss an algorithm that explains how to compute $L$ and $U$ for any $2\times2$ matrix $A$. But for now, let's focus on recognizing LU factored forms.

<a id="identifying-lu-factorizations"></a>
## Identifying LU Factorizations

**Example:** Which of the following products is the LU factorization of a matrix $A$?

1. $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
2. $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$
3. $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Remember that in the LU factorization of a matrix $A$, we write $A=LU$ where the matrix $L$ is a unit lower triangular matrix (a lower triangular matrix that has only $1$'s on the main diagonal) and the matrix $U$ is an upper triangular matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ ∗ & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

With that in mind, let's inspect each product in turn.

- The first product is in $LU$ form because the left matrix is a unit lower triangular matrix, and the right matrix is an upper triangular matrix.
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
- The second product is *not* in $LU$ form. Although the left matrix is a lower triangular matrix, it is not a *unit* lower triangular matrix because it does not have $1$'s on the diagonal.
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$
- The third product is *not* in $LU$ form because the matrix on the right is not an upper triangular matrix, as it has a non-zero entry below the diagonal.
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only."

<a id="an-algorithm-for-finding-the-lu-factorization-of-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## An Algorithm for Finding the LU Factorization of a 2x2 Matrix

Let $\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 6 & -6\end{bmatrix}$ To perform LU factorization, we need to write $A$ as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 6 & -6\end{bmatrix}
$$

To find the matrices $L$ and $U$, we reduce the matrix $A$ to row echelon form. Whilst doing this, we simultaneously write down the row operations applied to $A$ in terms of matrix multiplication using elementary matrices:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 6 & -6\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + 3R_{1}⟺\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(E_{1}) \cdot A \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= U
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix is upper triangular, so it is our matrix $U$. Now, since $E_1A=U$, we can invert $E_1$ to arrive at the desired LU factorization:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1}A &= U \\
A &= E_{1}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(L) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(U)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Caution:** To get a unit lower triangular matrix at the end, we have the following restriction while reducing a matrix $A$ to row echelon form:

- we can only add a multiple of the $1$st row to the $2$nd row ($R_2:=R_2+k R_1$), and
- the other elementary row operations (swapping or multiplying a row by a number) are not allowed.

<a id="finding-the-lu-factorization-of-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## Finding the LU Factorization of a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Given $\begin{bmatrix}7 & 9 \\ -7 & -6\end{bmatrix}$ find its LU decomposition.

**Explanation**

To find the matrices $L$ and $U$, we reduce the matrix $A$ to row echelon form. Whilst doing this, we simultaneously write down the row operations applied to $A$ in terms of matrix multiplication using elementary matrices.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{bmatrix}7 & 9 \\ -7 & -6\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + 1 \cdot R_{1}⟺\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(E_{1}) \cdot A \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}7 & 9 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= U
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix is upper triangular, so it is our matrix $U$. Now, since $E_1A=U$, we can invert $E_1$ to arrive at the desired LU factorization:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1}A &= U \\
A &= E_{1}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(L)\begin{bmatrix}7 & 9 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(U)
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the LU Factorization of a Singular 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Given
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 \\ 12 & 9\end{bmatrix}$, find its LU decomposition.

**Explanation**

To find the matrices $L$ and $U$, we reduce the matrix $A$ to row echelon form. Whilst doing this, we simultaneously write down the row operations applied to $A$ in terms of matrix multiplication using elementary matrices.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 \\ 12 & 9\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + (-3)R_{1}⟺\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(E_{1}) \cdot A \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= U
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix is upper triangular, so it is our matrix $U$. Now, since $E_1A=U$, we can invert $E_1$ to arrive at the desired LU factorization:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1}A &= U \\
A &= E_{1}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}U \\
A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(L)\begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(U)
\end{aligned}
$$
