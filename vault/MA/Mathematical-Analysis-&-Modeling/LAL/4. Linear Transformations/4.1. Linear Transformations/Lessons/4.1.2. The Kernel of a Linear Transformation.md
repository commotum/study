## Introduction

The **kernel** of a linear transformation is the set of vectors that map to the zero vector. We define it formally as follows:

> *For a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$, the **kernel** of $\mathbf{T}$ is the set of all the vectors $\mathbf{u}$ such that $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}$. We can write this as*
> $\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{Ker}(\mathbf{T}) = \left\{\;\mathbf{u} \; & \; \mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0} \right\}\end{bmatrix}$.

The diagram below shows the kernel of a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}: V \rightarrow V$ in a vector space $V$.

![](<../Source/4.1.2. The Kernel of a Linear Transformation/Images/440e63deccaf707e7d4cd12fc39b5db5.png>)

Notice that, because of linearity, the zero vector is always in the kernel of a linear transformation:

$$
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{T}(0\cdot\mathbf{v})=0\cdot\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})=0
$$

for any vector $\mathbf{v}$.

<a id="determining-whether-given-vectors-belong-to-the-kernel-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## Determining Whether Given Vectors Belong to the Kernel of a Linear Transformation

**Example:** Consider the linear transformation
$\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$
and the vectors
$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
Which of the given vectors belong to the kernel of $\mathbf{T}$?

**Explanation**

A vector $\mathbf{u}$ belongs to the kernel of a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ if

$$
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
$$

Therefore, we need to compute $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_i)$ for each vector $\mathbf v_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(v_{1}) &= T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}) = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
T(v_{2}) &= T(\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}) = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
T(v_{3}) &= T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}) = \begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 12\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\,\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_1)=\mathbf{0}
$$

we conclude that, of the given vectors, the only vector that belongs to the kernel of $\,\mathbf{T}$ is $\,\mathbf{v}_1$.

<a id="calculating-a-component-of-a-vector-in-the-kernel-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## Calculating a Component of a Vector in the Kernel of a Linear Transformation

**Example:** Consider the linear transformation $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{bmatrix}$ and the vector $\begin{bmatrix}k \\ 2 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}$ Find the value of $\,k\,$ such that $\,\mathbf{u}\in\textrm{Ker}(\mathbf{T})$.

**Explanation**

A vector $\mathbf{u}$ belongs to the kernel of a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ if

$$
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
$$

So, we need to solve the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(u) &= 0 \\
\begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot k + 12 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 6 \\ -2 \cdot k + 4 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot 6 \\ 6 \cdot k - 3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 6\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}3k + 6 \\ -2k - 4 \\ 6k + 12\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we get the system

$$
{3k + 6 = 0; - 2k - 4 = 0; 6k + 12 = 0
$$

The solution to all three of these equations is $k=-2$.

<a id="finding-the-kernel-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## Finding the Kernel of a Linear Transformation

We know how to determine whether a given vector is in the kernel of a linear transformation. But how do we find the kernel of a linear transformation?

First, remember that if $T$ is the standard matrix of $\mathbf{T}$, then

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{Ker}(\mathbf{T}) = \textrm{Null}(T) = \left\{\;\mathbf{x} \; & \; T\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0} \right\}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, to find all the vectors in the kernel of a linear transformation, we just need to find the null space of the corresponding standard matrix. We can do this by solving the matrix equation

$$
T\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
$$

---

<a id="finding-the-kernel-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## Finding the Kernel of a Linear Transformation

**Example:** Consider the linear transformation $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ Find $\textrm{Ker}(\mathbf{T})$.

**Explanation**

Finding the kernel of $\mathbf{T}$ is equivalent to finding the null space of the standard matrix of $\mathbf{T}$.

For the given linear transformation, the standard matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6\end{bmatrix}$

Setting $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ we then need to solve the equation

$$
T\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
$$

First, we reduce $T$ to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + (-\frac{3}{2})R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The basic variables are the variables whose coefficients are pivots. In this case, the basic variable is $x$, and we have one free variable, $y$. That means we need to find $x$ in terms of $y$.

From the first equation, we get $x=-2y$.

Therefore, the kernel of the linear transformation $\,\mathbf{T}$ is $\begin{bmatrix}-2y \\ y\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="the-nullity-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## The Nullity of a Linear Transformation

The **nullity** of a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ is the dimension of its kernel. This means that

$$
\text{nullity}(\mathbf{T}) = \dim(\textrm{Ker}(\mathbf{T})) = \text{nullity}(T)
$$

To compute the dimension of the kernel of $\mathbf{T}$, we just have to reduce the matrix $T$ to row echelon form and count the number of non-pivot columns.

---

<a id="finding-the-nullity-of-a-linear-transformation"></a>
## Finding the Nullity of a Linear Transformation

**Example:** Given the linear transformation $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{bmatrix}$ find $\,\textrm{nullity}(\mathbf{T})$.

**Explanation**

First, we write down the standard matrix of $\mathbf{T}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 4 & 10 & -1 \\ -6 & -12 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Recall that

$$
\textrm{nullity}(\mathbf{T}) = \textrm{nullity}(T) = \textrm{dim}(\textrm{Null}(T))
$$

In order to find $\textrm{dim}(\textrm{Null}(T))$, we reduce $T$ to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 4 & 10 & -1 \\ -6 & -12 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2} &:=R_{2} + (-2)R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \\ -6 & -12 & 1\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} + 3R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has no non-pivot columns. Therefore,

$$
\textrm{nullity}(\mathbf{T})=\textrm{dim}(\textrm{Null}(T))=0
$$
