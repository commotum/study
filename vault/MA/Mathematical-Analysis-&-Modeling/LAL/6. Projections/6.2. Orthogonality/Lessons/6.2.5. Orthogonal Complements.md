## Introduction

If a vector $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to all the vectors in a set $W$, we say that $\mathbf{u}$ is **orthogonal to $W$,** and we write $\mathbf{u} \perp W$.

For example, consider the set of vectors $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ Can we find a *non-zero* vector $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$ that is orthogonal to both vectors in this set?

We know that if such a vector $\mathbf u$ does exist, then the dot product of $\mathbf u$ with each vector in the set will be zero:

$$
{u \cdot w_{1} = 0; u \cdot w_{2} = 0
$$

So let's calculate these dot products:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u \cdot w_{1} &= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = x_{1} + x_{2} \\
u \cdot w_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = 2x_{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, to find the components of $\mathbf{u}$, we just need to solve the following system of equations:

$$
{x_{1} + x_{2} = 0; 2x_{2} = 0
$$

Notice that the system's matrix is already in echelon form and has $2$ pivot columns (the $1$st and $2$nd). So, $x_3$ is a free variable.
Now, from the second equation, we have $x_2=0$. Substituting this into the first equation, we obtain $x_1=0$.

Setting $x_3=1$, we get the vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, we have found a vector $\mathbf{u}$ that is orthogonal to both $\mathbf{w}_1$ and $\mathbf{w}_2$.

## Identifying True Statements About Orthogonality

**Example:** For the vector $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}$ which of the following statements are true?

- $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to the set $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to the set $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ -\frac{1}{6}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of $H$ and $V$ as $\{\mathbf{h}_1,\mathbf{h}_2\}$ and $\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\}$ respectively.

Now, we have to compute the dot product between $\mathbf{u}$ and the vectors in each set.

- For the set $H$:
$u \cdot h_{1}|= 3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-6) + 6 \cdot 1 = 0, ✓; u \cdot h_{2}|= 3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-3) + 6 \cdot (-1) =-6 \ne 0\cdot$
So $\mathbf u \perp \mathbf h_1$ but $\mathbf u \not\perp \mathbf h_2$. Because $\mathbf u$ is not orthogonal to every vector in $H$, we have $\mathbf{u}\not \perp H$.

- For the set $V$:
$u \cdot v_{1}|= 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-1) + 6 \cdot (-\frac{1}{6}) = 0✓; u \cdot v_{2}|= 3 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 0 = 0✓$
So $\mathbf u \perp \mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf u \perp \mathbf v_2$. Because $\mathbf u$ is orthogonal to every vector in $V$, we have $\mathbf{u}\perp V$.

<a id="calculating-the-components-of-a-vector-orthogonal-to-a-set"></a>
## Calculating the Components of a Vector Orthogonal to a Set

**Example:** Given that $\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b \\ c\end{bmatrix}$ is a non-zero vector that is orthogonal to the set $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ find the value of $\dfrac{a+c}{b}$.

**Explanation**

The vector $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to the set $W$ if

$$
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}_1=0
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}_2=0
$$

So, we need to solve the following system:

$$
{u \cdot w_{1} = 0; u \cdot w_{2} = 0
$$

We write the corresponding augmented matrix $M$ and reduce it to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + (-1)R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, we have $2$ pivot columns (the $1$st and $2$nd ones), so $x_3$ is a free variable. From the second equation, we get $x_2=-x_3$. Substituting this into the first equation, we get $x_1=-3x_3$. So, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-3x_{3} \\ -x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Setting $x_3=1$, we get the vector
$\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, we obtain $a=-3$, $b=-1$, and $c=1$.

Finally,

$$
\dfrac{a+c}{b}=\dfrac{-3+1}{-1}=2
$$

## Orthogonal Complements

Now, let's consider a *subspace* $H$. The set containing all vectors $\mathbf{v}$ that are orthogonal to $H$ is called the **orthogonal complement** of $H$, and we denote it by $H^{\perp}$.

For example, the orthogonal complement $H^\perp$ of the 2D-plane $H$ passing through the origin in the three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ (shown below) is the line that passes through the origin and is perpendicular to the plane.

![](<../Source/6.2.5. Orthogonal Complements/Images/92318c28fb12d1a1280b87acfaf21ba4.png>)

How can we check whether a vector $\mathbf v$ belongs to $H^{\perp}$? Sometimes, $H$ can contain many vectors (often infinitely many), so it may not be possible to check that $\mathbf v$ is orthogonal to every individual vector in $H$.

To check whether a vector belongs to $H^{\perp}$, we use the following theorem:

> *A vector $\mathbf{v}$ belongs to $H^{\perp}$ if it is orthogonal to a set of vectors that spans $H$.*

In other words:

> *If $H=\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{h}_1,\mathbf{h}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_n\}$, then $\mathbf{v} \in H^{\perp}$ if and only if $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to $\{\mathbf{h}_1,\mathbf{h}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_n\}$.*

For example, if the 2D-plane $H$ passing through the origin in the three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ (shown below) is spanned by the vectors $\mathbf{h}_1$ and $\mathbf{h}_2$, then the orthogonal complement $H^\perp$ of $H$ consists of all vectors that are perpendicular to both $\mathbf{h}_1$ and $\mathbf{h}_2$.

![](<../Source/6.2.5. Orthogonal Complements/Images/e0fa79fe4da36d8db92d8159e86a6c8a.png>)

Some other important things to notice about $H^{\perp}$ are as follows:

- $H^{\perp}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$
- $(H^{\perp})^{\perp} = H$
- $H \cap H^{\perp} = \{\mathbf 0\}$

<a id="identifying-which-vectors-belong-to-the-orthogonal-complement-of-a-set"></a>
## Identifying Which Vectors Belong to the Orthogonal Complement of a Set

**Example:** Consider the vector subspace $\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ -1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ Which of the following vectors belong to $H^{\perp}$?
$\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 5 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors that span the subspace $H$ as $\mathbf{h}_1$ and $\mathbf{h}_2$.

Now, a vector $\mathbf{v}$ belongs to $H^{\perp}$ if $\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to the set of vectors that spans the subspace $H$. So, we have to find the products $\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{h}_j$ for $i=1,2,3$ and $j=1,2$.

- For $\mathbf{v}_1$, we have the following:
$v_{1} \cdot h_{1}|= 3 \cdot (-2) + 5 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot 3 = 1 \ne 0 \mid \cdot$
So $\mathbf v_1 \not\perp \mathbf h_1$, which means $\mathbf{v}_1 \notin H^{\perp}$. We do not need to test $\mathbf v_1\cdot \mathbf h_2$.
- For $\mathbf{v}_2$, we have the following:
$v_{2} \cdot h_{1}|= 2 \cdot (-2) + 4 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot 3 = 4 \ne 0 \mid \cdot$
So $\mathbf v_2 \not\perp \mathbf h_1$, which means $\mathbf{v}_2 \notin H^{\perp}$. We do not need to test $\mathbf v_2\cdot \mathbf h_2$.
- For $\mathbf{v}_3$, we have the following:
$\begin{bmatrix}v_{3} \cdot h_{1}|= 5 \cdot (-2) + 8 \cdot (-1) + 6 \cdot 3 = 0 & ✓ \\ v_{3} \cdot h_{2}|= 5 \cdot 2 + 8 \cdot (-2) + 6 \cdot 1 = 0 & ✓\end{bmatrix}$
So $\mathbf v_3 \perp \mathbf h_1$ and $\mathbf v_3 \perp \mathbf h_2$, which means $\mathbf{v}_3 \in H^{\perp}$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "$\mathbf{v}_3$ only".

<a id="finding-the-orthogonal-complement-of-a-span"></a>
## Finding the Orthogonal Complement of a Span

**Example:** Find $H^{\perp}$ if $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors that span the subspace $H$ as $\mathbf{h}_1$ and $\mathbf{h}_2$.

A vector $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$ belongs to $H^{\perp}$ if

$$
\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{h}_1=0
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{h}_2=0
$$

So, we need to solve the following system:

$$
{x \cdot h_{1} = 0; x \cdot h_{2} = 0
$$

We write the corresponding augmented matrix $M$ and reduce it to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} - \frac{1}{2}R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{3}{2} & \frac{1}{2} & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, we have $2$ pivot columns (the $1$st and the $2$nd ones), so $x_3$ is a free variable. From the second equation we get

$$
x_2=-\dfrac{1}{3}x_3
$$

Substituting this into first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1 = -\dfrac{1}{3}x_3
$$

So, the general solution to the system is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{3}x_{3} \\ -\frac{1}{3}x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, our orthogonal complement is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{3}x_{3} \\ -\frac{1}{3}x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$
