## Introduction

The vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$, paired with the dot product, is called a **Euclidean space**.

For a vector $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the **norm** (or **length**) is given by

$$
\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert = \sqrt{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{x}} = \sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+\ldots+x_n^2}
$$

For example, consider the vector $\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 12\end{bmatrix}$ The norm of this vector is calculated as

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖x‖ &= \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-5)^{2} + (12)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{25 + 144} \\
&= \sqrt{169} \\
&= 13
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that the dot product of a vector with itself is always non-negative,

$$
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq 0
$$

so it's always possible to take the square root $\sqrt{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}}$.

---

<a id="calculating-the-norm-of-a-vector"></a>
## Calculating the Norm of a Vector

**Example:** Given $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -4 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}$ find $\|\mathbf{x}\|$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the norm, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖x‖ &= \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2} + x_{3}^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{3^{2} + (-4)^{2} + (-5)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{9 + 16 + 25} \\
&= \sqrt{50} \\
&= 5\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-distance-between-two-vectors"></a>
## The Distance Between Two Vectors

In a Euclidean space, the **distance** between the vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ is the norm of the difference $\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}$, that is

$$
\text{d}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \Vert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \Vert
$$

To create a formula for the distance, we write the vectors in component form:
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix}$

Now, we can express the norm in terms of the components:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, y) &= ‖x - y‖ \\
&= \sqrt{(x_{1} - y_{1})^{2} + (x_{2} - y_{2})^{2} + ⋯ + (x_{n} - y_{n})^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, we can use the formula to find the distance between the vectors $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, y) &= ‖x - y‖ \\
&= \sqrt{(x_{1} - y_{1})^{2} + (x_{2} - y_{2})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(1 - (-5))^{2} + (4 - 1)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{6^{2} + 3^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{45} \\
&= 3\sqrt{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-distance-between-two-vectors"></a>
## Finding the Distance Between Two Vectors

**Example:** Given the vectors $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ find the distance $\text{d}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the distance, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, y) &= ‖x - y‖ \\
&= \sqrt{(x_{1} - y_{1})^{2} + (x_{2} - y_{2})^{2} + (x_{3} - y_{3})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-1 - 2)^{2} + (2 - 0)^{2} + (3 - (-1))^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-3)^{2} + 2^{2} + 4^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{9 + 4 + 16} \\
&= \sqrt{29}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="factoring-a-constant-out-of-the-norm"></a>
## Factoring a Constant Out of the Norm

We can factor a constant out of the norm, provided that we take the absolute value of the constant:

$$
\Vert k \mathbf{x} \Vert= \vert k \vert \cdot \Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert
$$

For example, consider the vector $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$ To compute the norm of a multiple of $\mathbf{x}$, say ${\color{blue}-2}\mathbf{x}$, we can factor the absolute value $\mid {\color{blue}-2} \mid$ out of the norm as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖- 2x‖ &= \mid - 2 \mid \cdot ‖x‖ \\
&= 2 \cdot \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2}} \\
&= 2 \cdot \sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}} \\
&= 2 \cdot \sqrt{9 + 16} \\
&= 2 \cdot \sqrt{25} \\
&= 2 \cdot 5 \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note:** We can verify that we'd get the same result if we multiplied the $\color{blue}-2$ first, before computing the norm. Multiplying by ${\color{blue}-2}$, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then, computing the norm, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖- 2x‖ &= \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-6)^{2} + (-8)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{36 + 64} \\
&= \sqrt{100} \\
&= 10.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="factoring-a-constant-out-of-the-norm"></a>
## Factoring a Constant Out of the Norm

**Example:** If $\left\Vert {-\dfrac{\mathbf{x}}{2}} \right\Vert = 5$, find $\Vert 3 \mathbf{x} \Vert$.

**Explanation**

Remember that we can factor a constant out of the norm, provided that we take the absolute value of the constant:

$$
\Vert k \mathbf{x} \Vert= \vert k \vert \cdot \Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert
$$

Using this fact, we can solve for the norm $\Vert x \Vert$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖- \frac{x}{2}‖ &= 5 \\
‖- \frac{1}{2}x‖ &= 5 \\
\mid - \frac{1}{2} \mid \cdot ‖x‖ &= 5 \\
\frac{1}{2} \cdot ‖x‖ &= 5 \\
‖x‖ &= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we can evaluate $\Vert 3 \mathbf{x} \Vert$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖3x‖ &= 3 \cdot ‖x‖ \\
&= 3 \cdot 10 \\
&= 30
\end{aligned}
$$

## Normalizing a Vector

A **unit vector** is a vector whose length is equal to $1$. If we divide a *non-zero* vector $\mathbf{x}$ by its norm $\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert$, we obtain a unit vector

$$
\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\Vert\mathbf{x}\Vert}
$$

since

$$
\Vert \mathbf{v} \Vert =\left\Vert \dfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert} \right\Vert = \dfrac{1}{\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert}\cdot\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert = 1
$$

The process of dividing a vector $\mathbf{x}$ by its norm $\Vert \mathbf{x} \Vert$ is called **normalizing the vector $\mathbf{x}$.** We say that the unit vector

$$
\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\Vert\mathbf{x}\Vert}
$$

is *in the same direction* as $\mathbf{x}$.

---

<a id="normalizing-a-vector"></a>
## Normalizing a Vector

**Example:** Normalize the vector $\begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

To normalize a vector $\mathbf{x}$, we have to divide the vector by its norm:

$$
\dfrac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}
$$

Therefore, to normalize $\mathbf{x}$, we first calculate $\|\mathbf{x}\|$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖x‖ &= \sqrt{x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-8)^{2} + 6^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{64 + 36} \\
&= \sqrt{100} \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we divide $\mathbf{x}$ by its length:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x}{‖x‖} &= \frac{x}{10} \\
&= \frac{1}{10}x \\
&= \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{-8}{10} \\ \frac{6}{10}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{4}{5} \\ \frac{3}{5}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
