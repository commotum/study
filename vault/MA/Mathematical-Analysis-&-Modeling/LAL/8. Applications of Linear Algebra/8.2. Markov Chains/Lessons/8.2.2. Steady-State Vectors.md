## Introduction

Suppose we have a population where people live either in the city or the countryside. A study carried out by the local authority shows that, each year,

- $6\%$ of the city population relocates to the countryside,
- $3\%$ of the countryside population relocates to the city, and
- everyone else remains where they are.

Additionally, $70\%$ of the population lived in the city and $30\%$ in the countryside at the time of the study.

We can represent this situation using a stochastic matrix $P$ and initial state vector $\mathbf x_0$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0.94 & 0.03 \\ 0.06 & 0.97\end{bmatrix}
$$

As we've seen, to find the state vector $\mathbf x_{k+1}$ that corresponds to the $(k+1)$th year, we left-multiply the state vector corresponding to the $k$th year by $P$. This gives rise to the following Markov chain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1} &= Px_{0}, \\
x_{2} &= Px_{1}, \\
x_{3} &= Px_{2}, \\
⋮\begin{vmatrix}⋮ \\ x_{k + 1} &= Px_{k} \\ ⋮\end{vmatrix}⋮
\end{aligned}
$$

The local authority wants to determine whether the state vectors settle down to a fixed state. With the aid of a computer, they compute the following states:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1} &= Px_{0} = \begin{bmatrix}0.667 \\ 0.333\end{bmatrix} \\
x_{2} &= Px_{1} = \begin{bmatrix}0.63697 \\ 0.36303\end{bmatrix} \\
x_{3} &= Px_{2} = \begin{bmatrix}0.60964 \\ 0.39036\end{bmatrix} \\
&=⋮ \\
x_{100} &= Px_{99} = \begin{bmatrix}0.333363 \\ 0.666637\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix} \\
x_{101} &= Px_{100} = \begin{bmatrix}0.33336 \\ 0.66664\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Looking at the data, we see that the vectors $\mathbf x_{100}$ and $\mathbf x_{101}$ are both approximately equal to the vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Furthermore, computing $\mathbf x_{102}, \mathbf{x}_{103}, \ldots$ all give vectors approximately equal to $\mathbf q$. This suggests that the sequence $\mathbf x_k$ converges to $\mathbf q$:

$$
x_k \rightarrow \mathbf q \quad\textrm{as}\quad k\to\infty
$$

The vector $\mathbf q$ is called a **steady-state vector** of the matrix $P$. Here, our steady-state vector suggests that after enough time has passed, one-third of the population will live in the city and two-thirds will live in the countryside.

Finally, notice that for large values of $k$, we have

$$
P\mathbf x_k \approx \mathbf x_{k+1}
$$

This observation indicates how to find $\mathbf q$ without resorting to computers.

<a id="steady-state-vectors"></a>
## Steady-State Vectors

Suppose we have a stochastic matrix $P$ and a stochastic vector $\mathbf{q}$. Then, $\mathbf{q}$ is a **steady-state vector** of $P$ if

$$
P\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}
$$

For example, to show that the stochastic vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

is a steady-state vector for the stochastic matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}0.94 & 0.03 \\ 0.06 & 0.97\end{bmatrix}$

we compute the matrix product $P\mathbf q\mathbin$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Pq &= \begin{bmatrix}0.94 & 0.03 \\ 0.06 & 0.97\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}0.94 & 0.03 \\ 0.06 & 0.97\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}0.94 + 2 \cdot 0.03 \\ 0.06 + 2 \cdot 0.97\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= q✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
P\mathbf q = \mathbf q
$$

the vector $\mathbf q$ is a steady-state vector of $P$.

## Identifying Steady-State Vectors of Stochastic Matrices

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}0.4 & 0.2 \\ 0.6 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}$

Consider the stochastic matrix $P$ and vector $\mathbf{q}$ shown above. Which of the following statements are true?

1. $\mathbf{q}$ is a stochastic vector
2. $P\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}$
3. $\mathbf{q}$ is a steady-state vector of $P$

**Explanation**

Recall that a steady-state vector of a stochastic matrix $P$ is a stochastic vector $\mathbf{q}$ such that

$$
P\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}
$$

With that in mind, let's examine our statements.

- Statement I is true. Indeed, $\mathbf{q}$ is a stochastic vector since its entries are non-negative and add up to $1$.
- Statement II is true. Indeed, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}0.4 & 0.2 \\ 0.6 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}$
- Statement III is true. Indeed, $\mathbf{q}$ is a stochastic vector such that $P\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}$. So, it's a steady-state vector of $P$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I, II, and III."

<a id="finding-the-steady-state-vector-of-a-stochastic-matrix"></a>
## Finding the Steady-State Vector of a Stochastic Matrix

To find a steady-state vector of a stochastic matrix $P$, we seek a stochastic vector $\mathbf{q}$ such that

$$
P\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{q}
$$

Let's begin by writing this equation in a slightly different form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Pq &= q \\
Pq - q &= 0 \\
Pq - Iq &= 0 \\
(P - I)q &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the vector we seek is a solution to the matrix equation

$$
(P-I)\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}
$$

Let's use this idea to find a steady-state vector for the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}0.25 & 0.5 \\ 0.75 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$

First, we compute $(P-I)$:
$\begin{bmatrix}0.25 & 0.5 \\ 0.75 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$

Then, to solve

$$
(P-I)\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}
$$

we reduce the augmented matrix $M$ to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-0.75 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0.75 & -0.5 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-0.75 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has one pivot column (the $1$st one). Thus, $x_2$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1= \dfrac 23 x_2
$$

Hence, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{3}x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since the steady-state vector must be stochastic, its entries should add up to $1$. Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{3}x_{2} + x_{2} &= 1 \\
\frac{5}{3}x_{2} &= 1 \\
x_{2} &= \frac{3}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, our steady-state vector is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{5} \\ \frac{3}{5}\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="regular-stochastic-matrices"></a>
## Regular Stochastic Matrices

A stochastic matrix $P$ is **regular** if there exists a positive integer $n$ such that $P^n$ has all positive elements.

Let's consider some examples:

- The stochastic matrix $P$, given by
$\begin{bmatrix}0.25 & 0.5 \\ 0.75 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$
has all positive elements. Therefore, it is a regular stochastic matrix.
- The stochastic matrix $Q$, given by
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0.5 \\ 1 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$
contains a zero element. However, if we compute its square, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0.5 \\ 1 & 0.5\end{bmatrix}$
which has all positive elements. Therefore, $Q$ is a regular stochastic matrix.

Regular stochastic matrices have the following important property:

> *Any regular stochastic matrix has a **unique** steady-state vector $\mathbf q$. Moreover, the Markov chain
> $x_{1}|= Px_{0},; x_{2}|= Px_{1},; x_{3}|= Px_{2},; ⋮\begin{vmatrix}⋮ \\ x_{k + 1}|= Px_{k} \\ ⋮\end{vmatrix}⋮$
> converges to $\mathbf q$ for **any** initial state $\mathbf x_0$.*

<a id="finding-the-steady-state-vector-of-a-stochastic-2x2-matrix"></a>
## Finding the Steady-State Vector of a Stochastic 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** The table below describes how the customers of a particular electronics store feel about their next computer purchase.

|  |  | From: |  |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
|  |  | **Laptop** | **Desktop** |
| To: | **Laptop** | $0.9$ | $0.8$ |
| **Desktop** | $0.1$ | $0.2$ |  |

It is found that $10 \%$ of customers who currently use a laptop would choose a desktop on their next purchase, while $80 \%$ of customers who currently use a desktop would choose a laptop on their next purchase.

Given that the store has $5\,400$ customers, all of whom purchase a new computer every two years, how many customers would eventually own a desktop computer if the given probabilities remain constant over many two-year periods?

**Explanation**

From the table, we get the corresponding stochastic matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}0.9 & 0.8 \\ 0.1 & 0.2\end{bmatrix}$

Notice that $P$ contains only positive entries. So, the matrix $P$ is regular. As a result, after many two-year periods, the distribution will eventually converge to the unique steady-state vector of $P$, and this convergence does not depend on the initial state.

Recall that a steady-state vector of a stochastic matrix $P$ is a stochastic vector $\mathbf{q}$ such that

$$
Pq = q⟹(P - I)q = 0
$$

Computing $P-I$, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0.9 & 0.8 \\ 0.1 & 0.2\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, the matrix equation

$$
(P-I)\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}
$$

is equivalent to the system of linear equations with the augmented matrix $M$, which we reduce to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-0.1 & 0.8 & 0 \\ 0.1 & -0.8 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-0.1 & 0.8 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has one pivot column (the $1$st one). Thus, $x_2$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_{1} = 8x_{2}
$$

Hence, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}8x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$

Since the steady-state vector must be stochastic, its entries should add up to $1$. Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
8x_{2} + x_{2} &= 1 \\
9x_{2} &= 1 \\
x_{2} &= \frac{1}{9}
\end{aligned}
$$

Our steady-state vector is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{8}{9} \\ \frac{1}{9}\end{bmatrix}
$$

So,

$$
{\color{blue}{\dfrac{1}{9}}}
$$

of the $5\,400$ customers would eventually own a desktop computer. Therefore, the total number of people who would eventually own a desktop computer is

$$
\dfrac{1}{9} \cdot 5\,400 = 600
$$

<a id="finding-the-steady-state-vector-of-a-stochastic-3x3-matrix"></a>
## Finding the Steady-State Vector of a Stochastic 3x3 Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}0.1 & 0.3 & 0.5 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.7 & 0.4 & 0.4\end{bmatrix}$

Find the steady-state vector $\mathbf q$ of the stochastic matrix $P$ shown above.

**Explanation**

Recall that a steady-state vector of a stochastic matrix $P$ is a stochastic vector $\mathbf{q}$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
Pq &= q \\
Pq - q &= 0 \\
Pq - Iq &= 0 \\
(P - I)q &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Computing $P-I$, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0.1 & 0.3 & 0.5 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.7 & 0.4 & 0.4\end{bmatrix}
$$

The matrix equation

$$
(P-I)\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}
$$

is equivalent to the system of linear equations with the augmented matrix $M$, which we reduce to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-0.9 & 0.3 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0.2 & -0.7 & 0.1 & 0 \\ 0.7 & 0.4 & -0.6 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{1}:=20R_{1} \\ R_{2}:=10R_{2} \\ R_{3}:=20R_{3} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}-18 & 6 & 10 & 0 \\ 2 & -7 & 1 & 0 \\ 14 & 8 & -12 & 0\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}↔R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & -7 & 1 & 0 \\ -18 & 6 & 10 & 0 \\ 14 & 8 & -12 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + 9R_{1} \\ R_{3}:=R_{3} - 7R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & -7 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -57 & 19 & 0 \\ 0 & 57 & -19 & 0\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{3}:=R_{3} + R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & -7 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -57 & 19 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has two pivot columns (the $1$st and $2$nd). Thus, $x_3$ is a free variable. From the second equation, we obtain

$$
x_2=\dfrac{1}{3}x_3
$$

Substituting this into the first equation, we obtain

$$
2x_1 -7\left(\dfrac{1}{3}x_3\right) + x_3 = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow\qquad x_1 = \dfrac{2}{3}x_3
$$

Hence, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{3}x_{3} \\ \frac{1}{3}x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since the steady-state vector must be stochastic, its entries should add up to $1$. Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{3}x_{3} + \frac{1}{3}x_{3} + x_{3} &= 1 \\
2x_{3} &= 1 \\
x_{3} &= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, our steady-state vector is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{6} \\ \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$
