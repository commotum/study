## Introduction

Consider the following data set:

| $x$ | **$100$** | **$110$** | **$120$** | **$130$** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $12$ | $11$ | $12$ | $15$ |

Our goal is to describe how to use matrices to carry out a linear regression. That is, we want to find an equation of the form

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x
$$

that best fits the given data points.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Linear Regression With Matrices/Images/c0181831a6e790c04476f51670da497c.png>)

Let's write our problem in matrix notation. Substituting the values of $x$ into the regression equation and equating the results to the corresponding observed values of $y$, we obtain the system

$$
{β_{0} + 100β_{1} = 12; β_{0} + 110β_{1} = 11; β_{0} + 120β_{1} = 12; β_{0} + 130β_{1} = 15
$$

which can be expressed in matrix notation as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 100 \\ 1 & 110 \\ 1 & 120 \\ 1 & 130\end{bmatrix}
$$

The matrix $X$ is called the **design matrix,** and $\mathbf{y}$ is called the **observation vector**.

Since our points do not lie on a straight line, there will be no parameters $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ such that this equation is true. So instead, we seek parameters $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ that best approximate the solution.

In other words, we seek to find a column vector $\boldsymbol\beta$ such that

$$
\| \mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|
$$

is as small as possible.

Notice that this is exactly the least-squares problem for the system

$$
X \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{y}
$$

Therefore, the coefficients of the line that best fits the data can be found as the solution to the corresponding least-squares problem!

This approach to finding parameters for a linear regression model is often called **ordinary least squares (OLS) regression**.

Pre-multiplying the equation

$$
X \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{y}
$$

by $X^T$, we obtain the corresponding normal equation

$$
X^T \! X {\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Since the columns of $X$ are linearly independent, the matrix ${X^T}X$ is invertible, and

$$
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = (X^T \! X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Substituting $X$ and $\mathbf{y}$ into the formula above and simplifying, we get

$$
β^(^) = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0.1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the line of best fit (regression line) is

$$
y = 1+ 0.1x
$$

## Identifying Elements of a Regression Line in Terms of Matrices and Vectors

**Example:** The vector of coefficients of the line $y=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ that best fits the data above can be found using linear regression as

| $x$ | **$-6$** | **$-3$** | **$-1$** | **$0$** | **$4$** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $-7$ | $-4$ | $2$ | $8$ | $10$ |

$\begin{bmatrix}β_{0} \\ β_{1}\end{bmatrix}$

where $X$ is the corresponding design matrix and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector. Which are the design matrix $X$ and the observation vector $\mathbf{y}$ in this context?

**Explanation**

| $x$ | **$\color{blue}-6$** | **$\color{blue}-3$** | **$\color{blue}-1$** | **$\color{blue}0$** | **$\color{blue}4$** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $\color{red}-7$ | $\color{red}-4$ | $\color{red}2$ | $\color{red}8$ | $\color{red}10$ |

The coefficients of the line that best fits the data can be found as the least-squares solution ${\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$ to the system

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}1 & -6 \\ 1 & -3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 4\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(X)\begin{bmatrix}β_{0} \\ β_{1}\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(β) &= \begin{bmatrix}-7 \\ -4 \\ 2 \\ 8 \\ 10\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(y)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $X$ is the design matrix, $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is the parameter vector, and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector.

## Finding a Least-Squares Regression Line

**Example:** Find the line of the form $y=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ that best fits the data above.

| $x$ | **$-3$** | **$-1$** | **$0$** | **$4$** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $10$ | $8$ | $6$ | $3$ |

*Hint: You can use the fact that*
$\begin{bmatrix}26 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}$
*where $X$ is the corresponding design matrix and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector.*

**Explanation**

The coefficients of the line that best fits the data can be found as the least-squares solution ${\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$ to the system

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}1 & -3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 4\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(X)\begin{bmatrix}β_{0} \\ β_{1}\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(β) &= \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 8 \\ 6 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(y)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $X$ is the design matrix, $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is the parameter vector, and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector.

Pre-multiplying this equation by $X^T$, we obtain the corresponding normal equation

$$
X^T \! X {\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Since the columns of $X$ are linearly independent, the matrix ${X^T}X$ is invertible and

$$
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = (X^T \! X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Substituting the given values of $({X^T} X)^{-1}$ and $X^T \mathbf{y}$ into the formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
β^(^) &= (X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}y \\
&= \frac{1}{104}\begin{bmatrix}26 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}27 \\ -26\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}6.75 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence,

$$
y = 6.75 - x
$$

## Finding a Least-Squares Regression Line in Context

**Example:** An experiment consists of measuring how much a spring stretches (in inches) when one end of the spring is attached to the ceiling while a mass of $x$ ounces hangs from the other end. This data is shown below.

| Mass $(x)$ | **$1$** | **$2$** | **$4$** | **$5$** |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Stretch $(y)$ | $0.8$ | $1.8$ | $3.2$ | $4.2$ |

Find the line of the form $y=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ that best fits the data above. According to the model, how much will the spring stretch if we use an $8$-ounce mass, assuming that the trend continues?

*Hint: You can use the fact that*
$\begin{bmatrix}23 & -6 \\ -6 & 2\end{bmatrix}$
*where $X$ is the corresponding design matrix and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector.*

**Explanation**

The coefficients of the line that best fits the data can be found as the least-squares solution ${\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$ to the system

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(X)\begin{bmatrix}β_{0} \\ β_{1}\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(β) &= \begin{bmatrix}0.8 \\ 1.8 \\ 3.2 \\ 4.2\end{bmatrix}^(⏞)^(y)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $X$ is the design matrix, $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is the parameter vector, and $\mathbf{y}$ is the observation vector.

Pre-multiplying this equation by $X^T$, we obtain the corresponding normal equation

$$
X^T \! X {\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Since the columns of $X$ are linearly independent, the matrix ${X^T}X$ is invertible and

$$
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}} = (X^T \! X)^{-1} X^T \mathbf{y}
$$

Substituting the given values of $({X^T} X)^{-1}$ and $X^T \mathbf{y}$ into the formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
β^(^) &= (X^{T}X)^{-1}X^{T}y \\
&= \frac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix}23 & -6 \\ -6 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 38.2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0.04 \\ 0.82\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have the line

$$
y = 0.04+ 0.82x
$$

Finally, to predict how much the spring will stretch if an $8$-ounce mass is hung from it, we substitute $x=8$ into the equation of the line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= 0.04 + 0.82(8) \\
&= 6.6
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, according to the model, if an $8$ ounce mass is hung on the spring, the spring will stretch $6.6$ inches.

**Note**: Since we're extrapolating ($x=8$ is not within the range of the original set of $x$-values), our result *may* be unreliable.

## Least-Squares vs. Minimizing the Sum of the Squared Residuals

Given a dataset of paired numerical observations $(x,y)$ and the corresponding linear regression model

$$
\widehat{y} =f(x)
$$

the **residual** for a particular observation $(x, y)$ is defined as

$$
\begin{aligned}
Residual &= Actual - Estimated \\
&= y - f(x) \\
&= y - y^(^)
\end{aligned}
$$

We subtract the *estimated* value predicted by the model from the actual *observed* value. The residuals for the data set we considered earlier are shown below.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Linear Regression With Matrices/Images/145cd294ca6ed57aab703f6fe2f0587e.png>)

In the past, you may have seen that calculating a regression line typically means finding a line where the sum of the squared residuals

$$
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^4 r_i^2
$$

is as small as possible.

Using matrix notation, the sum of the squared residuals can be viewed as

$$
\sum_{i=1}^4 r_i^2 = \| \mathbf{y} - X\beta\|^2
$$

Therefore, the condition that the sum of the squared residuals is minimized is equivalent to minimizing $\| \mathbf{y} - X\beta\|^2$. This, in turn, is equivalent to minimizing $\| \mathbf{y} - X\beta\|$.

Therefore, the problem of minimizing the squared residuals is equivalent to solving the least squares problem for

$$
X \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{y}
$$
