## Introduction

In this lesson, we discuss some properties of line integrals of scalar functions $f(x,y)$ with respect to arc length. Recall that we denote these types of integrals as
$\displaystyle\int\limits_C f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s$,
where $C$ is some path in the $xy$-plane. The first property we'll consider is the line integral of **unity**, i.e.,

$$
f(x,y) = 1
$$

Firstly, by setting

$$
f(x,y) = 1
$$

we can write our line integral as

$$
\displaystyle\int\limits_C \, \textrm{d}s
$$

Now suppose that $C$ is the path along the line segment from $(0,0)$ to $(4,3)$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/486696dd9bb127f0e5ef0c28ecb1e301.png>)

As it turns out, the line integral with respect to arc length of unity along a path $C$ equals the arc length of $C$.

In this case, the arc length of $C$ is given by the length of the hypotenuse of a right triangle. Therefore, by the Pythagorean theorem, we have

$$
\displaystyle\int\limits_C \, \textrm{d}s = \sqrt{4^2+3^2} = 5
$$

---

<a id="calculating-line-integrals-of-unity"></a>
## Calculating Line Integrals of Unity

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle\int_C \,\textrm d s$, where $C$ is the arc of a semicircle of radius $4$ traversed once in the counterclockwise direction, as shown above.

![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/238460fd6d89a120c21c88a681a7f2e3.png>)

**Explanation**

The line integral of unity with respect to arc length over $C$ is equal to the arc length $L$ of $C$, i.e.,

$$
\int_C \,\textrm d s = L
$$

In this case, $C$ is a semicircle of radius $r = 4$. Its arc length is given by

$$
L = πr = 4π
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int_C \,\textrm d s = 4\pi
$$

## Calculating Line Integrals Over Piecewise-Smooth Curves

Let's once again consider the line integral with respect to arc length of $f(x,y)$, given by
$\displaystyle\int\limits_C f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s$
where

$$
C = C_1\cup C_2
$$

is the **piecewise-smooth** path shown below.

![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/413ce51324bc482acc8cdcfd544bc8ab.png>)

We can show that the line integral along the entire path $C$ equals the sum of the line integrals across the paths $C_1$ and $C_2$:

$$
\int_{C}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s = \int_{C_1}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s + \int_{C_2}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s
$$

So, if

$$
f(x,y)=1
$$

then we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{C}ds &= ∫_{C_{1}}ds + ∫_{C_{2}}ds \\
&= 3 + 2 \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

This result naturally extends to line integrals across piecewise-smooth paths comprised of three or more parts. Let's see an example.

---

<a id="calculating-line-integrals-over-piecewise-smooth-curves"></a>
## Calculating Line Integrals Over Piecewise-Smooth Curves

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle\int_C \,\textrm d s$, where $C$ is the path shown above, traversed once counterclockwise.

![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/bb0d714825f714ebc1e0612dd9fc507e.png>)

**Explanation**

The path $C$ is made up of three parts, as shown below:

![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/2dab4e2a2bea7b9217543ff7144f9dce.png>)

Now, we make use of the following facts:

- If $C = C_1 \cup C_2 \cup C_3$ is a piecewise-smooth curve, we have
$\int_{C}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s = \int_{C_1}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s + \int_{C_2}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s + \int_{C_3}f(x,y)\,\textrm d s$.
- The line integral of unity with respect to arc length over $C$ is equal to the arc length $L$ of $C$, i.e.,
$\displaystyle\int_C \,\textrm d s = L$.

The path $C_1$ is a semicircle of radius $r = 3$. Therefore, we have

$$
\int_{C_1} \,\textrm ds = \dfrac{1}{2}(2 \pi r) = 3\pi
$$

The paths $C_2$ and $C_3$ are hypotenuses of right triangles whose legs have lengths $3$ and $4$. Therefore, we have

$$
\int_{C_2} \,\textrm ds = \int_{C_3} \,\textrm ds = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int_C \,\textrm d s = 3\pi + 5 + 5 = 3\pi + 10
$$

## Independence of Parametrization and Orientation

Let's once again consider the line integral with respect to arc length of $f(x,y)$:
$\displaystyle\int\limits_C f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s$

There are two more important properties of this type of line integral that we need to be aware of.

- There is usually more than one way to parametrize a curve. However, line integrals with respect to arc length are *independent of the choice of parametrization*. In other words, we get the same result regardless of which parametrization we choose.For example, the paths $C_1$ and $C_2$, given by
$\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}:r_{1}(t)|= ⟨\cos t, \sin t⟩, & 0 \le t < 2π \\ C_{2}:r_{2}(u)|= ⟨\cos 2u, \sin 2u⟩, & 0 \le u < π\end{bmatrix}$
both traverse a circle of radius $1$ once in the counterclockwise direction starting from $(1,0)$. However, since line integrals with respect to arc length are independent of parametrization, we have
$\displaystyle\int\limits_{C_1} f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s = \displaystyle\int\limits_{C_2} f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s$

- Line integrals with respect to arc length are also *independent of the orientation* of $C$. For example, suppose that $C$ is a path along a particular curve traversed from the point $A$ to the point $B$. Then, $-C$ is a path along the same curve but traversed from $B$ to $A$, as shown below.![](<../Source/4.1.3. Properties of Line Integrals of Scalar Functions/Images/d2d145b9efc240efe5e5aca718a52bbf.png>)
Independence of orientation means that
$\displaystyle\int\limits_C f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s = \displaystyle\int\limits_{-C} f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s$.
- Finally, it's important to mention that all of the properties discussed in this lesson apply to line integrals with respect to arc length over space curves, i.e., line integrals of the form $\displaystyle\int_{C} f(x,y,z)\,\textrm d s$.

---

<a id="applying-independence-of-parametrization-and-orientation"></a>
## Applying Independence of Parametrization and Orientation

**Example:** Consider the curves $C_1$ and $C_2$, defined parametrically as follows:

$\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}:r_{1}(t)|= ⟨\frac{1}{9}t^{3}, t⟩, & 0 \le t \le 3 \\ C_{2}:r_{2}(u)|= ⟨3u^{3}, 3u⟩, & 0 \le u \le 1\end{bmatrix}$

Now suppose $\displaystyle\int_{C_1} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = k$ for some function $f(x,y)$ and real number $k$. Which of the following must be true?

1. $\displaystyle\int_{C_2} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = 3k$
2. $\displaystyle\int_{C_2} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = k$
3. $\displaystyle\int_{-C_1} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = k$

**Explanation**

Line integrals with respect to arc length are independent of the choice of parametrization and orientation.

With that in mind, let's examine each statement.

- Statement I is false, while statement II is true. Notice that $C_1$ and $C_2$ are different parameterizations of the same curve. Since line integrals with respect to arc length are independent of the choice of parameterization, we have
$\int_{C_2} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = k$.
To see that $C_1$ and $C_2$ represent the same curve, we can convert each parametrization to Cartesian form. In doing so, we find that both parameterizations give the Cartesian curve
$x = \dfrac{1}{9}y^3, \quad 0\leq y\leq 3$.
- Statement III is true. Reversing the curve's orientation does *not* change the value of a line integral with respect to arc length. Therefore, we have
$\int_{-C_1} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = \int_{C_1} f(x,y)\,\textrm ds = k$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."
