## Introduction

Since the integration of vector functions is done component-by-component, the properties of integrals of vector functions are immediately inherited from those of the integrals of real-valued functions.

Suppose that $\mathbf f(t)$ and $\mathbf g(t)$ are vector-valued functions that are continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$, and $\alpha$ is a scalar.
Then, the *vector addition and scalar multiplication rules* are as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∫_{a}^{b}(f(t) + g(t))dt = ∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt + ∫_{a}^{b}g(t)dt & vector addition rule \\ ∫_{a}^{b}αf(t)dt = α∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt & scalar constant multiple rule\end{bmatrix}
$$

For example, if

$$
\mathbf f(t) = \left\langle t, \: \cos t\right\rangle
$$

and

$$
\mathbf g(t) = \left\langle -t^2, \: \sin t \right\rangle
$$

then we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫[f(t) + 2g(t)]dt &= ∫f(t)dt + 2∫g(t)dt \\
&= ∫⟨t, \cos t⟩dt + 2∫⟨ - t^{2}, \sin t⟩dt \\
&= ⟨∫tdt, ∫\cos tdt⟩ + ⟨2∫ - t^{2}dt, 2∫\sin tdt⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{t^{2}}{2} + C_{1}, \sin t + C_{2}⟩ + ⟨ - \frac{2t^{3}}{3} + C_{3},-2\cos t + C_{4}⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{3t^{2} - 4t^{3}}{6}, \sin t - 2\cos t⟩ + C
\end{aligned}
$$

where $C_1, \, C_2, \, C_3, \, C_4$ are arbitrary real constants and

$$
{\mathbf C} = \langle C_1+C_3, C_2+C_4 \rangle
$$

is a constant vector.

<a id="applying-the-vector-addition-and-scalar-multiplication-rules"></a>
## Applying the Vector Addition and Scalar Multiplication Rules

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle{\int_0^2} [3 \mathbf f(t)+\mathbf g(t)] \,\textrm{d}t$ given that $\displaystyle{\int_0^2} \mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t=\left\langle 16, \: 2,\: -2\right\rangle$ and $\displaystyle{\int_0^2}\mathbf g(t)\,\textrm{d}t=\left\langle 2,-4,2\right\rangle$.

**Explanation**

From the vector addition and scalar multiplication rules, we have

$$
\displaystyle {\int_0^2} \big[ \, 3 \mathbf f(t)+\mathbf g(t) \, \big] \, \textrm d t = \displaystyle {3\int_0^2} \mathbf f(t) \, \textrm d t + \displaystyle {\int_0^2} \mathbf g(t) \, \textrm d t
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}[3f(t) + g(t)]dt &= 3∫_{0}^{2}f(t)dt + ∫_{0}^{2}g(t)dt \\
&= 3⟨16, 2,-2⟩ + ⟨2,-4, 2⟩ \\
&= ⟨48, 6,-6⟩ + ⟨2,-4, 2⟩ \\
&= ⟨50, 2,-4⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

## The Rule for Reversal of Integration Limits

Given a vector-valued function $\mathbf f(t)$ that is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$, the *rule for reversal of integration limits* states the following:

$$
∫_{b}^{a}f(t)dt = - ∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt
$$

---

<a id="reversing-the-limits-of-integration"></a>
## Reversing the Limits of Integration

**Example:** Calculate
$\displaystyle\int_0^2\mathbf f(t) + \langle 1,\:-1,\:1\rangle\,\textrm{d}t$
given that
$\displaystyle\int_2^0\mathbf f(t) \,\textrm{d}t= \left\langle -3, \: 2, \: 1 \right\rangle$.

**Explanation**

Let's reverse the limits of integration on the known result:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}f(t)dt &= - ∫_{2}^{0}f(t)dt \\
&= - ⟨ - 3, 2, 1⟩ \\
&= ⟨3,-2,-1⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, applying the vector addition rule, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}f(t) + ⟨1,-1, 1⟩dt &= ∫_{0}^{2}f(t)dt + ∫_{0}^{2}⟨1,-1, 1⟩dt \\
&= ⟨3,-2,-1⟩ + ⟨t,-t, t⟩ \mid _{0}^{2} \\
&= ⟨3,-2,-1⟩ + ⟨2,-2, 2⟩ \\
&= ⟨5,-4, 1⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-adjacent-intervals-rule"></a>
## The Adjacent Intervals Rule

Given a vector-valued function $\mathbf f(t)$ that is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$ and $p \in (a,b)$, the *adjacent intervals rule* states the following:

$$
∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt = ∫_{a}^{p}f(t)dt + ∫_{p}^{b}f(t)dt
$$

---

<a id="applying-the-adjacent-intervals-rule"></a>
## Applying the Adjacent Intervals Rule

**Example:** Calculate
$\displaystyle\int_{-1}^0\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t$
given that
$\displaystyle\int_0^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t= \left\langle \dfrac{3}{2}, \: 1, \: 3 \right\rangle$
and
$\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t=\left\langle 0, \: 2, \: 6 \right\rangle$.

**Explanation**

Using the adjacent intervals, we get

$$
\int_{-1}^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t=\int_{-1}^0\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t+\int_0^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t
$$

We can rearrange the above as follows:

$$
\int_{-1}^0\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t = \int_{-1}^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t - \int_0^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm{d}t
$$

Finally, using the given information, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{-1}^{0}f(t)dt &= ∫_{-1}^{1}f(t)dt - ∫_{0}^{1}f(t)dt \\
&= ⟨0, 2, 6⟩ - ⟨\frac{3}{2}, 1, 3⟩ \\
&= ⟨ - \frac{3}{2}, 1, 3⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-product-rules"></a>
## The Product Rules

Suppose that $\mathbf f(t)$ is a vector-valued function that is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$ and $\mathbf c$ is a constant vector.
Then, the *products rules* are as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∫_{a}^{b}[c \cdot f(t)]dt = c \cdot (∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt) & dot product rule \\ ∫_{a}^{b}[c \cdot f(t)]dt = c \cdot ∫_{a}^{b}f(t)dt & cross product rule\end{bmatrix}
$$

---

<a id="applying-the-product-rules"></a>
## Applying the Product Rules

**Example:** Find $\displaystyle {\int_0^1} \mathbf c \times \mathbf f(t) \, \textrm d t$
given that
$\displaystyle\int_0^1\mathbf f(t)\,\textrm d t = \mathbf i-3\,\mathbf j-4\,\mathbf k$
and
$\mathbf c=\mathbf j-\mathbf k$.

**Explanation**

The cross-product rule states that

$$
{\int_0^1} \mathbf c \times \mathbf f(t) \, \textrm d t = \mathbf c \times {\int_0^1} \mathbf f(t) \, \textrm d t
$$

Applying the cross-product rule, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{1}c \cdot f(t)dt &= c \cdot ∫_{0}^{1}f(t)dt \\
&= (j - k) \cdot (i - 3j - 4k) \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}i & j & k \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -3 & -4\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}1 & -1 \\ -3 & -4\end{vmatrix}i -\begin{vmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & -4\end{vmatrix}j +\begin{vmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & -3\end{vmatrix}k \\
&= (-4 - 3)i - (0 + 1)j + (0 - 1)k \\
&=-7i - j - k
\end{aligned}
$$
