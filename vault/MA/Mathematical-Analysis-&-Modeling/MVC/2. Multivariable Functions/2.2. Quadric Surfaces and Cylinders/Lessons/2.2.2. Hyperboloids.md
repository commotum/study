## Introduction

A **hyperboloid of one sheet** centered at $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ is a quadric surface whose equation is given by

$$
\dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{(z-z_0)^2}{c^2} =1
$$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants.

For example, the graph of the hyperboloid of one sheet

$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=1
$$

is shown below. It has its center at the origin, and the $z$-axis is the **axis of symmetry** of the hyperboloid.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/307c4da6a4ecae081d14f1e22c9cad72.png>)

The intercepts of this hyperboloid with the coordinate axes are as follows:

- the $x$-intercepts are $(\pm1,0,0)$
- the $y$-intercepts are $(0,\pm1,0)$
- there are no $z$-intercepts

<a id="hyperboloids-of-two-sheets"></a>
## Hyperboloids of Two Sheets

A **hyperboloid of two sheets** centered at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is given by

$$
\dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{(z-z_0)^2}{c^2} =-1
$$

**Watch out!** This equation is similar to the general equation of a hyperboloid of one sheet. The only difference is the right-hand side is now $-1$ instead of $+1$.

For example, the graph of the hyperboloid of two sheets

$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=-1
$$

is represented below. It has its center at the origin, and the $z$-axis is the **axis of symmetry** of the hyperboloid.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/8e403276d0ddc1ece75aa650f3c6fb38.png>)

The intercepts of this hyperboloid with the $z$-axis are $(0,0,\pm1)$. The surface does not intersect the $x$- or $y$-axes.

Calculating the intercepts of a hyperboloid with the coordinate axes is done in the same way as we've seen before with spheres and ellipsoids. Let's see some examples.

There is a simple way to distinguish between hyperboloids of one and two sheets by simply looking at the equations:

- *one* minus sign indicates hyperboloids of *one* sheet:
$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2} \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}-} \dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1$
- *two* minus signs indicate hyperboloids of *two* sheets:
$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2} \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}-} \dfrac{z^2}{c^2}= \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}-}1$

<a id="identifying-the-intercepts-of-a-hyperboloid"></a>
## Identifying the Intercepts of a Hyperboloid

**Example:** Given the hyperboloid of one sheet $\dfrac{(x-3)^2}9 + \dfrac{(y+4)^2}{16} - \dfrac{(z-5)^2}{25} = 1$, find its intercepts with the $x$-axis.

**Explanation**

To find the $x$-intercepts, we plug $y=0$ and $z=0$ into the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
((x - 3)^{2})/(9) + ((0 + 4)^{2})/(16) - ((0 - 5)^{2})/(25) &= 1 \\
((x - 3)^{2})/(9) + 1 - 1 &= 1 \\
((x - 3)^{2})/(9) &= 1 \\
(x - 3)^{2} &= 9 \\
x - 3 &= ± \sqrt{9} \\
x &= 3 ± 3 \\
x &= 0, 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the intercepts with the $x$-axis are $(0,0,0)$ and $(6,0,0)$.

<a id="the-domain-of-a-hyperboloid"></a>
## The Domain of a Hyperboloid

Let's consider the graphs a typical one-sheet and two-sheet hyperboloids centered at $O$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/dfbad99e0f86ba4e9b99badaa928ef9e.png>)

Notice that a hyperboloid of two sheets is defined for any $(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. In fact, for any point $(x,y)$ in the $xy$-plane, there are exactly two points on the hyperboloid.

On the other hand, the graph of a hyperboloid of one sheet does not contain points over the central elliptical region (close to the axis of symmetry).

For example, given the hyperboloid of one sheet

$$
x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1
$$

let's find the domain of $z = z(x,y)$.

From the hyperboloid equation, we make $z$ the subject as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + y^{2} - z^{2} &= 1 \\
x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 &= z^{2} \\
z^{2} &= x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 \\
z &= ± \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2} - 1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the square root is defined only for non-negative arguments, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x^{2} + y^{2} - 1 & \ge 0 \\ x^{2} + y^{2} & \ge 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

which gives the *exterior* and boundary of the circle

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 1
$$

Therefore, the domain of $z(x,y)$ for the hyperboloid

$$
x^2+y^2-z^2 = 1
$$

is

$$
\left \{(x,y) \:: \: x^2 + y^2 \geq 1 \right\}
$$

<a id="identifying-the-domain-of-a-hyperboloid"></a>
## Identifying the Domain of a Hyperboloid

**Example:** Given the hyperboloid of two sheets $(x-1)^2 + y^2 - \dfrac{z^2}{4} = -1$, find the domain of $z = z(x,y)$.

**Explanation**

From the hyperboloid equation, we make $z$ the subject as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} - \frac{z^{2}}{4} &= -1 \\
(x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} + 1 &= \frac{z^{2}}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{z^{2}}{4} &= (x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} + 1 \\
z^{2} &= 4((x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} + 1) \\
z &= ± 2\sqrt{(x - 1)^{2} + y^{2} + 1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The square root is defined only for non-negative arguments. However, notice that for all $(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2$, we have

$$
(x-1)^2 + y^2 + 1 \geq 0
$$

Therefore, the domain of $z(x,y)$ is $\mathbb R^2$.

<a id="alternative-orientations-of-the-axis-of-symmetry"></a>
## Alternative Orientations of the Axis of Symmetry

So far, we've encountered hyperboloids whose axes of symmetry are parallel to the $z$-axis.
Let's now consider the cases when a hyperboloid is oriented along the $x$- or the $y$-axes.

For a hyperboloid of one sheet centered at the origin, the other two cases are as follows:

- When the $y$ term is negative, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis.
$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} =1$![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/a0f0d5eb32e97dc0a52d7314ccf2f32e.png>)

- When the $x$ term is negative, the axis of symmetry is the $x$-axis.
$-\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} =1$![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/18964579536e1a5f74baccadde311c61.png>)

Likewise, for a hyperboloid of two sheets centered at the origin, the other two cases are as follows:

- When the $y$ term is negative, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis.
$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} =-1$![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/993df2b0f63fa3ca17c897e6f5451c54.png>)

- When the $x$ term is negative, the axis of symmetry is the $x$-axis.
$-\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} =-1$![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/d5d23da80aa23e048aba7f1907bcd545.png>)

<a id="traces-of-hyperboloids-of-one-sheet"></a>
## Traces of Hyperboloids of One Sheet

The traces of a hyperboloid of one sheet are either ellipses or hyperbolas.

For example, let's find the traces with the coordinate planes for the hyperboloid of one sheet given by

$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=1
$$

- The trace of this surface in the $xy$-plane (when $z=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} + y^{2} - 0^{2}|= 1; x^{2} + y^{2}|= 1$,
which is a circle centered at the origin of radius $1$ in the $xy$-plane.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/9e062d03e0d2d0d63e5779dac93fac9b.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $xz$-plane (when $y=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} + 0^{2} - z^{2}|= 1; x^{2} - z^{2}|= 1$,
which is a hyperbola in the $xz$-plane centered at the origin.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/c1a499bb69b1679c66ffe614cf4a82d0.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $yz$-plane (when $x=0$) is given by the equation
$0^{2} + y^{2} - z^{2}|= 1; y^{2} - z^{2}|= 1$,
which is a hyperbola in the $yz$-plane centered at the origin.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/13fad789833181eeca69c9563cb055f1.png>)

---

<a id="traces-of-hyperboloids-of-two-sheets"></a>
## Traces of Hyperboloids of Two Sheets

The traces of a hyperboloid of two sheets are either ellipses, hyperbolas, a single point, or the empty set.

For example, let's find the traces with the coordinate planes for the hyperboloid of two sheets given by

$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=-1
$$

- The trace of this surface in the $xy$-plane (when $z=0$) is the empty set since the surface does not intersect the $xy$-plane.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/ee0d6626d8c372dcdc2b917c42487b9e.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $xz$-plane (when $y=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} + 0^{2} - z^{2}|=-1; x^{2} - z^{2}|=-1$,
which is a hyperbola in the $xz$-plane centered at the origin.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/ab1c50b9124b16c09d10c23e322d0f93.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $yz$-plane (when $x=0$) is given by the equation
$0^{2} + y^{2} - z^{2}|=-1; y^{2} - z^{2}|=-1$,
which is a hyperbola in the $yz$-plane centered at the origin.

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/831395dfc764996d191d1c453a2ae302.png>)

There are a few other cases we should note:

- The trace of our surface with the plane $z=1$ is a single point (the vertex of the upper sheet):
$x^2+y^2 - 1^2 = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x,y,z) = (0,0,1)$
Likewise, the trace of our surface with the plane $z=-1$ is another point (the vertex of the lower sheet):
$x^2+y^2 - (-1)^2 = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x,y,z) = (0,0,-1)$
- The trace of our surface with the horizontal plane $z=k$ for $\mid k \mid > 1$ is a circle:
$x^2+y^2 - k^2 = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow x^2+y^2 = k^2-1$

![](<../Source/2.2.2. Hyperboloids/Images/fde9298e42993b35edc84f44cc957c03.png>)

<a id="finding-traces-of-hyperboloids"></a>
## Finding Traces of Hyperboloids

**Example:** The projection of the intersection between the hyperboloid of two sheets
$-\dfrac{(x-5)^2}{8} + \dfrac{y^2}{50} + (z-3)^2 = -1$
and the plane $z = 4$ onto the $xy$-plane is a hyperbola. What are the lengths of the semi-axes of the hyperbola?

**Explanation**

To find the intersection of the hyperboloid and the plane $z = 4$, we substitute $z = 4$ into the equation of the hyperboloid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
- ((x - 5)^{2})/(8) + \frac{y^{2}}{50} + (z - 3)^{2} &= -1 \\
&= - ((x - 5)^{2})/(8) + \frac{y^{2}}{50} + 1 =-1 \\
- ((x - 5)^{2})/(8) + \frac{y^{2}}{50} &= -2 \\
((x - 5)^{2})/(8) - \frac{y^{2}}{50} &= 2 \\
((x - 5)^{2})/(16) - \frac{y^{2}}{100} &= 1 \\
((x - 5)^{2})/(4^{2}) - \frac{y^{2}}{10^{2}} &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us the equation of a hyperbola with semi-axes of lengths $4$ and $10$.
