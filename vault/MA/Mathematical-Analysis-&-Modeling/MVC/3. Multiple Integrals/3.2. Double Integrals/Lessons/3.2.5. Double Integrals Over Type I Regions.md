## Introduction

How do we represent the double integral of a function over a non-rectangular region as a repeated integral?

Specifically, let's consider

$$
\displaystyle \iint\limits_D (x-2y) \, \textrm d A, %= \int_a^b \int_c^d (x-2y) \,\textrm d y\, \textrm d x
$$

where $D$ is the finite region enclosed by the lines $x=1$, $x=3$, $y = x$ and

$$
y = \dfrac12(x+4)
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/374f92e0a3a383a857c2418cbbf4644b.png>)

First, notice that $D$ is a *type I* region.
To determine the lower and upper functions, we draw a vertical arrow through the region.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/0d715ac2949014283474115237366cc9.png>)

So, the region $D$ can be defined as

$$
D = \left\{(x,y) \:: \: {\color{red}1} \leq x \leq {\color{blue}3}, \quad {\color{red}x} \leq y \leq {\color{blue}\dfrac12(x+4)} \right\}
$$

Now, we can express the integral as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)(x - 2y)dA &= ∫_((left limit))^((right limit))[∫_((lower function))^((upper function))(x - 2y)dy]dx \\
&= ∫_{1}^{3}[∫_{x}^{(x + 4)/2}(x - 2y)dy]dx
\end{aligned}
$$

And that's our answer! Notice that, when we have a type I region,

- the limits for $y$ are functions of $x$, and
- the limits for $x$ are constants.

## Representing a Double Integral as a Repeated Integral When the Limits are Given

**Example:** Consider the finite region $D$ in the $xy$-plane enclosed by the lines $x=0$, $y=0$, and the curve $y=\sqrt{4 - x^2}$. What is $\displaystyle \iint\limits_D (x+y)\,\mathrm{d}A$ expressed as a repeated integral?

**Explanation**

First, let's represent $D$ as a type I region. To determine the lower and upper functions, we draw a vertical arrow through the region, as usual.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/14f102586d04e8d0746c319f2ae457a6.png>)

As a result, we get

$$
D = \big\{(x,y) \:: \: 0 \leq x \leq 2, \quad 0 \leq y \leq \sqrt{4-x^2} \big\}
$$

Therefore, we can express the integral as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)(x + y)dA &= ∫_{0}^{2}∫_{0}^{\sqrt{4 - x^{2}}}(x + y)dydx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Representing a Double Integral as a Repeated Integral When the Limits are Not Given

**Example:** If $D$ is the finite region in the first quadrant enclosed by the curves $y=3x$ and $y=x^3$, then what is $\displaystyle \iint\limits_{D} f\left({x,y} \right) \, \mathrm{d}A$ expressed as a repeated integral?

**Explanation**

First, let's sketch our region.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/4436a12852e62e082097841fa7fa1561.png>)

Now, let's represent $D$ as a type I region.

- To determine the $x$-limits, we need to find the $x$-coordinates of the points at which the curves intersect. So, we set the following equation and solve it for $x$:
$x^{3}|= 3x; x^{3} - 3x|= 0; x(x^{2} - 3)|= 0$
So, $x$-limits are $0 \le x \le \sqrt{3}$. Note that we disregarded the solution $x=-\sqrt 3$ since $x\geq 0$ in our region.
- To determine the lower and upper functions, we draw a vertical arrow through the region, as usual.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/f0558707f61014c153cc8376fd9a9f38.png>)

As a result, we get

$$
D = \left\{(x,y) \:: \: 0 \leq x \leq \sqrt{3}, \quad x^3 \leq y \leq 3x \right\}
$$

Therefore, we can express the integral as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)f(x, y)dA &= ∫_{0}^{\sqrt{3}}∫_{x^{3}}^{3x}f(x, y)dydx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{\sqrt{3}}[∫_{x^{3}}^{3x}f(x, y)dy]dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating Repeated Integrals With Variable Limits

Suppose that we want to evaluate the repeated integral

$$
\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x}^{2x} (x-2y) \ \mathrm{d} y \ \mathrm{d} x
$$

Notice that the domain of integration is a type I region.

To compute this integral, we must bear the following important points in mind.

- We start with the inner integral, as usual.
- When integrating with respect to $y$, we treat $x$ as though it were a constant.
- The limits of integration for the inner integral are functions of $x$. Once again, we treat $x$ as though it were a constant and substitute the integration limits as usual. After this step, there should be no more $y$'s in our integral.
- Finally, we compute the outer integral.

Let's apply these steps to solve the given problem.

First, we evaluate the inner integral by integrating with respect to $y$, treating $x$ as a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{1}[∫_{x}^{2x}(x - 2y)dy]dx &= ∫_{0}^{1}[xy - y^{2}]_{x}^{2x}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[x \cdot (2x) - (2x)^{2}] - [x \cdot (x) - (x)^{2}]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[2x^{2} - 4x^{2}] - [x^{2} - x^{2}]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1} - 2x^{2}dx \\
&=-2∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we integrate with respect to $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-2∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}dx &= -2[\frac{x^{3}}{3}]_{0}^{1} \\
&=-\frac{2}{3}[x^{3}]_{0}^{1} \\
&=-\frac{2}{3}[1^{3} - 0^{3}] \\
&=-\frac{2}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating a Repeated Integral Defined Over a Type I Region

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int_0^1 \int_0^x x^2y \,\textrm{d}y \,\textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

The integral is defined over a type I region.

First, we evaluate the inner integral by integrating with respect to $y$, treating $x$ as a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{x}x^{2}ydydx &= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}[∫_{0}^{x}ydy]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}[\frac{y^{2}}{2}]_{0}^{x}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}(\frac{x^{2}}{2} - 0)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{1}x^{4}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we integrate with respect to $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{1}x^{4}dx &= \frac{1}{2}[\frac{x^{5}}{5}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{5} - 0) \\
&= \frac{1}{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating a Double Integral Defined Over a Type I Region

**Example:** Evaluate the double integral $\displaystyle \iint \limits_{D} xy\,\mathrm{d}A,\:$
where $D$ is the finite region below the $x$-axis enclosed by the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$.

**Explanation**

Since

$$
y \leq 0
$$

we obtain

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 4 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = -\sqrt{4-x^2}
$$

Let's sketch our region.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/3a70282170b649d4c76265cefd90012b.png>)

Notice that $D$ is a type I region.

- We already know the intersection of the circle with the line $y=0$.
So, the $x$-limits are
$-2 \le x \le 2$.
- To determine the lower and upper functions, we draw a vertical arrow through the region.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Double Integrals Over Type I Regions/Images/5833d31c6d9bf6891307c40c65594659.png>)

As a result, the integral is defined over the following type I region:

$$
D = \Big\{(x,y) \:: \: -2 \leq x \leq 2, \quad -\sqrt{4-x^2} \leq y \leq 0 \Big\}
$$

So, we can express the integral as follows:

$$
\iint \limits_{D} xy\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{-2}^2 \int_{-\sqrt{4-x^2}}^{0} xy\,\mathrm{d}y \: \mathrm{d}x
$$

First, we evaluate the inner integral by integrating with respect to $y$, treating $x$ as a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{-2}^{2}∫_{-\sqrt{4 - x^{2}}}^{0}xydydx &= ∫_{-2}^{2}x[∫_{-\sqrt{4 - x^{2}}}^{0}ydy]dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}x[\frac{y^{2}}{2}]_{-\sqrt{4 - x^{2}}}^{0}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{-2}^{2}x(0 - (-\sqrt{4 - x^{2}})^{2})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{-2}^{2}x(- (4 - x^{2}))dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{-2}^{2}x(x^{2} - 4)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{-2}^{2}(x^{3} - 4x)dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we integrate with respect to $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2}∫_{-2}^{2}(x^{3} - 4x)dx &= \frac{1}{2}[\frac{x^{4}}{4} - 2x^{2}]_{-2}^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}((4 - 8) - (4 - 8)) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 0 \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$
