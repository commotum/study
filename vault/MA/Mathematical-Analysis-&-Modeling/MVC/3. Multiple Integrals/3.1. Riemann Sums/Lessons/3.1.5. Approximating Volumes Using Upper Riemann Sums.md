## Introduction

In this lesson, we will describe a process for calculating an **upper bound** for the volume enclosed between a surface $z=f(x,y)$ and the $xy$-plane.

As an example, consider the rectangle $R$ in the $xy$-plane, defined as

$$
R = {(x, y): 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1}
$$

A sketch of the region $R$ is shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/c0a74d25d25e61e93c2a97518e1a919c.png>)

Now consider the function

$$
f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

defined over $R$, shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/4af6f1be61894fefa8779f60c2dd9e7e.png>)

The space underneath the surface and above the region $R$ defines a fixed volume $V$. Our goal is to find an upper bound for $V$. We do this by approximating this volume using rectangular solids.

We start by partitioning the $x$- and $y$-intervals of $R$. Let's pick two subintervals of equal length for both $x$ and $y$.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/07f91e726da8c13345b2920917d134d3.png>)

The region $R$ is now partitioned into $4$ rectangles:

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/7b49c1cb78941be2932124ed979fdf36.png>)

We seek an *upper* bound for the volume $V$. So, we let $M_{ij}$ be the *largest* value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We then define a rectangular solid in each subregion whose base is $R_{ij}$ and whose height is $M_{ij}$.

For example, the rectangular solid corresponding to $R_{11}$ is given below.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/a49a62e1d9f09a8b670c60ab24654377.png>)

The volume of this rectangular solid is

$$
M_{11}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{11}) = \dfrac14M_{11}
$$

Similarly, the volumes of the rectangular solids formed by the partitions $R_{21}, R_{12}$, and $R_{22}$ respectively are

$$
\dfrac14M_{21}, \qquad \dfrac14M_{12}, \qquad \dfrac14M_{22}
$$

Drawing all four rectangular solids, we get a volume that looks as follows:

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/bfacf791cdc8ecc5641be55cd5a6513d.png>)

The upper bound $U$ for our volume is the sum of the volumes of our four rectangular solids. This gives

$$
U = \dfrac14(M_{11} + M_{21} + M_{12} + M_{22})
$$

The number $U$ is called the **upper Riemann sum with respect to the given partition**.

**Note:** We don't yet know the values of $M_{ij}$, the height of each solid. We'll learn to figure these out a little later in the lesson. For now, we will just focus on writing $U$ as an expression of $M_{ij}$.

## Calculating an Expression for the Upper Bound in Terms of Maximum Values

**Example:** Consider the partition of the rectangular region $R$ shown above and the positive function $f(x,y)$ defined on $R$. Find an expression for the upper Riemann sum of $f$ with respect to this partition.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/7bd8e396e842ee10967b4656ad76cdd5.png>)

*Hint: $M_{ij}$ denotes the maximum value of $f$ on the subregion $R_{ij}$*

**Explanation**

To calculate the upper Riemann sum $U$ with respect to the given partition, we compute the sum of the volumes of $2$ rectangular solids, one for each $R_{ij}$.

The volume of the rectangular solid corresponding to each subregion $R_{ij}$ is given by

$$
M_{ij}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{ij})
$$

where $M_{ij}$ denotes the maximum value of $f$ on the subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *maximum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *upper* sum.

So in our case, we have

$$
U = M_{11}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + M_{21}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{21})
$$

Now, notice that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) = 8
$$

and

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{21}) = 4
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= 8 \cdot M_{11} + 4 \cdot M_{21} \\
&= 8M_{11} + 4M_{21}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-maximum-value-of-the-function-in-each-partition"></a>
## Calculating the Maximum Value of the Function in Each Partition

Recall the region $R$ that we encountered earlier:

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/e0d82c3a69c228f2e3fd63e35fef29b6.png>)

Earlier, we saw that an upper bound for the volume $V$ of

$$
z = f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

defined over the region $R$ with respect to the partition $P$ (shown above) is given by

$$
U = \dfrac14(M_{11} + M_{21}+ M_{12} + M_{22})
$$

where $M_{ij}$ is the maximum value of $f$ over the subregion $R_{ij}$. Our goal now is to calculate the values of $M_{ij}$.

We start by considering $M_{11}$, so let's zoom in on $R_{11}$:

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/065ca1bee2f46c46f7562ca41fa42518.png>)

Now, let's take a closer look at the function $f(x,y)$:

$$
f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

Notice that

- as we *increase* $x$, the value of $f$ *decreases* (because we're *subtracting* $x^2$), and
- as we *increase* $y$, the value of $f$ *decreases* (because we're *subtracting* $y^2$).

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/25040c7a58995d4d98fa4ddd08b224a2.png>)

As a result, the *maximum* value of $f$ in this subregion is attained when we have the *smallest* possible $x$ and the *smallest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *bottom left corner* of the subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/8f6fdb74cb5ff315510de1c1fc576ebc.png>)

Since the function $f(x,y)$ decreases as $x$ and $y$ increase everywhere on $R$, the maximum value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$ *always* occurs at the bottom left corner!

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/771540972d749f582b70fe1121536499.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each bottom left corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{11} &= f(0, 0) = 3 \\
M_{21} &= f(\frac{1}{2}, 0) = 2.75 \\
M_{12} &= f(0, \frac{1}{2}) = 2.75 \\
M_{22} &= f(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}) = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $U$, we get

$$
U = \dfrac14(3+2.75+2.75+2.5) = 2.75
$$

Remember that $U$ is the *upper* Riemann sum, which is an *upper* bound for the volume between $z=f(x,y)$ and the region $R$. Therefore, we conclude that the volume between $z = f(x,y)$ and the region $R$ must be smaller than (or equal to) $2.75$.

<a id="updating-our-notation"></a>
## Updating Our Notation

We need to introduce just a bit more notation. To help us, let's consider the region $R$ and the partition $P$ shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/6dcc037d18942cbf36dbba6c2b5e290d.png>)

It's convenient to express our region $R$ in a way that does not involve a diagram. We can do this using the Cartesian product.

Using the Cartesian product, we have

$$
R = [1,4]\times [1,3]
$$

Similarly:

- The partition of the $x$-interval $[1,4]$ can be expressed as $P_1 = \{1,2,3,4\}$.
- The partition of the $y$-interval $[1,3]$ can be expressed as $P_2 = \{1,3\}$.
- Therefore, we can write the partition as $P = P_1\times P_2$.

Finally, we write $U(f,P)$ for the upper Riemann sum of the function $f$ with respect to $P$.

**Note:** We write $U(f,P)$ because the answer we get for the upper bound $U$ depends on the function $f$ and the partition $P$ that we choose. If we choose a different function or partition, then we'll get a different upper bound.

## Calculating an Upper Riemann Sum in the Case of a Strictly Decreasing Function

**Example:** Let $R = [1,5] \times [2,6]$ be a region in the $xy$-plane, and let $f(x,y) = 65-x^2-y^2$.
Further, let $P$ be a partition of $R$, where

$P_{1} = {1, 3, 5}, P_{2} = {2, 4, 6}, P = P_{1} \cdot P_{2}$.

Calculate the upper Riemann sum $U(f,P)$.

**Explanation**

First, let's draw the region $R$ and the partition $P$.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/9b2abc9b7e3a6bfcb6d7041a24fce9aa.png>)

The upper sum $U(f,P)$ is given by

$$
U(f,P) = M_{11}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + M_{21}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{21}) + M_{12}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{12}) + M_{22}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{22})
$$

where $M_{ij}$ is the *maximum* value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *maximum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *upper* sum.

Taking into account the fact that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{ij})=4
$$

for all $4$ regions, the formula for $U(f,P)$ reduces to

$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f, P) &= 4 \cdot M_{11} + 4 \cdot M_{21} + 4 \cdot M_{12} + 4 \cdot M_{22} \\
&= 4(M_{11} + M_{21} + M_{12} + M_{22})
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, notice that

- $f(x,y) = 65-x^2-y^2$ decreases as $x$ increases, and
- $f(x,y) = 65-x^2-y^2$ decreases as $y$ increases.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/143b73cdc2be16101dddbb200c3c39b6.png>)

As a result, the *maximum* value of $f$ in each subregion is attained when we have the *smallest* possible $x$ and the *smallest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *bottom left corner* of each subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/09e5f4950e59ffc259e3ed3bef452a2f.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each bottom left corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{11} &= f(1, 2) = 60 \\
M_{21} &= f(3, 2) = 52 \\
M_{12} &= f(1, 4) = 48 \\
M_{22} &= f(3, 4) = 40
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $U$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f, P) &= 4(60 + 52 + 48 + 40) = 800
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating an Upper Riemann Sum in the Case of a Strictly Increasing Function

**Example:** Let $R = [0,2]\times [1,3]$ be a region in the $xy$-plane, and let $f(x,y) = x+2y$.
Further, let $P$ be a partition of $R$, where

$P_{1} = {0, 1, 2}, P_{2} = {1, 2, 3}, P = P_{1} \cdot P_{2}$.

Calculate the upper Riemann sum $U(f,P)$.

**Explanation**

First, let's draw the region $R$ and the partition $P$.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/94f8ae5652f9bf448a84bd30a23dbe09.png>)

The upper sum $U(f,P)$ is given by

$$
U(f,P) = M_{11}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + M_{21}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{21}) + M_{12}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{12}) + M_{22}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{22})
$$

where $M_{ij}$ is the *maximum* value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *maximum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *upper* sum,

Taking into account the fact that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{ij})=1
$$

for all $4$ subregions, the formula for $U(f,P)$ reduces to

$$
U(f,P) = M_{11} + M_{21} + M_{12} + M_{22}
$$

Now, notice that

- $f(x,y) = x+2y$ increases as $x$ increases, and
- $f(x,y) = x+2y$ increases as $y$ increases.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/c32290b459f231155ebed12f7dbb2413.png>)

As a result, the *maximum* value of $f$ in each subregion is attained when we have the *largest* possible $x$ and the *largest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *top right corner* of each subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.5. Approximating Volumes Using Upper Riemann Sums/Images/e53b15ce6facb291ce49ed2b43c131db.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each top right corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{11} &= f(1, 2) = 5 \\
M_{21} &= f(2, 2) = 6 \\
M_{12} &= f(1, 3) = 7 \\
M_{22} &= f(2, 3) = 8
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $U$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f, P) &= 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 = 26
\end{aligned}
$$
