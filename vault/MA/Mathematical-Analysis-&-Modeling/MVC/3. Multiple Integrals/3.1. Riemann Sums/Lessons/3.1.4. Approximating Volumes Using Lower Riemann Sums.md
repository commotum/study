## Introduction

In this lesson, we will describe a process for calculating a **lower bound** for the volume enclosed between a surface $z=f(x,y)$ and the $xy$-plane.

As an example, consider the rectangle $R$ in the $xy$-plane, defined as

$$
R = {(x, y): 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1}
$$

A sketch of the region $R$ is shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/3f1e2767aa926b9faf407733405c2290.png>)

Now consider the function

$$
f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

defined over $R$, shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/a5fb4b39e807b26d944cb18b5839bacc.png>)

The space underneath the surface and above the region $R$ defines a fixed volume $V$. Our goal is to find a lower bound for $V$. We do this by approximating this volume using rectangular solids.

We start by partitioning the $x$- and $y$-intervals of $R$. Let's pick two subintervals of equal length for both $x$ and $y$.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/e4959e4e02d7e28527dde703341e7be4.png>)

The region $R$ is now partitioned into $4$ rectangles:

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/37d83b077c2de14acaa6e594d7569287.png>)

We seek a *lower* bound for the volume $V$. So, we let $m_{ij}$ be the *smallest* value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We then define a rectangular solid in each subregion whose base is $R_{ij}$ and whose height is $m_{ij}$.

For example, the rectangular solid corresponding to $R_{11}$ is given below.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/bd8fffd1cdb50468a6fadb4745c14dfa.png>)

The volume of this rectangular solid is

$$
m_{11}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{11}) = \dfrac14m_{11}
$$

Similarly, the volumes of the rectangular solids formed by the partitions $R_{21}, R_{12}$, and $R_{22}$ respectively are

$$
\dfrac14m_{21}, \qquad \dfrac14m_{12}, \qquad \dfrac14m_{22}
$$

Drawing all four rectangular solids, we get a volume that looks as follows:

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/3c41ecb69052ac6284ead133e6263eb0.png>)

The lower bound $L$ for our volume is the sum of the volumes of our four rectangular solids. This gives

$$
L = \dfrac14(m_{11} + m_{21}+ m_{12}+m_{22})
$$

The number $L$ is called the **lower Riemann sum with respect to the given partition**.

**Note:** We don't yet know the values of $m_{ij}$, the height of each solid. We'll learn to figure these out a little later in the lesson. For now, we will just focus on writing $L$ as an expression of $m_{ij}$.

<a id="calculating-an-expression-for-the-lower-bounds"></a>
## Calculating an Expression for the Lower Bounds

**Example:** Consider the partition of the rectangular region $R$ shown above and the positive function $f(x,y)$ defined on $R$. Express the lower Riemann sum of $f$ with respect to this partition.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/bdb990e68c9b789f02af33ceaed8ea6b.png>)

**Explanation**

To calculate the lower Riemann sum $L$ with respect to the given partition, we compute the sum of the volumes of $4$ rectangular solids, one for each $R_{ij}$.

The volume of the rectangular solid corresponding to each subregion $R_{ij}$ is given by

$$
m_{ij}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{ij})
$$

where $m_{ij}$ denotes the minimum value of $f$ on the subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *minimum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *lower* sum.

So in our case, we have

$$
L = m_{11}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + m_{21}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{21}) + m_{12}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{12}) + m_{22}\cdot \textrm{Area}(R_{22})
$$

Now, notice that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) = \textrm{Area}(R_{21}) = 3
$$

and

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{12}) = \textrm{Area}(R_{22}) = 1
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= 3 \cdot m_{11} + 3 \cdot m_{21} + m_{12} + m_{22} \\
&= 3m_{11} + 3m_{21} + m_{12} + m_{22}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-minimum-value-of-the-function-in-each-partition"></a>
## Calculating the Minimum Value of the Function in Each Partition

Recall the region $R$ that we encountered earlier:

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/cea9cd14cdc4e2cbfe6b954769a7840f.png>)

Earlier, we saw that a lower bound for the volume $V$ of

$$
z = f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

defined over the region $R$ with respect to the partition $P$ (shown above) is given by

$$
L = \dfrac14(m_{11} + m_{21}+ m_{12}+m_{22})
$$

where $m_{ij}$ is the minimum value of $f$ over the subregion $R_{ij}$. Our goal now is to calculate the values of $m_{ij}$.

We start by considering $m_{11}$, so let's zoom in on $R_{11}$:

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/9856e949147d4791f7a6fcab1834b19c.png>)

Now, let's take a closer look at the function $f(x,y)$:

$$
f(x,y) = 3-x^2-y^2
$$

Notice that

- as we *increase* $x$, the value of $f$ *decreases* (because we're *subtracting* $x^2$), and
- as we *increase* $y$, the value of $f$ *decreases* (because we're *subtracting* $y^2$).

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/48d502f81d5be8a29e3fe0bf4dc9c643.png>)

As a result, the *minimum* value of $f$ in this subregion is attained when we have the *largest* possible $x$ and the *largest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *top right corner* of the subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/7c065f226b371eaeb289b59de99aed75.png>)

Since the function $f(x,y)$ decreases as $x$ and $y$ increase everywhere on $R$, the minimum value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$ *always* occurs at the top right corner.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/7ce365a0ab09500eb353052ca694945d.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each top right corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{11} &= f(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}) = 2.5 \\
m_{21} &= f(1, \frac{1}{2}) = 1.75 \\
m_{12} &= f(\frac{1}{2}, 1) = 1.75 \\
m_{22} &= f(1, 1) = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $L$, we get

$$
L = \dfrac14(2.5+1.75+1.75+1) = 1.75
$$

Remember that $L$ is the *lower* Riemann sum, which is a *lower* bound for the volume between $z = f(x,y)$ and the region $R$. Therefore, we conclude that the volume between $z = f(x,y)$ and the region $R$ must be greater than (or equal to) $1.75$.

<a id="updating-our-notation"></a>
## Updating Our Notation

We need to introduce just a bit more notation. To illustrate, let's consider the region $R$ and the partition $P$ shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/e2c418f321e198f8b17089728801da88.png>)

It's convenient to express our region $R$ and partition $P$ in a way that does not involve a diagram. We can do this using the Cartesian product.

Using the Cartesian product, we have

$$
R = [1,4]\times [1,3]
$$

Similarly:

- The partition of the $x$-interval $[1,4]$ can be expressed as $P_1 = \{1,2,3,4\}$.
- The partition of the $y$-interval $[1,3]$ can be expressed as $P_2 = \{1,3\}$.
- Therefore, we can write the partition as $P = P_1\times P_2$.

Finally, we write $L(f,P)$ for the lower Riemann sum of the function $f$ with respect to $P$.

**Note:** We write $L(f,P)$ because the answer we get for the lower bound $L$ depends on the function $f$ and the partition $P$ that we choose. If we choose a different function or partition, then we'll get a different lower bound.

<a id="calculating-a-lower-riemann-sum-for-a-strictly-decreasing-function"></a>
## Calculating a Lower Riemann Sum for a Strictly Decreasing Function

**Example:** Let $R = [1,3] \times [0,2]$ be a region in the $xy$-plane, and let $f(x,y) = 20-x^2-y^2$.
Further, let $P$ be a partition of $R$, where

$P_{1} = {1, 2, 3}, P_{2} = {0, 1, 2}, P = P_{1} \cdot P_{2}$.

Calculate the lower Riemann sum $L(f,P)$.

**Explanation**

First, let's draw the region $R$ and the partition $P$.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/46cc42002ea537e8af73cd4dc56915ef.png>)

The lower Riemann sum $L(f,P)$ is given by

$$
L(f,P) = m_{11}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + m_{21}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{21}) + m_{12}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{12}) + m_{22}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{22})
$$

where $m_{ij}$ is the minimum value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *minimum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *lower* sum.

Taking into account the fact that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{ij}) = 1
$$

for all $4$ subregions, the formula for $L(f,P)$ reduces to

$$
L(f,P) = m_{11} + m_{21}+ m_{12} + m_{22}
$$

Now, notice that

- $f(x,y) = 20-x^2-y^2$ decreases as $x$ increases, and
- $f(x,y) = 20-x^2-y^2$ decreases as $y$ increases.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/5c7c567ad1a1236e0bed12cbb74d8657.png>)

As a result, the *minimum* value of $f$ in each subregion is attained when we have the *largest* possible $x$ and the *largest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *top right corner* of each subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/2c0d05fce61626b7c70063064b2eb549.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each top right corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{11} &= f(2, 1) = 15 \\
m_{21} &= f(3, 1) = 10 \\
m_{12} &= f(2, 2) = 12 \\
m_{22} &= f(3, 2) = 7
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $L$, we get

$$
L(f,P) = 15+10+12+7 = 44
$$

## Calculating a Lower Riemann Sum for a Strictly Increasing Function

**Example:** Let $R = [0,2] \times [1,3]$ be a region in the $xy$-plane, and let $f(x,y) = x+2y$.
Further, let $P$ be a partition of $R$, where

$P_{1} = {0, 1, 2}, P_{2} = {1, 2, 3}, P = P_{1} \cdot P_{2}$.

Calculate the lower Riemann sum $L(f,P)$.

**Explanation**

First, let's draw the region $R$ and the partition $P$.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/64b9a595613bbab374eb084adcd919f8.png>)

The lower sum $L(f,P)$ is given by

$$
L(f,P) = m_{11}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{11}) + m_{21}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{21}) + m_{12}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{12}) + m_{22}\cdot\textrm{Area}(R_{22})
$$

where $m_{ij}$ is the *minimum* value of $f$ in each subregion $R_{ij}$. We pick the *minimum* value of $f$ because we're finding the *lower* sum.

Taking into account the fact that

$$
\textrm{Area}(R_{ij}) = 1
$$

for all $4$ subregions, the formula for $L(f,P)$ reduces to

$$
L(f,P) = m_{11} + m_{21}+ m_{12} + m_{22}
$$

Now, notice that

- $f(x,y) = x+2y$ increases as $x$ increases, and
- $f(x,y) = x+2y$ increases as $y$ increases.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/cf15a413bc2fd256a2782ed519c67d13.png>)

As a result, the *minimum* value of $f$ in each subregion is attained when we have the *smallest* possible $x$ and the *smallest* possible $y$. This is the value at the *bottom left corner* of each subregion.

![](<../Source/3.1.4. Approximating Volumes Using Lower Riemann Sums/Images/8877f481ed010f430a9590b25e256f00.png>)

Calculating the value of $f$ at each bottom left corner, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_{11} &= f(0, 1) = 2 \\
m_{21} &= f(1, 1) = 3 \\
m_{12} &= f(0, 2) = 4 \\
m_{22} &= f(1, 2) = 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting the above values in our expression for $L$, we get

$$
L(f, P) = 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 14
$$
