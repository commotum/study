## Introduction

Recall that for a point $(\rho,\theta,\phi)$ in spherical polar coordinates,

- $\rho \geq 0$ is the distance from the point to the origin,
- $[MATH: {\color{black}\theta}\in [0, 2\pi)]$ is the angle between the point and the positive $x$-axis, and
- $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle the corresponding vector makes with the positive $z$-axis.

When we fix one of these coordinates, we obtain some simple surfaces. Let's describe them:

- First, let's consider the equation
$\rho = c$,
for $c \gt 0$. This equation describes the set of points on a surface whose distance from the origin equals $c$. So, we obtain a sphere of radius $c$ centered at $O$.![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/c5d10ebd42b4d62f6f6ddd5cd178cbcb.png>)
- Next, consider the equation
$\theta=c$,
where $[MATH: c\in [0,2\pi).]$ This equation describes the set of points that make an angle of $c$ radians with the positive $x$-axis. Therefore, we obtain a *half-plane* whose boundary is the $z$-axis.![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/ee6645716b0607568075c2f339b20c07.png>)
- Finally, consider the equation
$\phi = c$,
where $c\in (0,\pi)$. This equation describes the set of points that make an angle of $c$ radians with the positive $z$-axis. Therefore, we obtain a *half-cone* whose axis is the $z$-axis.![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/8aac453c60706903c90c8c05b01ce0cb.png>)

## Identifying the Spherical Representation of a Surface From an Image

**Example:** What is the equation of the surface shown above in spherical polar coordinates?

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/96e952bf87ea43bab876f5dbde7660cd.png>)

**Explanation**

In the picture, we are given a half-cone with the $z$-axis as its axis.

We write the position of a point (vector) in spherical polar coordinates as $(\rho,\theta,\phi)$, where

- $\rho \geq 0$ is the distance from the point to the origin,
- $[MATH: {\color{black}\theta}\in [0, 2\pi)]$ is the angle between the point and the positive $x$-axis, and
- $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle the corresponding vector makes with the positive $z$-axis.

Every point on the given half-cone makes an angle of

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

radians with the positive $z$-axis. On the other hand, $\rho$ and $\theta$ can vary arbitrarily in their respective domains.

Therefore, the spherical polar equation of the surface is

$$
\phi = \dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

## Identifying the Spherical Representation of a Surface From an Equation

**Example:** What surface is defined by the equation $\rho=5$ in spherical polar coordinates?

**Explanation**

We write the position of a point (vector) in spherical polar coordinates as $(\rho,\theta,\phi)$, where

- $\rho \geq 0$ is the distance from the point to the origin,
- $[MATH: {\color{black}\theta}\in [0, 2\pi)]$ is the angle between the point and the positive $x$-axis, and
- $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle the corresponding vector makes with the positive $z$-axis.

With that in mind, let's examine our equation:

$$
\rho=5
$$

This equation specifies that for any point on the surface, the distance from the point to the origin must equal $5$. On the other hand, $\theta$ and $\phi$ can vary arbitrarily in their respective domains.

Therefore, our surface is a sphere of radius $5$.

## Expressing Part of a Sphere in Spherical Coordinates

**Example:** Use spherical polar coordinates to describe the solid enclosed by the quarter-sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=25$ for $y \geq 0$ and $z \leq 0$, shown above.

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/5654932cdfd91d86596d690f06295ce4.png>)

**Explanation**

We write the position of a point (vector) in spherical polar coordinates as $(\rho,\theta,\phi)$, where

- $\rho \geq 0$ is the distance from the point to the origin,
- $[MATH: {\color{black}\theta}\in [0, 2\pi)]$ is the angle between the point and the positive $x$-axis, and
- $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle the corresponding vector makes with the positive $z$-axis.

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/56b93c553c9a936450ae7f7f62aa0966.png>)

Notice that inside our region, the spherical polar coordinates vary within the following domains:

- $\rho \in [0,5]$
- $\theta \in \left[0,\pi\right]$
- $\phi \in \left[\dfrac{\pi}2,\pi\right]$

Therefore, we have

$$
\left\{(\rho, \theta, \phi) \,: \, 0 \leq \rho \leq \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}5}, \: {0} \leq \theta \leq \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\pi}, \: \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\dfrac{\pi}2} \leq \phi \leq {\pi} \right\}
$$

## Expressing Part of a Shifted Sphere in Spherical Coordinates

**Example:** Use spherical polar coordinates to describe the solid enclosed by the hemisphere $x^2+y^2+z^2+4z=0$ for $x \geq 0$, shown above.

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/a486e3c62a157d0581ad2221d2947aab.png>)

**Explanation**

We write the position of a point (vector) in spherical polar coordinates as $(\rho,\theta,\phi)$, where

- $\rho \geq 0$ is the distance from the point to the origin,
- $[MATH: {\color{black}\theta}\in [0, 2\pi)]$ is the angle between the point and the positive $x$-axis, and
- $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle the corresponding vector makes with the positive $z$-axis.

If $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ are the spherical coordinates of a point, then its Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$ can be found by using the following formulas:

$$
x = \rho\cos\theta\sin\phi, \qquad y = \rho\sin\theta\sin\phi, \qquad z = \rho\cos\phi
$$

Notice that the region $R$ represents a half-sphere of radius $2$ centered at $(0,0,-2)$.

With that in mind, let's now write the

$$
x^2+y^2+z^2+4z=0
$$

in spherical polar coordinates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2} + 4z &= 0 \\
ρ^{2} + 4ρ\cos ϕ &= 0 \\
ρ(ρ + 4\cos ϕ) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\rho$ cannot be identical to zero on the entire sphere, we must have

$$
\rho = -4\cos\phi
$$

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Surfaces in Spherical Polar Coordinates/Images/136ebbc722e56fc5a4c38e5789005004.png>)

Notice that inside our region, the spherical polar coordinates vary within the following domains:

- $\rho \in [0,-4\cos\phi]$
- $\theta \in \left[-\dfrac\pi 2,\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$
- $\phi \in \left[\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]$

Therefore, we have

$$
\left\{(\rho, \theta, \phi) \,: \, 0 \leq \rho \leq -4\cos\phi, \: -\dfrac\pi 2 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \: \dfrac{\pi}{2} \leq \phi \leq \pi \right\}
$$
