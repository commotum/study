## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to carry out a hypothesis test on the population mean $\mu$ of a normally distributed population when the population variance $\sigma^2$ is known.

Suppose the scores on a particular science test are normally distributed with mean $\mu = 69$ points and standard deviation $\sigma = 14$ points. After changing some test criteria, the test administrators suspect that the mean score is now greater than $69$ points. They decide to carry out a hypothesis at the $5\%$ significance level. They sample $30$ test results and found that the sample mean was

$$
\overline{x} = 75
$$

points.

Is there sufficient evidence to conclude that the mean score is now greater than $69$ points?

To answer this question, we proceed in a two-fold manner:

- Firstly, we'll write down the null and alternative hypotheses and establish the critical region of the test.
- Secondly, we'll compute the test statistic and determine whether the result is statistically significant.

We are told that the population is normally distributed. Therefore, the distribution of the sample mean is also normal, namely,

$$
\overline{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n} \right)
$$

and we have that

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\overline{X}$ is the sample mean,
- $\mu$ is the population mean,
- $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

Let's write down our null and alternative hypotheses:

- $H_0: \mu = 69$ is the null hypothesis
- $H_1: \mu \gt 69$ is the alternative (one-tailed) hypothesis

Next, we'll compute the critical region. Remember that the critical region contains all $z$-values that are *collectively* unlikely under the null hypothesis and therefore cause the null hypothesis to be rejected.

To find the critical region for this test at the

$$
\alpha = 5\%
$$

level of significance, we need to find a particular value $z_p$ of $Z$ such that

$$
P(Z\geq z_p) = \alpha = 0.05
$$

To do this, we can use the following percentage points table.

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $\,\color{blue}1.645\,$ | $1.960$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

From the table,

$$
P(Z > 1.645) = 0.05
$$

So, we have that

- $z_{0.05} = 1.645$ is our critical value, and
- $Z\geq 1.645$ the critical region.

![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/f15693e4409ca65055004fac4e698203.png>)

Now that we've established the critical region, let's compute the test statistic.

---

<a id="computing-the-test-statistic"></a>
## Computing the Test Statistic

We have the following critical region at the $5\%$ significance level.

![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/83bc92867a1e8491fcdc7604e689089d.png>)

We're told that out of a sample of $30$ tests, the mean of the sample was $75$ points. Therefore, we have the following sample data:

$$
\overline{x} = 75, \qquad n = 30
$$

Assuming the null hypothesis is true, i.e., $\mu=69$, and that $\sigma=14$, we can compute the test statistic as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= (x^(―) - μ)/(σ/\sqrt{n}) \\
&= \frac{75 - 69}{14/\sqrt{30}} \\
&= 2.347
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that our test statistic ($2.347$) lies in the critical region, as shown below.

![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/d071f74ea3a3daddbe3fedf75619a8e2.png>)

Therefore, we conclude the following:

- If we assume that the population mean is $\mu=69$ (i.e., the null hypothesis is true), getting a sample with a mean of $\overline{x}=75$ or more has a probability that is *smaller than* $0.05 = 5\%$, our significance level.
- Therefore, we have a statistically significant result, and we reject the null hypothesis.
- In conclusion, there is *sufficient* evidence that, at the $5\%$ level of significance, we have $\mu \gt 69$.

Now, let's summarize the general process for one-tailed tests.

## Summary of One-Tailed Hypothesis Tests

To conduct a *one-tailed* hypothesis test for the population mean with known population variance, we proceed as follows:

- **Step 1.** Check the conditions:The population variance $\sigma^2$ must be *known*.The distribution must be *normal* (or the sample size must be *sufficiently large* - we'll discuss this case in more detail shortly).
- **Step 2.** Define the test:Pick a significance level ($\%$).State the *null* and the *alternative* hypotheses:$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$$H_1: \mu \gt \mu_0\:$ for a right-tailed test or $\:H_1: \mu \lt \mu_0$ for a left-tailed test.Here, $\mu_0$ is the value of $\mu$ under the null hypothesis.
- **Step 3.** Compute the test statistic:Assuming the null hypothesis, compute the *test statistic* $z = \dfrac{\overline{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$, where $\overline{x}$ is the sample mean, $\mu$ is the population mean, $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and $n$ is the sample size. Compute the *critical value* using the standard normal distribution, and write down the *critical region*.
- **Step 4.** Interpret the result:Reject the null hypothesis if the test statistic $z$ lies inside the critical region. In cases like this, we say that there is *sufficient* evidence to reject the null hypothesis at the given significance level.Don't reject the null hypothesis if the test statistic $z$ lies outside the critical region. In cases like this, we say that there is *insufficient* evidence to reject the null hypothesis at the given significance level.

Let's now look at an example where the critical region lies on the left tail of the distribution.

## Testing a Hypothesis Given a Normal Population: One-Tailed Tests

**Example:** Consider a sample of size $n=45$ from a normal population with standard deviation $\sigma=7$. We wish to carry out a hypothesis test at a $5\%$ significance level to determine whether there is sufficient evidence that the population mean $\mu$ is smaller than $18$.

Given that the mean of the sample is $\overline{x}=17$, which of the following statements are true?

1. The **one-tailed** critical region for the test statistic is approximately $Z \leq -2.576$
2. At the $5\%$ level of significance, there is **insufficient** evidence that $\mu \lt 18$
3. At the $5\%$ level of significance, there is **sufficient** evidence that $\mu \lt 18$

*Hint: Given a random variable $Z \sim N(0,1)$, the table below shows the $z$-scores $z_p$ such that $P(Z > z_p) = p$ for some particular values of $p$.*

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $1.645$ | $1.960$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

**Explanation**

We are told that the population is normally distributed. Therefore, the distribution of sample means is also normal, namely,

$$
\overline{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n} \right)
$$

and we have that

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\overline{X}$ is the sample mean,
- $\mu$ is the population mean,
- $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

In our example:

- $H_0: \mu = 18$ is the null hypothesis
- $H_1: \mu \lt 18$ is the alternative (one-tailed) hypothesis

Assuming the null hypothesis, i.e., $\mu=18$, we compute the test statistic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= (x^(―) - μ)/(σ/\sqrt{n}) \\
&= \frac{17 - 18}{7/\sqrt{45}} \\
&\approx - 0.958
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's now examine our statements.

- Statement I is false. According to the table, the $5\%$ one-tailed critical value is $z \approx 1.645$. $p$$0.100$$0.050$$0.025$$0.010$$0.005$$z_p$$1.282$$\color{blue}1.645$$1.960$$2.326$$2.576$However, since the alternative hypothesis is $\mu \lt 18$, we must consider the left tail. We do this using the symmetry of the normal distribution. So, our critical region is $Z \leq -1.645$.

- Statement II is true, while statement III is false. Our test statistic ($-0.958$) does not lie in the critical region, as shown below.![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/91fc9d296fbce93a9742564a453c0d37.png>)
So, we do not reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. As a result, we conclude the following:$\qquad$ *There is **insufficient** evidence that, at the $5\%$ level of significance, we have $\mu \lt 18$.*

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

## Two-Tailed Hypothesis Tests

To conduct a **two-tailed** hypothesis test for the population mean with known population variance, we proceed similarly to the one-tailed case.

The only differences are the following:

- The *alternative hypothesis* will be $H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0$.
- We compute the *critical value* and write down the *critical region* by considering both tails of the distribution. Remember that for a two-tailed test, if the significance level is $\alpha$, then we allow $\dfrac\alpha2$ at either tail.

Let's see an example.

---

<a id="testing-a-hypothesis-given-a-normal-population-two-tailed-tests"></a>
## Testing a Hypothesis Given a Normal Population: Two-Tailed Tests

**Example:** Consider a sample of size $n=50$ from a normal population with standard deviation $\sigma=12$. We wish to carry out a hypothesis test at the $10\%$ level of significance to determine whether there is sufficient evidence that the population mean $\mu$ does not equal $26$.

Given that the mean of the sample is $\overline{x}=25$, which of the following statements are true?

1. The **two-tailed** critical region for the test statistic is approximately $Z \leq -1.645$ or $Z \geq 1.645$
2. At the $10\%$ level of significance, there is **insufficient** evidence that $\mu \neq 26$
3. At the $10\%$ level of significance, there is **sufficient** evidence that $\mu \neq 26$

*Hint: Given a random variable $Z \sim N(0,1)$, the table below shows the $z$-scores $z_p$ such that $P(Z > z_p) = p$ for some particular values of $p$.*

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $1.645$ | $1.960$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

**Explanation**

We are told that the population is normally distributed. Therefore, the distribution of sample means is also normal, namely,

$$
\overline{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n} \right)
$$

and we have that

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\overline{X}$ is the sample mean,
- $\mu$ is the population mean,
- $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

In our example:

- $H_0: \mu = 26$ is the null hypothesis
- $H_1: \mu \neq 26$ is the alternative (two-tailed) hypothesis

Assuming the null hypothesis, i.e., $\mu=26$, we compute the test statistic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= (x^(―) - μ)/(σ/\sqrt{n}) \\
&= \frac{25 - 26}{12/\sqrt{50}} \\
&\approx - 0.589
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's now examine our statements.

- Statement I is true. According to the table, the $5\%$ *two-tailed* critical value (the same as the $10\% \div 2 = 5\%$ *one-tailed* critical value) is $z \approx 1.645$.
$p$$0.100$$0.050$$0.025$$0.010$$0.005$$z_p$$1.282$$\color{blue}1.645$$1.960$$2.326$$2.576$
Since we are considering both tails, our critical region is
$Z \leq -1.645 \qquad\textrm{or}\qquad Z \geq 1.645$.
- Statement II is true, while statement III is false. Our test statistic ($-0.589$) does not lie in the critical region, as shown below.![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/cabab408796c3a3f7f124d98113767ce.png>)
So, we do not reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. As a result, we conclude the following:$\qquad$ *There is **insufficient** evidence that, at the $10\%$ level of significance, we have $\mu \neq 26$.*

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

## Large Samples

Up to now, we've assumed that the underlying population is normally distributed. However, if we have a *sufficiently large* sample, then according to the central limit theorem, we can use the approximation

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \approx N(0,1)
$$

Typically, "sufficiently large" means that

$$
n \geq 30
$$

So, even though we might not know the underlying distribution, we can still use the following formula for the test statistic:

$$
z = \dfrac{\overline{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}
$$

---

**Example:** Consider a sample of size $n = 250$ from a population with standard deviation $\sigma = 7$. We wish to carry out a hypothesis test at the $2.5 \%$ significance level to determine whether there is sufficient evidence that the population mean $\mu$ is smaller than $10$.

Given that the mean of the sample is $\overline{x} = 9$, which of the following statements are true?

1. The critical region for the test statistic is approximately $Z \leq -2.576$
2. At the $2.5 \%$ level of significance, there is **insufficient** evidence that $\mu \lt 10$
3. At the $2.5 \%$ level of significance, there is **sufficient** evidence that $\mu \lt 10$

$$

$$

$$

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\overline{X}$ is the sample mean,
- $\mu$ is the population mean,
- $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

In our example:

- $H_0: \mu = 10$ is the null hypothesis
- $H_1: \mu \lt 10$ is the alternative (one-tailed) hypothesis

Assuming the null hypothesis, i.e., $\mu = 10$, we compute the test statistic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= (x^(―) - μ)/(σ/\sqrt{n}) \\
&= \frac{9 - 10}{7/\sqrt{250}} \\
&\approx - 2.259
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's now examine our statements.

- Statement I is false. According to the table, the $2.5 \%$ one-tailed critical value is $z \approx 1.960$.
$p$$0.100$$0.050$$0.025$$0.010$$0.005$$z_p$$1.282$$1.645$$\color{blue}1.960$$2.326$$2.576$
Since the alternative hypothesis is $\mu \lt 10$, we must consider the left tail. So, our critical region is
$Z \leq -1.960$.
- Statement II is false, while statement III is true. Our test statistic ($-2.259$) lies in the critical region, as shown below.![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/5ae70ff9c6bfabe8c2f29c8731d2d96d.png>)
So, we reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. As a result, we conclude the following:$\qquad$ *There is **sufficient** evidence that, at the $2.5 \%$ level of significance, we have $\mu \lt 10$.*

## Applications of Hypothesis Testing

**Example:** The manufacturer claims that the weight of a particular chocolate bar has population mean $\mu = 50 \, \textrm{g}$ and population standard deviation of $\sigma = 5 \, \textrm{g}$. An inspector selected $150$ bars of chocolate and found that their mean weight is $49.3 \, \textrm{g}$.
They wish to carry out a hypothesis test to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to challenge the manufacturer's claim.

By carrying out a hypothesis test at a $5\%$ significance level to determine whether there is sufficient evidence that the population mean $\mu$ does not equal $50 \, \textrm{g}$, determine which of the following statements are true:

1. The critical region for the test statistic is approximately $Z \leq -1.645$ or $Z \geq 1.645$.
2. At the $5\%$ level of significance, there is **insufficient** evidence that $\mu \neq 50 \, \textrm{g}$
3. At the $5\%$ level of significance, there is **sufficient** evidence that $\mu \neq 50 \, \textrm{g}$

*Hint: Given a random variable $Z \sim N(0,1)$, the table below shows the $z$-scores $z_p$ such that $P(Z > z_p) = p$ for some particular values of $p$.*

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $1.645$ | $1.960$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

**Explanation**

We do not know the distribution of the population. However, the sample size

$$
n = 150 \geq 30
$$

is *sufficiently large*. Therefore, according to the central limit theorem, the distribution of sample means is also approximately normal. In other words, we can use the approximation

$$
\overline{X} \sim N\left(\mu, \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n} \right)
$$

and we have that

$$
\dfrac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\overline{X}$ is the sample mean,
- $\mu$ is the population mean,
- $\sigma^2$ is the population variance, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

In our example:

- $H_0: \mu = 50$ is the null hypothesis
- $H_1: \mu \neq 50$ is the alternative (two-tailed) hypothesis

Also, we have

$$
\overline{x} = 49.3 \, \textrm{g} \qquad n = 150, \qquad \sigma = 5 \, \textrm{g}
$$

Assuming the null hypothesis, i.e., $\mu=50$, we compute the test statistic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= (x^(―) - μ)/(σ/\sqrt{n}) \\
&= \frac{49.3 - 50}{5/\sqrt{150}} \\
&=-1.715
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's now examine our statements.

- Statement I is false. According to the table, the $5\%$ *two-tailed* critical value (the same as the $5\% \div 2 = 2.5\%$ *one-tailed* critical value) is $z \approx 1.960$.
$p$$0.100$$0.050$$0.025$$0.010$$0.005$$z_p$$1.282$$1.645$$\color{blue}1.960$$2.326$$2.576$
Since we are considering both tails, our critical region is
$Z \leq -1.960 \qquad\textrm{or}\qquad Z \geq 1.960$.
- Statement II is true, while statement III is false. Our test statistic ($-1.715$) does not lie in the critical region, as shown below.![](<../Source/8.1.7. Hypothesis Tests for One Mean- Known Population Variance/Images/726028ed77464e86d2ed5f418c7bd787.png>)
So, we can't reject the null hypothesis $H_0$. As a result, we conclude the following:$\qquad$ *There is **insufficient** evidence that, at the $5\%$ level of significance, we have $\mu \neq 50$.*

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."
