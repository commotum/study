## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll use moment-generating functions to prove certain properties about sums of independent random variables with known distributions.

First, let's introduce some notation:

> *The random variables $X$ and $Y$ are **equal in distribution,** written as*
> $X \overset{d}{=} Y$
> *if the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of $X$ and $Y$ are the same for all real values of $x{:}$*
> $F_X(x) = F_Y(x), \qquad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}$,
> *where $F_X(x)$ and $F_Y(x)$ are the CDFs of $X$ and $Y$ respectively.*

We're now ready for the main result of this lesson:

> *Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables, and $M_X(t)$ and $M_Y(t)$ be the moment-generating functions of $X$ and $Y$, respectively. If*
> $M_X(t) = M_Y(t)$
> *for all values of $t$ in an open interval around $t=0$, then*
> $X \overset{d}{=} Y$.
> *This result is called the **uniqueness property of moment-generating functions.***

This means a random variable's probability distribution is *uniquely determined* by its MGF. Therefore, if the MGF of a random variable matches that of a known distribution, they have the same distribution.

We can use this result to identify the distributions of sums of independent random variables. Let's see some examples.

## Identifying the Distribution of a Sum of Discrete Random Variables

**Example:** Consider the independent random variables $Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_{10}$ where $Y_i\sim \mathrm{B}(5,0.3)$ for $1\leq i \leq 10$. What is the distribution of $Y=Y_1+Y_2+\cdots + Y_{10}$?

*Hint: You may wish to make use of the following results.*

| Distribution | $M(t)$ | $t$-domain |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $U \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \}$ | $\dfrac{1}{n} \left(e^{x_1t} + e^{x_2t} + \ldots + e^{x_nt} \right)$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |
| $\textrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ | $1-p+pe^t$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |
| $B(n,p)$ | $(1-p+pe^t)^n$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |
| $\textrm{Geom}(p)$ | $\dfrac{pe^t}{1-(1-p)e^t}$ | $t<-\ln (1-p)$ |
| $\textrm{NB}(n,p)$ | $\left(\dfrac{pe^t}{1-(1-p)e^t}\right)^n$ | $t<-\ln (1-p)$ |
| $\text{Po}(\lambda)$ | $e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |

**Explanation**

Recall that a random variable's probability distribution is uniquely determined by its moment-generating function (MGF). Therefore, if the MGF of a random variable $X$ matches that of a known distribution, then $X$ has the same distribution.

For a binomial random variable $Y_i \sim \textrm{B}(n,p)$, we have

$$
M_{Y_i}(t) = (1-p+pe^t)^n
$$

In our case, since $Y_i \sim \textrm{B}(5,0.3)$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{Y_{i}}(t) &= (1 - 0.3 + 0.3e^{t})^{5} \\
&= (0.7 + 0.3e^{t})^{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

If

$$
Y = Y_1+Y_2+\cdots + Y_{10}
$$

where $Y_1, Y_2,\ldots Y_{10}$ are independent random variables with moment-generating function $M_{Y_i}(t)$, then the moment-generating function of $Y$ is

$$
M_Y(t) = M_{Y_1}(t) \cdot M_{Y_2}(t) \cdots M_{Y_{10}}(t)
$$

In our case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{Y}(t) &= (0.7 + 0.3e^{t})^{5} \cdot (0.7 + 0.3e^{t})^{5}⋯(0.7 + 0.3e^{t})^{5}^(⏞)^(10times) \\
&= (0.7 + 0.3e^{t})^{50}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $M_Y(t)$ is the MGF of a binomial random variable with $n = 50$ and $p= 0.3$.

Therefore, we conclude that $Y\sim B(50,0.3)$.

## Identifying the Distribution of a Sum of Continuous Random Variables

**Example:** Consider the independent random variables $Y_1\sim \chi^2(2)$ and $Y_2\sim \chi^2(3)$. What is the distribution of $Y=Y_1+Y_2$?

*Hint: You may wish to make use of the following results.*

| Distribution | $M(t)$ | $t$-domain |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $U[a,b]$ | ${(e^{tb} - e^{ta})/(t(b - a)),\begin{vmatrix}t \ne 0 \\ 1,\end{vmatrix}t = 0$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |
| $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ | $\exp\left({\mu t+\dfrac12\sigma^2t^2}\right)$ | $\,\,\,t\in\mathbb R\,\,\,$ |
| $\chi^2(k)$ | $(1-2t)^{-k/2}$ | $t<\dfrac12$ |
| $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ | $\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}$ | $t<\lambda$ |
| $\Gamma(\alpha,\lambda)$ | $\left(\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^\alpha$ | $t<\lambda$ |

**Explanation**

Recall that a random variable's probability distribution is uniquely determined by its moment-generating function (MGF). Therefore, if the MGF of a random variable $X$ matches that of a known distribution, then $X$ has the same distribution.

For a chi-square random variable $Y_i \sim \chi^2(k)$, we have

$$
M_{Y_i}(t) = \left(1 - 2t \right)^{-k/2}, \quad t < \dfrac12
$$

In our case, since $Y_{1} \sim \chi^2(2)$, we have

$$
M_{Y_1}(t) = \left(1 - 2t \right)^{-1}, \quad t < \dfrac12
$$

Similarly, since $Y_{2} \sim \chi^2(3)$, we have

$$
M_{Y_{2}}(t) = \left(1 - 2t \right)^{-3/2}, \quad t < \dfrac12
$$

If

$$
Y = Y_1+Y_2
$$

where $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ are independent random variables with moment-generating functions $M_{Y_1}(t)$ and $M_{Y_2}(t)$, respectively, then the moment-generating function of $Y$ is

$$
M_Y(t) = M_{Y_1}(t) M_{Y_2}(t)
$$

So, for

$$
t < \dfrac12
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{Y}(t) &= (1 - 2t)^{-1} \cdot (1 - 2t)^{-3/2} \\
&= (1 - 2t)^{-5/2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $M_Y(t)$ has the form of the MGF of a chi-square random variable with $k = 5$.

Therefore, we conclude that $Y_1+Y_2 \sim \chi^2(5)$.

## Identifying True Statements Regarding Sums of Random Variables

**Example:** Let $X_1 \sim N(5, 4)$ and $X_2 \sim N(3, 2)$ be independent, and $Y = X_1 + X_2$. Which of the following statements are true?

1. $M_Y(t) = M_{X_1}(t) \cdot M_{X_2}(t)$
2. $Y \stackrel{d}{=} X_1 + X_2$
3. $Y \sim N(8, 6)$

*Hint: The moment-generating function of a normally distributed random variable $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is given by*
$M_X(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}, \qquad t\in\mathbb R$.

**Explanation**

Let's inspect each statement in turn.

- Statement I is true. In general, if $Y = X_1 + X_2$, where $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent random variables with MGF's $M_{X_1}(t)$ and $M_{X_2}(t)$, respectively, then the MGF of $Y$ is
$M_Y(t) = M_{X_1}(t) M_{X_2}(t)$.
- Statement II is true. Since $Y = X_1 + X_2$, we know that $Y$ and $X_1+X_2$ have the same probability distribution. We represent this as $Y \stackrel{d}{=} X_1 + X_2$.
- Statement III is true. A normally distributed random variable $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has the following MGF:
$M(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}, \qquad t\in\mathbb R$
So the MGF's for $X_1 \sim N(5, 4)$ and $X_2 \sim N(3, 2)$ are
$M_{X_1}(t) = e^{5t + 2t^2}, \qquad M_{X_2}(t) = e^{3t + t^2}$,
and we have
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1}}(t)M_{X_{2}}(t); = e^{5t + 2t^{2}} \cdot e^{3t + t^{2}}; = e^{8t + 3t^{2}}$.
Since $M_Y(t)$ is the MGF for the distribution $N(8, 6)$, we conclude that $Y \sim N(8, 6)$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I, II, and III."

## Proving Some Well-Known Results

Let's use our knowledge of the uniqueness property of moment-generating functions to prove some general results.

- **Sums of Bernoulli Random Variables**: Suppose $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\sim \textrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ are independent. Then
$Y = X_1+X_2+ \cdots + X_n \sim B(n,p)$.
To prove this, let $M_{X_i}(t)$ be the moment-generating function of $X_i$ for $i=1,2,\ldots, n$. Then, we have
$M_{X_i}(t) = 1 - p + pe^t$.
By the multiplicative property of moment-generating functions, we have that
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1} + X_{2} + ⋯ + X_{n}}(t); = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t) \cdot ⋯ \cdot M_{X_{n}}(t); = (1 - p + pe^{t}) \cdot (1 - p + pe^{t}) \cdot ⋯ \cdot (1 - p + pe^{t}); = (1 - p + pe^{t})^{n}$,
which is the moment-generating function of a binomial random variable with distribution $B(n, p)$. Now, since any given distribution is characterized by its moment-generating function (meaning that no other distribution can have the same moment-generating function), we conclude that
$Y\sim B(n,p)$.
- **Sums of Binomial Random Variables**: Suppose $X_1\sim B(n_1,p)$ and $X_2\sim B(n_2,p)$ are independent. Then
$Y = X_1+X_2\sim B(n_1+n_2, p)$.
To prove this, let $M_{X_1}(t)$ and $M_{X_2}(t)$ be the moment-generating functions of $X_1$ and $X_2$ respectively. Then, we have
$M_{X_1}(t) = (pe^t+q)^{n_1}, \qquad M_{X_2}(t) = (pe^t+q)^{n_2}$.
By the multiplicative property of moment-generating functions, we have
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1} + X_{2}}(t); = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t); = (pe^{t} + q)^{n_{1}} \cdot (pe^{t} + q)^{n_{2}}; = (pe^{t} + q)^{n_{1} + n_{2}}$.
This is the moment-generating function of a binomial random variable with distribution $B(n_1+n_2, p)$. Therefore, by the uniqueness property, we conclude
$Y\sim B(n_1+n_2, p)$.
- **Sums of Poisson Random Variables**: If $X_1\sim \textrm{Po}(\lambda_1)$ and $X_2\sim \textrm{Po}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, then
$Y = X_1+X_2\sim \textrm{Po}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$.
To prove this, let $M_{X_1}(t)$ and $M_{X_2}(t)$ be the moment-generating functions of $X_1$ and $X_2$ respectively. Then, we have
$M_{X_1}(t) = e^{\lambda_1(e^t-1)}, \qquad M_{X_2}(t) = e^{\lambda_2(e^t-1)}$.
By the multiplicative property of moment-generating functions, we have
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1} + X_{2}}(t); = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t); = e^{λ_{1}(e^{t} - 1)} \cdot e^{λ_{2}(e^{t} - 1)}; = e^{λ_{1}(e^{t} - 1) + λ_{2}(e^{t} - 1)}; = e^{(λ_{1} + λ_{2})(e^{t} - 1)}$
This is the moment-generating function of a Poisson random variable with distribution $\textrm{Po}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. Therefore, by the uniqueness property, we conclude
$Y\sim \textrm{Po}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$.

- **Sums of Normal Random Variables**: Suppose $X_1\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $X_2\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ are independent. Then
$Y = X_1+X_2\sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.
To prove this, let $M_{X_1}(t)$ and $M_{X_2}(t)$ be the moment-generating functions of $X_1$ and $X_2$ respectively. Then, we have
$M_{X_1}(t) = \exp\left(\mu_1 t + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2_1 t^2\right), \qquad M_{X_2}(t) = \exp\left(\mu_2 t + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2_2 t^2\right)$,
where $\exp(x) = e^x$ is the exponential function.By the multiplicative property of moment-generating functions and by the properties of the exponential function, we have that
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1} + X_{2}}(t); = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t); = \exp (μ_{1}t + \frac{1}{2}σ_{1}^{2}t^{2}) \cdot \exp (μ_{2}t + \frac{1}{2}σ_{2}^{2}t^{2}); = \exp (μ_{1}t + \frac{1}{2}σ_{1}^{2}t^{2} + μ_{2}t + \frac{1}{2}σ_{2}^{2}t^{2}); = \exp ((μ_{1} + μ_{2})t + \frac{1}{2}(σ_{1}^{2} + σ_{2}^{2})t^{2})$.
This is the moment-generating function of a normal random variable with distribution $N(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$. Therefore, by the uniqueness property, we conclude
$Y\sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

- **Sums of Chi-Square Random Variables**:
Suppose $X_1\sim \chi^2(k_1)$ and $X_2\sim \chi^2(k_2)$ are independent. Then
$Y = X_1+X_2\sim \chi^2(k_1+k_2)$.
To prove this, let $M_{X_1}(t)$ and $M_{X_2}(t)$ be the moment-generating functions of $X_1$ and $X_2$ respectively. Then, we have
$M_{X_1}(t) = (1-2t)^{-k_1/2}, \qquad M_{X_2}(t) = (1-2t)^{-k_2/2}$.
By the multiplicative property of moment-generating functions, we have that
$M_{Y}(t)|= M_{X_{1} + X_{2}}(t); = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t); = (1 - 2t)^{-k_{1}/2} \cdot (1 - 2t)^{-k_{2}/2}; = (1 - 2t)^{- (k_{1} + k_{2})/2}$.
This is the moment-generating function of a chi-square random variable with distribution $\chi^2(k_1+k_2)$. Therefore, by the uniqueness property, we conclude
$Y\sim \chi^2(k_1+k_2)$.
