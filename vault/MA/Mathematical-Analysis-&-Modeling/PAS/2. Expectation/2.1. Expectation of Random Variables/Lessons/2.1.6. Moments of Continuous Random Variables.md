## Introduction

Suppose $X$ is a continuous random variable with probability density function $f(x)$ defined over a set $S$.

The values $\textrm{E}[X]$ and $\textrm{E}[X^2]$ are called the **first moment** and **second moment** of $X$, respectively.

Recall that the first moment $\textrm{E}[X]$ is also called the *expected value* of $X$ and is computed as follows:

$$
\displaystyle \textrm{E}[X] = \int_S x \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

To find second moment $\textrm{E}[X^2]$, the expected value of the the random variable $X^2$, we simply substitute $x^2$ for $x$ in the formula above, as follows:

$$
\displaystyle \textrm{E}[X^2] = \int_S x^2 \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

Let's compute the second moment $\textrm{E}[X^2]$ of the continuous random variable $X$ with the probability density function

$$
f(x) = \dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}, \qquad 0 \leq x \leq 1
$$

We compute the expected value of $X^2$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= ∫_{S}x^{2} \cdot f(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}dx \\
&= \frac{3}{2}∫_{0}^{1}x^{5/2}dx \\
&= \frac{3}{2}(\frac{2}{7}x^{7/2}) \mid _{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{3}{2}(\frac{2}{7} - 0) \\
&= \frac{3}{7}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-second-moment-of-a-random-variable"></a>
## Calculating the Second Moment of a Random Variable

**Example:** A continuous random variable $X$ has the probability density function
${\frac{1}{8},\begin{vmatrix}-2 < x < 0, \\ \frac{3}{2}x,\end{vmatrix}0 \le x < 1$.

Calculate $\textrm{E}[X^2]$.

**Explanation**

For a continuous random variable $X$ with probability density function $f(x)$ defined over a set $S$, the expected value of $X^2$ is given by

$$
\textrm{E}[X^2] = \int_{S} x^2 \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

So, we compute the expected value of $X^2$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= ∫_{-2}^{1}x^{2} \cdot f(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{0}x^{2} \cdot \frac{1}{8}dx + ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2}xdx \\
&= \frac{1}{8}∫_{-2}^{0}x^{2}dx + \frac{3}{2}∫_{0}^{1}x^{3}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{24}x^{3} \mid _{-2}^{0} + \frac{3}{8}x^{4} \mid _{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{24}(0 + 8) + \frac{3}{8}(1 - 0) \\
&= \frac{1}{3} + \frac{3}{8} \\
&= \frac{17}{24}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="higher-moments"></a>
## Higher Moments

It's also possible to define higher moments. For example, $\textrm{E}[X^3]$ is the **third moment** of the random variable $X$.

Notice the pattern:

- $\displaystyle \textrm{E}[X] = \int_S x \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x$ is the *first* moment
- $\displaystyle \textrm{E}[X^2] = \int_S x^2 \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x$ is the *second* moment
- $\displaystyle \textrm{E}[X^3] = \int_S x^3 \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x$ is the *third* moment

and so on. In general, the **$n$th moment** is given by

$$
\textrm{E}[X^n] = \int_S x^n \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

Finally, note that

$$
\textrm{E}[X], \quad \textrm{E}[X^2], \quad \textrm{E}[X^3], \quad \ldots
$$

are sometimes referred to as the first, second, and third **raw moments**. This is because it's possible to define other types of moments.

In general, $\textrm{E}[X^n]$ can be called either the $n$th *moment* or $n$th *raw moment*.

<a id="calculating-a-higher-moments-of-a-random-variable"></a>
## Calculating a Higher Moments of a Random Variable

**Example:** A continuous random variable $X$ has the probability density function
${1 - x,\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 1 \\ \frac{1}{2},\end{vmatrix}1 < x \le 2$.

Calculate $\textrm E [X^4]$.

**Explanation**

For a continuous random variable $X$ with probability density function $f(x)$ defined over a set $S$, the $n$th raw moment of $X$ is given by

$$
\textrm{E}[X^n] = \int_{S} x^n \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

So, we compute the fourth raw moment of $X$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{4}] &= ∫_{0}^{2}x^{4} \cdot f(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{4} \cdot (1 - x)dx + ∫_{1}^{2}x^{4} \cdot \frac{1}{2}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(x^{4} - x^{5})dx + \frac{1}{2}∫_{1}^{2}x^{4}dx \\
&= (\frac{1}{5}x^{5} - \frac{1}{6}x^{6}) \mid _{0}^{1} + \frac{1}{10}x^{5} \mid _{1}^{2} \\
&= (\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{6} - 0) + \frac{1}{10}(32 - 1) \\
&= \frac{1}{30} + \frac{31}{10} \\
&= \frac{47}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-the-mean-of-a-transformed-random-variable"></a>
## Computing the Mean of a Transformed Random Variable

**Example:** The continuous random variable $X$ has the following probability density function:
$f(x) = 2x, \quad 0 \leq x \leq 1$

Given that $\textrm{E}[X] = \dfrac{2}{3}$, compute $\textrm{E}[5X^2 - 2X]$.

*Hint: You may assume that $\textrm{E}[aX + bY] = a\textrm{E}[X] + b\textrm{E}[Y]$ for any random variables $X$ and $Y$ and constants $a$ and $b$.*

**Explanation**

First, we simplify using the hint and the properties of expected value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[5X^{2} - 2X] &= 5E[X^{2}] - 2E[X]
\end{aligned}
$$

For a continuous random variable $X$ with probability density function $f(x)$ defined over a set $S$, the $n$th raw moment of $X$ is given by

$$
\textrm{E}[X^n] = \int_S x^n \cdot f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

We already know $\textrm E [X]$. So, we compute the second raw moment of $X$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= ∫_{0}^{1}x^{2} \cdot 2xdx \\
&= 2∫_{0}^{1}x^{3}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}x^{4} \mid _{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(1 - 0) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting

$$
\textrm{E}[X] = \dfrac{2}{3}
$$

and

$$
\textrm{E}[X^2] = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

into the simplified expression, we conclude that

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[5X^{2} - 2X] &= 5E[X^{2}] - 2E[X] \\
&= 5 \cdot \frac{1}{2} - 2 \cdot \frac{2}{3} \\
&= \frac{5}{2} - \frac{4}{3} \\
&= \frac{7}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$
