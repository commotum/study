## Introduction

When conducting statistical research, it's often helpful to establish a lower bound for the sample size that's needed to achieve a margin of error that's smaller than some pre-specified amount. This allows those conducting the sample to achieve results whose errors are constrained and at the minimum level of time and cost.

For example, suppose you wish to conduct a random sample to estimate the proportion of voters in a particular district who plan to vote for a certain candidate in an upcoming election. You'd need to determine how many people to survey to ensure reliable results. A sample size that is too small might lead to misleading conclusions, while a sample that is too large wastes resources. This lesson explores how to determine the minimum sample size needed to strike the right balance.

Let's discuss how to determine a lower bound for the sample size required to find a confidence interval with a fixed margin of error for a population proportion $p$.

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, we know that the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

Suppose that we require the margin of error to be at most $\varepsilon$, i.e.,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \leq \varepsilon
$$

By solving this inequality for $n$, we obtain

$$
n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

We now have two possible cases:

- Case 1: We have an estimate for $p$.
- Case 2: We do *not* have an estimate for $p$.

Let's discuss each case separately.

---

<a id="cases-when-an-estimate-is-given"></a>
## Cases When an Estimate Is Given

The first case is when we have an estimate for $p$.

Consider a population where it is known that approximately $80\%$ of individuals have a particular characteristic. Suppose we want the smallest sample size $n$ required to ensure that the margin of error for a $95\%$ confidence interval is at most $0.03$.

The sample size must satisfy

$$
n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

Substituting the values

$$
\widehat{\,p}=80\%, \qquad z_{\alpha/2}=1.96, \qquad \varepsilon=0.03
$$

into our inequality for $n$, we get

$$
n \mid \ge ((1.96)^{2}0.8(1 - 0.8))/((0.03)^{2}); \approx 682.95
$$

Since $n$ must be a positive integer, we conclude that the sample size must be at least $n= 683$.

Note that this value of $n$ satisfies the usual constraints on $n$ and $p{:}$

- $np = 683\cdot 0.8\approx 546 > 5$
- $n(1-p) = 683\cdot 0.2\approx 137 > 5$

<a id="finding-the-minimum-sample-size-when-a-point-estimate-is-given"></a>
## Finding the Minimum Sample Size When a Point Estimate Is Given

**Example:** A company wants to estimate the proportion of customers who prefer their new product over a competing product. The company believes that approximately $84\%$ of customers prefer their product. Find a lower bound on the sample size $n$ required to ensure that the margin of error for the $95\%$ confidence interval for the proportion of satisfied customers is at most $0.06$.

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 1.96) = 0.025$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$.*

**Explanation**

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

If we require the margin of error to be at most $\varepsilon$, then

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \leq \varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

We are interested in finding a $95\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.95=0.05 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.025
$$

We are given that

$$
P(Z > 1.96) = 0.025
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = 1.96
$$

We're given the estimate

$$
\widehat{\,p} = 84\% = 0.84
$$

Therefore, substituting

$$
\varepsilon=0.06, \qquad \widehat{\,p}=0.84, \qquad z_{\alpha/2}=1.96
$$

into the inequality for $n$, we obtain

$$
n \mid \ge (z_{α/2}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε^{2}); = ((1.96)^{2}0.84(1 - 0.84))/((0.06)^{2}); = 143.42
$$

Finally, rounding up to the nearest integer, we get that

$$
n=\bbox[3pt, Gainsboro]{\color{blue}144}
$$

or larger.

<a id="cases-when-an-estimate-is-not-given"></a>
## Cases When an Estimate Is Not Given

Consider a population where some proportion $p$ of the population has a particular characteristic. Suppose we do *not* have an estimate for $p$, yet we want the smallest sample size $n$ required to ensure that the margin of error for a $95\%$ confidence interval is at most $0.03$. Is this even possible without an estimate for $p$?

As we saw previously, the minimum sample size is given by

$$
n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

We do not know $\widehat{\,p}$, and consequently, we do not know $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ either. However, to find a lower bound for $n$, we can simply replace the expression $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ with its maximum value for $\widehat{\,p}\in [0,1]$.

A plot of the function $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ for different values of $\widehat{\,p}$ is given below.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Estimating Sample Sizes for Proportions/Images/818b5191ad19e109ed1a84e383122009.png>)

From the diagram, we see that the maximum value of $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ occurs when

$$
\widehat{\,p} = \dfrac12
$$

Therefore, the maximum value of $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ is

$$
\dfrac12\cdot \left(1-\dfrac12\right) = \dfrac14
$$

Substituting

$$
\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p}) = \dfrac14, \qquad z_{\alpha/2}=1.96, \qquad \varepsilon=0.03
$$

into our inequality for $n$, we finally arrive at

$$
n \mid \ge (z_{α/2}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε^{2}); = ((1.96)^{2})/((0.03)^{2}) \cdot \frac{1}{4}; \approx 1067.11
$$

Therefore, we conclude that the sample size must be at least $n= 1068$.

Finally, note that when $\widehat{\,p}$ is unknown, we often require a significantly larger sample size to ensure the same margin of error compared to the case where $\widehat{\,p}$ is known.

## Finding the Minimum Sample Size When a Point Estimate Is Not Given

**Example:** The financial manager of a company wants to estimate the percentage of accounts receivable that are over one month overdue. Find a lower bound for sample size $n$ required to ensure that the margin of error for the $99\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion of accounts receivable that are over one month overdue is at most $0.08$.

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 2.576) = 0.005$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$.*

**Explanation**

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

If we require the margin of error to be at most $E$, then

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \leq E \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{E^2}
$$

We do not know $\widehat{\,p}$, and consequently, we do not know $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ either. However, to find a lower bound for $n$, we can simply replace the expression $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ with its maximum value for $\widehat{\,p}\in [0,1]$.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Estimating Sample Sizes for Proportions/Images/922ecb514d5cfa0e1eb83701371579d1.png>)

From the diagram, we see that the maximum value of $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ occurs when

$$
\widehat{\,p} = \dfrac12
$$

and therefore, the maximum value of $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ is

$$
\dfrac12\cdot \left(1-\dfrac12\right) = \dfrac14
$$

So, we have

$$
n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2}{4E^2}
$$

We are interested in finding a $99\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.99=0.01 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.005
$$

We are given that

$$
P(Z > 2.576) = 0.005
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.005} = 2.576
$$

Therefore, substituting

$$
E=0.08, \qquad z_{\alpha/2}=2.576
$$

into the inequality for $n$, we obtain

$$
n \mid \ge \frac{z_{α/2}^{2}}{4E^{2}}; = ((2.576)^{2})/(4(0.08)^{2}); \approx 259.21
$$

Finally, rounding up to the nearest integer, we get that $n=260$ or larger.

## Finding the Minimum Sample Size When an Interval Estimate Is Given

**Example:** A pizzeria estimates that the number of orders delivered within $30$ minutes is between $60\%$ and $72\%$. The restaurant's management decides that they want a more precise estimate.
Find a lower bound for sample size $n$ required to ensure that the margin of error for the $98\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion of the pizzeria's orders is at most $0.04$.

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$.*

**Explanation**

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

If we require the margin of error to be at most $\varepsilon$, then

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \leq \varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n \geq \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

We are interested in finding a $98\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.98=0.02 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.01
$$

We are given that

$$
P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.01} = 2.326
$$

We have the estimate $p \in [0.6, 0.72]$. So, to find our lower bound for $n$, we replace $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ with the maximum of the function

$$
f(p) = p(1-p)
$$

for $p\in[0.6, 0.72]$.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Estimating Sample Sizes for Proportions/Images/0393ffad5ed721d4266192253afe66ab.png>)

Since $f$ decreases in the given interval, the maximum occurs at $p=0.6$. Therefore, we replace $\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})$ with

$$
0.6(1-0.6)=0.24
$$

Finally, substituting

$$
\varepsilon=0.04, \qquad \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})=0.24, \qquad z_{\alpha/2}=2.326
$$

into the inequality for $n$, we obtain

$$
n \mid \ge (z_{α/2}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε^{2}); = ((2.326)^{2}0.24)/((0.04)^{2}); = 811.54
$$

Rounding up to the nearest integer, we get that $n=812$ or larger. Therefore,

$$
n_{\textrm{min}} = 812
$$

## Factors Affecting Sample Size

Suppose we conduct a random sample to find the proportion of voters who intend to vote for a particular candidate in an upcoming election. Let's assume we initially used a sample of size $n_1$ to construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for the population mean $\mu$.

After completing the initial survey, it's decided that a new sample must be carried out, and the resulting confidence interval should have the following features:

- The confidence level of the new confidence interval should be $95\%$ (unchanged).
- The new confidence interval should be one-half as wide as the original (increased precision).

To increase the precision while keeping the confidence level fixed, we'll need to *increase* the sample size. But by how much? Let's determine how much larger the second sample size $n_2$ should be compared to the first to satisfy the new conditions.

First, recall that, given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

Given a particular margin of error $\varepsilon$, we can deduce an approximate sample size:

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} = \varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n = \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

Let $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ be the margins of error of the confidence intervals $I_1$ and $I_2$, respectively. Since we want $I_2$ to half the length of $I_1$, we must have

$$
\varepsilon_1 = 2\varepsilon_2
$$

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{1} &= (z_{α/2}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε_{1}^{2}), \\
n_{2} &= (z_{α/2}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε_{2}^{2}), \\
\frac{n_{1}}{n_{2}} &= \frac{ε_{2}^{2}}{ε_{1}^{2}} \\
&= (\frac{ε_{2}}{ε_{1}})^{2} \\
&= (\frac{1}{2})^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives

$$
4 n_1 = n_2
$$

Therefore, $n_2$ must be $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\text{larger}}$ then $n_1$ by a factor of approximately $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}4}$.

## Understanding the Minimum Sample Size Formula

**Example:** Consider a population where some individuals have a particular characteristic. Let $I_1$ be a $90\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ of individuals having this characteristic obtained from a sample of size $n_1$.

Suppose we require a $99\%$ confidence interval $I_2$ to have approximately the same length as $I_1$. Fill in the missing information to find the sample size $n_2$ we should use to get $I_2$. You may assume that the point estimate $\widehat p$ is the same for the two samples.

You may use the following percentage points table for the normal distribution.

| $\quad z \quad$ | $0.95$ | $0.975$ | $0.98$ | $0.99$ | $0.995$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\Phi^{-1}(p)$ | $\,1.645\,$ | $\,1.960\,$ | $\,2.054\,$ | $\,2.326\,$ | $\,2.576\,$ |

**Explanation**

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm \overbrace{z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}}^{\large \textrm{margin of error}}
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion,
- $n$ is the sample size,
- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

Given a particular margin or error $\varepsilon$, we can deduce an approximate sample size:

$$
z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} = \varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n = \dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{\varepsilon^2}
$$

Let $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ be the margins of error of $I_1$ and $I_2$, respectively. Since we want $I_2$ to have the same length as $I_1$, then

$$
\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon_2
$$

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{1} &= (z_{0.05}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε_{1}^{2}), \\
n_{2} &= (z_{0.005}^{2}p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(ε_{2}^{2}), \\
\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}} &= \frac{z_{0.005}^{2}}{z_{0.05}^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

From the table, we have that

$$
z_{0.05} = 1.645
$$

and

$$
z_{0.005}=2.576
$$

Therefore

$$
\dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = \dfrac{2.576^2}{1.645^2} \approx 2.5
$$

which gives

$$
n_2\approx 2.5 n_1
$$

Finally, $n_2$ must be $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\text{larger}}$ than $n_1$ by a factor of approximately $2.5$.
