## Introduction

Suppose an insurance company offers two different products and wants to determine whether the proportion of satisfied customers on each plan differs. One way to do this is to take a random sample of customers on each plan, find each plan's proportion of satisfied customers, and then calculate a confidence interval for the difference between the two proportions.

In this lesson, we'll learn how to construct a confidence for the difference between two population proportions.

Suppose we have two populations: population $1$ and population $2$. Let the proportion of individuals in the first and second populations with a particular characteristic be $p_1$ and $p_2$, respectively. Independent random samples of size $n_1$ and $n_2$ are drawn from each population.

If the sample sizes are sufficiently large such that

$$
n_i \cdot \widehat{\,p}_i > 5, \qquad n_i\cdot (1-\widehat{\,p}_i)>5, \qquad i=1,2
$$

then, we know that the sampling distributions of our estimates $\widehat{\,p}_1$ and $\widehat{\,p}_2$ of $p_1$ and $p_2$ respectively are given by

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1 \sim N\left(p_1, \dfrac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1}\right), \qquad \widehat{\,p}_2 \sim N\left(p_2, \dfrac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}\right)
$$

Since the samples are independent, by the properties of expectation and variance, we have

$$
\textrm E [\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2] = p_1 - p_2, \qquad \textrm{Var} [\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2] = \dfrac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \dfrac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}
$$

Since $\widehat{\,p}_1$ and $\widehat{\,p}_2$ are normally distributed, their difference is also normal, and we have

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2 \sim N\left(p_1 - p_2, \dfrac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1} + \dfrac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}\right)
$$

In most practical situations, $p_1$ and $p_2$ are unknown. However, we can approximate the above distribution using estimates obtained from sample data. Thus, we can write

$$
Z= \dfrac{(\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2) - (p_1-p_2)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

Let's now look at how we can use this information to construct confidence intervals for differences in proportions.

<a id="confidence-intervals-for-the-difference-between-two-proportions"></a>
## Confidence Intervals for the Difference Between Two Proportions

Suppose we have two random samples of size $n_1$ and $n_2$ from two sufficiently large populations $p_1$ and $p_2$ with sample proportions $\widehat{\,p}_1$ and $\widehat{\,p}_2$, respectively. Let's also assume that the following conditions are satisfied:

- $n_1 \widehat{\,p}_1 > 5, \qquad n_1 (1-\widehat{\,p}_1) > 5$,
- $n_2 \widehat{\,p}_2 > 5, \qquad n_2 (1-\widehat{\,p}_2) > 5$.

Then, given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence limits for the population difference of proportions $p_1-p_2$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1-\widehat{\,p}_2 \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}
$$

where

- $P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}$ and $Z \sim N(0,1)$, and
- $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the corresponding $z$-score.

The quantity

$$
E =z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}
$$

is called the **margin of error.**

So, we have
$z$

and our confidence interval can be written as follows:

$$
(p^(^)_{1} - p^(^)_{2} - [margin of error], \mid p^(^)_{1} - p^(^)_{2} + [margin of error])
$$

Throughout this lesson, you may assume that if a population is "large", it is sufficiently larger than any sample drawn from it. We'll look at cases with small samples in future lessons.

<a id="finding-confidence-intervals-for-differences-in-proportions"></a>
## Finding Confidence Intervals for Differences in Proportions

**Example:** Consider a sample of size $n_1=75$ and a sample of size $n_2=62$ from two large populations where some individuals have a particular characteristic. Given that the samples are independent and the sample proportions of individuals having the characteristic in the two samples are, respectively, $\widehat{p}_1=0.52$ and $\widehat{p}_2=0.5$, find a $98\%$ confidence interval for the difference $p_1-p_2$ between the corresponding population proportions.

![](<../Source/7.2.4. Confidence Intervals for Two Proportions/Images/5f92d7f9384cd731df8b0a0c16931470.png>)

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$, as shown in the diagram above.*

**Explanation**

Since $n_1=75$, $n_2=62$,

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1=0.52
$$

and

$$
\widehat{\,p}_2=0.5
$$

we have that

- $n_1 \widehat{\,p}_1 = 75\cdot 0.52 = 39> 5$,
- $n_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1) = 75\cdot (1-0.52) = 36> 5$,
- $n_2 \widehat{\,p}_2 = 62\cdot 0.5 = 31> 5$,
- $n_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2) = 62\cdot (1-0.5) = 31> 5$.

As a result, we may use a normal approximation:

$$
\dfrac{(\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2) - (p_1-p_2)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}_1, \widehat{\,p}_2$ are the sample proportions,
- $p_1, p_2$ are the population proportions,
- $n_1, n_2$ are the sample sizes.

Then, given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population difference of proportions $p_1-p_2$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1-\widehat{\,p}_2 \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}
$$

where

$$
P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}
$$

and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

We are interested in finding a $98\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.98=0.02 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.01
$$

We are given that

$$
P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.01} = 2.326
$$

Therefore, a $98\%$ confidence interval for the difference of the population proportions $p_1-p_2$ is the following:

$$
(0.52 - 0.5) ± 2.326 \cdot \sqrt{(0.52(1 - 0.52))/(75) + (0.5(1 - 0.5))/(62)}
$$

which simplifies as

$$
0.02 ± 0.20
$$

Finally, the confidence interval is

$$
(0.02 - 0.20, \, 0.02 + 0.20) = \left(-0.18, 0.22\right)
$$

## Applications of Confidence Intervals

**Example:** The student council coordinator wants to estimate the difference in the proportion of eighth and ninth-grade students who vote in student elections. He conducts two simple random samples of students in his school. The summary statistics of the two samples are given in the table below.

|  | Eighth Grade | Ninth Grade |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Students | $62$ | $70$ |
| Students who vote in student elections | $48$ | $35$ |

Which of the following statements is true?

1. The end-points of the $98\%$ confidence interval for the difference between the population proportions are approximately $0.274 \pm 0.129$.
2. All values contained within the $98\%$ confidence interval are negative.
3. The confidence interval data suggests that $p_1 > p_2$.

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$. Assume that $p_1$ and $p_2$ correspond to the eighth and ninth-grade students, respectively.*

**Explanation**

Let us call $n_1, n_2$ the sample sizes of eighth and ninth-grade students, and $\widehat{\,p}_1, \widehat{\,p}_2$ the proportion of students who vote in student elections in the two samples.

Since $n_1=62$, $n_2=70$,

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1=\dfrac{48}{62}=\dfrac{24}{31}
$$

and

$$
\widehat{\,p}_2=\dfrac{35}{70} = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

we have that

- $n_1 \widehat{\,p}_1 = 48> 5$,
- $n_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1) = 62 - 48 = 14> 5$,
- $n_2 \widehat{\,p}_2 = 35> 5$,
- $n_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2) = 70- 35 = 35> 5$.

As a result, we may use a normal approximation:

$$
\dfrac{(\widehat{\,p}_1 - \widehat{\,p}_2) - (p_1-p_2)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}_1, \widehat{\,p}_2$ are the sample proportions,
- $p_1, p_2$ are the population proportions,
- $n_1, n_2$ are the sample sizes.

Then, given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population difference of proportions $p_1-p_2$ can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p}_1-\widehat{\,p}_2 \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_1(1-\widehat{\,p}_1)}{n_1}+\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}_2(1-\widehat{\,p}_2)}{n_2}}
$$

where

$$
P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}
$$

and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

We are interested in finding a $98\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.98=0.02 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.01
$$

We are given that that

$$
P(Z > 2.326) = 0.01
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.01} = 2.326
$$

Therefore, a $98\%$ confidence interval for the difference of the population proportions $p_1-p_2$ is the following:

$$
(\frac{24}{31} - \frac{1}{2}) ± 2.326 \cdot \sqrt{(\frac{48}{62}(1 - \frac{48}{62}))/(62) + (\frac{35}{70}(1 - \frac{35}{70}))/(70)}
$$

which simplifies as
$0.274 ± 0.186$.

Let's now examine our statements.

- Statement I is false. The end-points of our confidence interval are $0.274 \pm 0.186$.
- Statement II is false. All values within the confidence interval are positive:
$(0.274 - 0.186, \, 0.274 + 0.186) = (0.088, \, 0.460)$
- Statement III is true. Since all values within the confidence interval are positive, this suggests that the proportion of eighth-grade students who vote in student elections is greater than that of ninth-graders. In other words, $p_1 > p_2$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."
