## Introduction

Let's now discuss how to construct a confidence interval for the proportion of the population whose members have a particular characteristic.

As a concrete example, let $p$ represent the proportion of the United States voting population that will vote for candidate $A$ in the next presidential election.

Suppose we conduct a random sample of size $n=40$ from our population. After processing our results, we find that the proportion $\widehat{\,p}$ of sample members that plan to vote for candidate $A$ is given by

$$
\widehat{\,p} = 0.3
$$

This is our *estimate* of the population proportion $p$.

Of course, the quantity that we're *really* interested in is $p$, the proportion of the entire voting population that will vote for candidate $A$. We want to get an idea of how accurate our estimate is. Therefore, we wish to construct a confidence interval of the form

$$
(\widehat{\,p}-E, \: \widehat{\,p}+E)
$$

where $E$ is some **margin of error.**

Let's now discuss how to construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$.

---

<a id="constructing-confidence-intervals"></a>
## Constructing Confidence Intervals

We have

$$
n=40, \widehat{\,p} = 0.3
$$

and we wish to construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$.

To construct our confidence interval, the first thing to do is to establish the sampling distribution of $\widehat{\,p}$.

Since the United States has around $240$ million registered voters, the sample size $n=40$ is clearly less than $10\%$ of the population size. This means that, provided certain conditions are met, the following approximation can be applied:

$$
\widehat{\,p} \sim N\left(p, \dfrac{p(1-p)}{n} \right)
$$

Let's check that our usual conditions hold in this case (where we use $\widehat{\,p}$ as an estimate for $p$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
np^(^) &= 40 \cdot 0.3 \\
&= 12 \\
&> 5✓ \\
n(1 - p^(^)) &= 40 \cdot (1 - 0.3) \\
&= 28 \\
&> 5✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Since all three conditions hold, the above approximation is applicable.

By transforming $\widehat{\,p}$ to a standard normal random variable, we have

$$
Z= \dfrac{\widehat{\,p} - p}{\sqrt{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})/n}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

We wish to find a $z$-interval that we're $95\%$ confident that the random variable $Z$ lies within. This interval is indicated in the diagram below:

![](<../Source/7.1.4. Confidence Intervals for One Proportion/Images/ef967288e591c11ee1cd7f20f6d79eaf.png>)

To help with the notation, we define a new parameter $\alpha$. Since we're computing a $95\%$ confidence interval, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.95=0.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.025
$$

Notice that

$$
\dfrac{\alpha}{2}
$$

is precisely the area bounded by each "tail" that we're excluding from our confidence interval. Let's label the critical values at the endpoints of our interval as $\pm z_{\alpha/2}$:

![](<../Source/7.1.4. Confidence Intervals for One Proportion/Images/80c46885c5c97f5627cbd2e2adb6577b.png>)

According to our diagram,

$$
P(Z > z_{0.025}) = P(Z < -z_{0.025}) = 0.025
$$

Using a percentage points table for the standard normal distribution, we find that

$$
z_{0.025} \approx 1.96
$$

Therefore,

$$
P(Z > 1.96) = P(Z < -1.96) = 0.025
$$

In other words,

$$
P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.95
$$

Therefore, there is a $95\%$ probability that our random variable $Z$ will lie in the interval $(-1.96, 1.96)$.

Now, here's the trick. We solve the inequality inside the parentheses of the last probability statement for the population proportion $p$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-1.96 & < Z < 1.96 \\ -1.96 & < (p^(^) - p)/(\sqrt{p^(^)(1 - p^(^))/n}) < 1.96 \\ -1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} & < p^(^) - p < 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} \\ -p^(^) - 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} & < - p < - p^(^) + 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} \\ p^(^) + 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} & > p > p^(^) - 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} \\ p^(^) - 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)} & < p < p^(^) + 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, a $95\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion ${p}$ is given by

$$
\left(\widehat{\,p} -1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}},\: \widehat{\,p} +1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \right)
$$

Finally, substituting our values

$$
n=40, \qquad \widehat{\,p}=0.3
$$

we obtain that our $95\%$ confidence interval for ${p}$ is

$$
\big(0.3 - 0.142, \: 0.3 + 0.142 \big)
$$

which simplifies to

$$
\big(0.158, \: 0.442 \big)
$$

Now that we've seen how to construct a particular confidence interval, let's discuss the more general procedure.

---

<a id="confidence-intervals-for-proportions"></a>
## Confidence Intervals for Proportions

Suppose we have a random sample of size $n$ from a sufficiently large population with sample proportion $\widehat{\,p}$. Let's also assume that the following conditions are satisfied:

- $n \widehat{\,p} > 5$
- $n (1-\widehat{\,p}) > 5$

Then, for a given value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, a $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ is given by

$$
\left(\widehat{\,p} - z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}},\: \widehat{\,p} + z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}} \right)
$$

The endpoints of our confidence interval are the **confidence limits** and are given by

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}
$$

Each part of the formula above has a name:

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion
- $p$ is the population proportion
- $n$ is the sample size
- $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the corresponding $z$-score
- $E = z_{\alpha/2}\cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}$ is the margin of error

$$
z
$$

So, our confidence interval can be written as follows:

$$
(p^(^) - [margin of error],\begin{vmatrix}p^(^) + [margin of error]) \\ (p^(^) - [[math]z[/math]-score] \cdot [standard error], \mid p^(^) + [[math]z[/math]-score] \cdot [standard error]) \\ (p^(^) - z_{α/2} \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)},\end{vmatrix}p^(^) + z_{α/2} \cdot \sqrt{(p^(^)(1 - p^(^)))/(n)})
$$

Since computing a confidence interval amounts to finding the corresponding confidence limits, we will use the two terms interchangeably.

Finally, in the future, you may assume that if a population is "large," it is sufficiently larger than any sample drawn from it.

<a id="finding-confidence-intervals-for-population-proportions"></a>
## Finding Confidence Intervals for Population Proportions

**Example:** Consider a sample of size $n=100$ from a large population where some individuals have a particular characteristic. Given that the proportion of those in the sample with the characteristic is $\widehat{\,p}=0.64$, find a $90\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ of individuals having this characteristic.

![](<../Source/7.1.4. Confidence Intervals for One Proportion/Images/0ce9e4b15b73a3280cbe84f5627eadf1.png>)

*Hint: Use the fact that $P(Z > 1.645) = 0.05$, where $Z \sim N(0,1)$, as shown in the diagram above.*

**Explanation**

Since $n=100$ and

$$
\widehat{\,p}=0.64
$$

we have that

- $n \widehat{\,p} = 100 \cdot 0.64 = 64 > 5$, and
- $n(1-\widehat{\,p}) = 100 \cdot (1-0.64) = 36 > 5$.

As a result, we may use the following normal approximation:

$$
\dfrac{\widehat{\,p} - p}{\sqrt{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})/n}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ of the original distribution can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}
$$

where

$$
P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}
$$

and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

We are interested in finding a $90\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.9=0.1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.05
$$

We are given that

$$
P(Z > 1.645) = 0.05
$$

As a result,

$$
z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.05} = 1.645
$$

Therefore, a $90\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ is the following:

$$
0.64 ± 1.645 \cdot \sqrt{(0.64(1 - 0.64))/(100)}; 0.64 ± 0.079
$$

<a id="finding-confidence-intervals-for-population-proportions-in-context"></a>
## Finding Confidence Intervals for Population Proportions In Context

**Example:** A survey of a random sample of $1\,500$ U.S. households reveals that $780$ of them own at least one dog. Find a $95\%$ confidence interval for the proportion of all U.S. households that own at least one dog.

*Hint: Given a random variable $Z \sim N(0,1)$, the table below shows the $z$-scores $z_p$ such that $P(Z > z_p) = p$ for some particular values of $p$.*

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $1.645$ | $1.960$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

**Explanation**

First, let's interpret the data:

- the population consists of all U.S. households,
- some of the households surveyed own at least one dog, and some don't,
- the size of the random sample is $n=1\, 500$, and
- the proportion of households in the sample that own at least one dog can be found as follows:

$$
\widehat{p\,} = \dfrac{780}{1\, 500} = 0.52
$$

Now, we have that

- $n \widehat{\,p} =1\,500 \cdot 0.52 = 780 > 5$, and
- $n(1-\widehat{\,p}) = 1\,500 \cdot (1-0.52) = 720 > 5$.

As a result, we may use the following normal approximation:

$$
\dfrac{\widehat{\,p} - p}{\sqrt{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})/n}\phantom{\mid}} \sim N(0,1)
$$

where

- $\!\widehat{\,p}$ is the sample proportion,
- $p$ is the population proportion, and
- $n$ is the sample size.

Given a value $\alpha$ between $0$ and $1$, the corresponding $[100(1-\alpha)]\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ of the original distribution can be written as

$$
\widehat{\,p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{\widehat{\,p}(1-\widehat{\,p})}{n}}
$$

where

$$
P(Z > z_{\alpha/2}) = \dfrac{\alpha}{2}
$$

and $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

We are interested in finding a $95\%$ confidence interval. So, we have

$$
\alpha=1-0.95=0.05 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{\alpha}{2}=0.025
$$

We also need to find the $z$-score value $z_{0.025}$ such that

$$
P(Z > z_{0.025}) = 0.025
$$

where $Z \sim N(0,1)$.

![](<../Source/7.1.4. Confidence Intervals for One Proportion/Images/9e4777f0098dba7b34b5ac1eff2aa889.png>)

From the percentage points table of the normal distribution, we obtain that

$$
z_{0.025}=1.96{:}
$$

| $p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}0.025}$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $z_p$ | $1.282$ | $1.645$ | $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}1.960}$ | $2.326$ | $2.576$ |

Therefore, a $95\%$ confidence interval for the population proportion $p$ is the following:

$$
0.52 ± 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{(0.52(1 - 0.52))/(1500)}; 0.52 ± 0.025
$$
