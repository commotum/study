## Introduction

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two random variables with covariance $\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]$ and standard deviations $\textrm {SD}[X]$ and $\textrm {SD}[Y]$.

The **correlation coefficient of $X$ and $Y$,** also known as the **Pearson correlation coefficient**, is denoted $\rho$ (the Greek letter "rho") and is defined as

$$
\rho = \dfrac{\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]}{\textrm {SD}[X]\cdot \textrm {SD}[Y]}
$$

Note the following:

- The correlation coefficient satisfies
$-1\leq \rho \leq 1$.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent random variables, then
$\rho = 0$.
The reverse implication does not always hold. In other words, $\rho=0$ does *not* imply that $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
- Unlike the covariance, $\rho$ is a *dimensionless* quantity. This means we'll get the same answer regardless of the units used to measure $X$ and $Y$.
- Also unlike the covariance, $\rho$ tells the strength of the correlation between $X$ and $Y$. We typically have the following rule of thumb:If $\mid \rho \mid \gt 0.7$, the correlation is **strong,** and the data generated by our two random variables closely follow a straight line.![](<../Source/5.5.4. The Correlation Coefficient for Two Random Variables/Images/f0270db56b4f5649417e8ee6324fd37b.png>)If $0.3\leq \mid \rho \mid \leq 0.7$, the correlation is **weak.** The data generated by our random variables follow a straight line, but many are far from the line.![](<../Source/5.5.4. The Correlation Coefficient for Two Random Variables/Images/7eb82da0f2bdb708e68dbb8ed6c4c61f.png>)If $\mid \rho \mid \lt 0.3$, then the correlation is **negligible.** No (linear) correlation exists between the data points generated by the random variables.![](<../Source/5.5.4. The Correlation Coefficient for Two Random Variables/Images/cfe3ae895a0004fb5edf6dda9b728fec.png>)

Let's get some practice computing the correlation coefficient.

## Finding a Correlation Coefficient Given Variances and Covariance

**Example:** The variances of the random variables $X$ and $Y$ are $25$ and $36$, respectively. Given that $\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = 18$, find the correlation coefficient of $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

Recall that the correlation coefficient of the random variables $X$ and $Y$ is defined as

$$
\rho = \dfrac{\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]}{\textrm {SD}[X] \cdot \textrm {SD}[Y]}
$$

where

- $\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]$ is the covariance, and
- $\textrm {SD}[X]$ and $\textrm {SD}[Y]$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$.

In our case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
SD[X] &= \sqrt{Var[X]} = \sqrt{25} = 5, \\
SD[X] &= \sqrt{Var[Y]} = \sqrt{36} = 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting our data in the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
ρ &= \frac{Cov[X, Y]}{SD[X] \cdot SD[X]} \\
&= \frac{18}{5 \cdot 6} \\
&= \frac{3}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Correlation Coefficient of Two Discrete Random Variables

**Example:** The joint probability mass function $f(x,y)$ for the discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is shown above. Given that
$\textrm {E}[X]=0.4\qquad \textrm {E}[Y]=0.6, \qquad \textrm {SD}[X] \approx 0.4899, \qquad \textrm {SD}[Y] \approx 0.4899$,
find the correlation coefficient of $X$ and $Y$. Round your answer to $2$ decimal places.

| $f(x,y)$ | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ | $f_X$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $X=0$ | $0.1$ | $0.5$ | $0.6$ |
| $X=1$ | $0.3$ | $0.1$ | $0.4$ |
| $f_Y$ | $0.4$ | $0.6$ | $1$ |

**Explanation**

Recall that the correlation coefficient of random variables $X$ and $Y$ is defined as

$$
\rho = \dfrac{\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]}{\textrm {SD}[X] \cdot \textrm {SD}[Y]}
$$

where

- $\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]$ is the covariance, and
- $\textrm {SD}[X]$ and $\textrm {SD}[Y]$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$.

First, by applying the rule of the lazy statistician, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[XY] &= ∑_(x ∈ S_{X})∑_(y ∈ S_{Y})xyf(x, y) \\
&= 0 \cdot 0 \cdot 0.1 + 0 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.5 \\
&= + 1 \cdot 0 \cdot 0.3 + 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.1 \\
&= 0.1
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we compute the covariance and get

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= 0.1 - (0.4)(0.6) \\
&=-0.14
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the correlation coefficient is

$$
\begin{aligned}
ρ &= \frac{Cov[X, Y]}{SD[X] \cdot SD[Y]} \\
&\approx \frac{-0.14}{0.4899 \cdot 0.4899} \\
&\approx - 0.58
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to $2$ decimal places.

## Finding the Correlation Coefficient of Two Continuous Random Variables

**Example:** ${2(x + y),\begin{vmatrix}0 \le y \le x \le 1 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$

The joint probability density function $f(x,y)$ for the continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ is shown above. Given that
$\textrm {E}[X]= \dfrac{3}{4},\qquad \textrm {E}[Y]= \dfrac{5}{12}, \qquad \textrm {SD}[X]\approx 0.1936, \qquad \textrm {SD}[Y]\approx 0.2444$,
find the correlation coefficient of $X$ and $Y$. Round your answer to $2$ decimal places.

**Explanation**

Recall that the correlation coefficient of the random variables $X$ and $Y$ is defined as

$$
\rho = \dfrac{\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]}{\textrm {SD}[X] \cdot \textrm {SD}[Y]}
$$

where

- $\textrm{Cov}[X, Y]$ is the covariance, and
- $\textrm {SD}[X]$ and $\textrm {SD}[Y]$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$.

First, by applying the rule of the lazy statistician, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[XY] &= ∬_{R^{2}}xyf(x, y)dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[∫_{0}^{x}xy \cdot 2(x + y)dy]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[∫_{0}^{x}(2x^{2}y + 2xy^{2})dy]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[x^{2}y^{2} + \frac{2}{3}xy^{3}]_{y = 0}^{y = x}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(x^{4} + \frac{2}{3}x^{4})dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}\frac{5}{3}x^{4}dx \\
&= [\frac{1}{3}x^{5}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we compute the covariance and get

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= \frac{1}{3} - (\frac{3}{4})(\frac{5}{12}) \\
&= \frac{1}{48}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the correlation coefficient is

$$
\begin{aligned}
ρ &= \frac{Cov[X, Y]}{SD[X] \cdot SD[Y]} \\
&\approx ((\frac{1}{48}))/((0.1936) \cdot (0.2444)) \\
&\approx 0.44
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to $2$ decimal places.
