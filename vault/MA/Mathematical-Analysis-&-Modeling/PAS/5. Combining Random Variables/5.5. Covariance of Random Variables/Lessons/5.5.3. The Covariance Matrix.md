## Introduction

For a set of random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, the **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$ is defined as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X_{1}] & Cov[X_{1}, X_{2}] & ⋯ & Cov[X_{1}, X_{n}] \\ Cov[X_{2}, X_{1}] & Var[X_{2}] & ⋯ & Cov[X_{2}, X_{n}] \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ Cov[X_{n}, X_{1}] & Cov[X_{n}, X_{2}] & ⋯ & Var[X_{n}]\end{bmatrix}
$$

Note the following:

- The $i$th diagonal element is $\Sigma_{ii} = \textrm{Var}[X_i]$.
- The element in the $i$th row and $j$th column for $i \neq j$ is $\Sigma_{ij} = \textrm{Cov}[X_i, X_j]$.

Now, since

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X_i,X_j] = \textrm{Cov}[X_j,X_i]
$$

for any pair of random variables $X_i, X_j$, we have the following theorem:

> *The covariance matrix is always symmetric. Moreover, it is a positive semi-definite matrix.*

## Determining Elements of a Covariance Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}4 & 2 & 0 \\ a & 5 & b \\ c & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$

The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ for some random variables $X_1, X_2$, and $X_3$ is given above. Find $a + b + c$.

**Explanation**

For random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X_{1}] & Cov[X_{1}, X_{2}] & ⋯ & Cov[X_{1}, X_{n}] \\ Cov[X_{2}, X_{1}] & Var[X_{2}] & ⋯ & Cov[X_{2}, X_{n}] \\ ⋮ & ⋮ & ⋱ & ⋮ \\ Cov[X_{n}, X_{1}] & Cov[X_{n}, X_{2}] & ⋯ & Var[X_{n}]\end{bmatrix}
$$

That is,

- the $i$th diagonal element is $\Sigma_{ii} = \textrm{Var}[X_i]$, and
- the element in the $i$th row and $j$th column for $i \neq j$ is $\Sigma_{ij} = \textrm{Cov}[X_i, X_j]$.

Now, note that

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{Cov}[Y,X]
$$

As a result, the covariance matrix is always symmetric (moreover, it is a positive semi-definite matrix).

So, we can find the missing elements as follows:

- $a = \Sigma_{21} = \textrm{Cov}[X_2,X_1] = \textrm{Cov}[X_1,X_2] = \Sigma_{12} = 2$
- $b = \Sigma_{23} = \textrm{Cov}[X_2,X_3] = \textrm{Cov}[X_3,X_2] = \Sigma_{32} = 1$
- $c = \Sigma_{31} = \textrm{Cov}[X_3,X_1] = \textrm{Cov}[X_1,X_3] = \Sigma_{13} = 0$

Therefore,

$$
a + b + c = 2 + 1 +0 = 3
$$

<a id="finding-a-covariance-matrix-given-some-moments"></a>
## Finding a Covariance Matrix Given Some Moments

**Example:** Consider two random variables $X$ and $Y$ with expectations $\textrm{E}[X] = 4$, $\textrm{E}[Y] = 3$, mixed moment $\textrm{E}[XY] = 9$, and second raw moments $\textrm{E}[X^2] = 18$ and $\textrm{E}[Y^2] = 14$. Find the covariance matrix for $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}
$$

Recall that the variance of a random variable $X$ is determined by the formula

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm{E}[X^2] - \textrm{E}[X]^2
$$

and the covariance of two random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given by the formula

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{E}[XY] - \textrm{E}[X] \cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

First, let's find $\textrm{Var}[X]{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[X] &= E[X^{2}] - E[X]^{2} \\
&= 18 - 4^{2} \\
&= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[Y] &= E[Y^{2}] - E[Y]^{2} \\
&= 14 - 3^{2} \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Now lets find $\textrm{Cov}[X,Y]{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= 9 - 4 \cdot 3 \\
&=-3
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the covariance matrix is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Finding a Covariance Matrix Given a Discrete Bivariate Distribution

**Example:** The joint probability mass function $f(x,y)$ for the discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given below.

| $f$ | $Y=1$ | $Y=3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $X=2$ | $0.3$ | $0.2$ |
| $X=4$ | $0.1$ | $0.4$ |

Given that $\textrm{E}[X] = 3$ and $\textrm{E}[Y] = 2.2$, find the covariance matrix for $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}$

Recall that the variance of a random variable $X$ is determined by the formula

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm{E}[X^2] - \textrm{E}[X]^2
$$

and the covariance of two random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given by the formula

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{E}[XY] - \textrm{E}[X] \cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

First, let's find the marginal mass functions for $X$ and $Y$. These are given by the row and column totals, respectively.

| $f$ | $Y=1$ | $Y=3$ | $f_X$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $X=2$ | $0.3$ | $0.2$ | $0.5$ |
| $X=4$ | $0.1$ | $0.4$ | $0.5$ |
| $f_Y$ | $0.4$ | $0.6$ | $1$ |

Now, we can find $\textrm{Var}[X]$. If $X$ is defined on a support $S_X$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= ∑_(x ∈ S_{X})x^{2} \cdot f_{X}(x) \\
&= 2^{2} \cdot 0.5 + 4^{2} \cdot 0.5 \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[X] &= E[X^{2}] - E[X]^{2} \\
&= 10 - 3^{2} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly, if $Y$ is defined on a support $S_Y$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[Y^{2}] &= ∑_(y ∈ S_{y})y^{2} \cdot f_{Y}(y) \\
&= 1^{2} \cdot 0.4 + 3^{2} \cdot 0.6 \\
&= 5.8
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[Y] &= E[Y^{2}] - E[Y]^{2} \\
&= 5.8 - 2.2^{2} \\
&= 0.96
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, let's find $\textrm{Cov}[X,Y]$. By the rule of the lazy statistician, we find $\textrm{E}[XY]{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[XY] &= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)xy \cdot f(x, y) \\
&= (2)(1)f(2, 1) + (4)(1)f(4, 1) + (2)(3)f(2, 3) + (4)(3)f(4, 3) \\
&= 2 \cdot 0.3 + 4 \cdot 0.1 + 6 \cdot 0.2 + 12 \cdot 0.4 \\
&= 7
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, using the covariance formula, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= 7 - 3 \cdot 2.2 \\
&= 0.4
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the covariance matrix is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}$

## Finding a Covariance Matrix Given a Continuous Bivariate Distribution

**Example:** The random variables $X$ and $Y$ have the joint probability density function

${x + \frac{3}{2}y^{2},\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$.

If $\textrm{E}[X] = \dfrac{7}{12}$, $\textrm{E}[Y] = \dfrac{5}{8}$, and $\textrm{E}[XY]=\dfrac{17}{48}$, find the covariance matrix for $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}
$$

Recall that the variance of a random variable $X$ is determined by the formula

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm{E}[X^2] - \textrm{E}[X]^2
$$

and the covariance of two random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given by the formula

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{E}[XY] - \textrm{E}[X] \cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

First, we find $\textrm{Var}[X]$.
We compute $\textrm{E}[X^2]$ using the rule of the lazy statistician:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}x^{2} \cdot f(x, y)dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}(x + \frac{3}{2}y^{2})dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}(x^{3} + \frac{3}{2}x^{2}y^{2})dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[\frac{1}{4}x^{4} + \frac{1}{2}x^{3}y^{2}]_{x = 0}^{x = 1}dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2}y^{2})dy \\
&= [\frac{1}{4}y + \frac{1}{6}y^{3}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} \\
&= \frac{5}{12}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[X] &= E[X^{2}] - E[X]^{2} \\
&= \frac{5}{12} - (\frac{7}{12})^{2} \\
&= \frac{11}{144}
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly, we compute $\textrm{E}[Y^2]$ using the rule of the lazy statistician:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[Y^{2}] &= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}y^{2} \cdot f(x, y)dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}y^{2}(x + \frac{3}{2}y^{2})dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}(xy^{2} + \frac{3}{2}y^{4})dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[\frac{1}{2}x^{2}y^{2} + \frac{3}{2}xy^{4}]_{x = 0}^{x = 1}dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(\frac{1}{2}y^{2} + \frac{3}{2}y^{4})dy \\
&= [\frac{1}{6}y^{3} + \frac{3}{10}y^{5}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{6} + \frac{3}{10} \\
&= \frac{7}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[Y] &= E[Y^{2}] - E[Y]^{2} \\
&= \frac{7}{15} - (\frac{5}{8})^{2} \\
&= \frac{73}{960}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, let's find $\textrm{Cov}[X,Y]$. Using the given results, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= \frac{17}{48} - (\frac{7}{12}) \cdot (\frac{5}{8}) \\
&=-\frac{1}{96}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the covariance matrix is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}Var[X] & Cov[X, Y] \\ Cov[Y, X] & Var[Y]\end{bmatrix}
$$
