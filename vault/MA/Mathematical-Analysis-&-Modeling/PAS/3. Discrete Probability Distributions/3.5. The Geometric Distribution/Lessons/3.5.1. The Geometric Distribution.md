## Introduction

A **Bernoulli trial** is a random experiment whose outcome can be interpreted as either "success" or "failure."

For example, suppose we throw a die and define a "success" as the event of getting a six and a "failure" as getting any other number. Then, the throw of a die can be thought of as a Bernoulli trial with the following probabilities:

$$
P(\textrm{six}) = \dfrac16, \qquad P(\textrm{not six}) = \dfrac56
$$

Now suppose that a fair die is repeatedly thrown *until* we get a six. What is the probability that we need to throw the die precisely ${\color{blue}{3}}$ times to get our first six?

First, notice that each throw of the die is a Bernoulli trial. And since we throw the die repeatedly until we get a six, we have a *sequence* of Bernoulli trials.

Let $X$ be the number of times the die is thrown until we get a six. We wish to compute

$$
P(X = {\color{blue}{3}})
$$

To get our first six on the *third* throw, we need to throw "not a six" on the *first two* throws, followed by a "six" on the *final* throw. Assuming that each throw of the die is independent, by the multiplication law for independent events, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X = 3) &= P(not six) \cdot P(not six) \cdot P(six) \\
&= \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{5}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \\
&= \frac{25}{216}
\end{aligned}
$$

The random variable $X$ in this example follows a so-called **geometric distribution**. Let's now discuss geometric distributions a little more generally.

---

<a id="the-probability-mass-function-of-a-geometric-distribution"></a>
## The Probability Mass Function of a Geometric Distribution

Suppose that we have a sequence of Bernoulli trials, each resulting in success or failure, and with a probability of success $p$. Let the random variable $X$ represent the number of Bernoulli trials *until the first success*.

It can be shown that $X$ follows a **geometric distribution** and has the following probability mass function:

$$
{(1 - p)^{x - 1}p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Let's break this formula down a bit:

- The factor $(1-p)^{x-1}$ represents the probability of getting exactly $(x-1)$ failures, each with probability $(1-p)$.
- The factor ${\color{purple}{p}}$ represents the probability of getting a single success.
- The condition $x=1,2,3,\ldots$ indicates that the sequence of Bernoulli trials terminates after a minimum of $1$ trial, and there is no maximum number of trials.

If a random variable $X$ follows a geometric distribution, we say it is a geometric random variable and write

$$
X\sim \textrm{Geom}(p)
$$

---

<a id="applying-the-probability-mass-function-of-a-geometric-distribution"></a>
## Applying the Probability Mass Function of a Geometric Distribution

Let $X$ be the number of times a fair die is thrown until we get a six. Since we have a sequence of Bernoulli trials, each with a probability of success

$$
p=\dfrac16
$$

the number of throws until the first six can be modeled as a geometric random variable, and we write

$$
X\sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac16\right)
$$

Therefore, the probability of getting our first six on the $\color{blue}3$rd roll is

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X = 3) &= f(3) \\
&= (1 - \frac{1}{6})^{3 - 1} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \\
&= (\frac{5}{6})^{2} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \\
&= \frac{25}{36} \cdot \frac{1}{6} \\
&= \frac{25}{216}
\end{aligned}
$$

which agrees with our previous result.

<a id="computing-a-probability-at-a-point"></a>
## Computing a Probability at a Point

**Example:** Given $X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)$, compute $P(X = 3)$.

**Explanation**

If $X \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$, then $X$ has the following probability mass function:

$$
{(1 - p)^{x - 1}p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Here,

$$
X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)
$$

so the distribution of $X$ in this case is

$$
{(\frac{1}{3})^{x - 1}(\frac{2}{3}),\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X = 3) &= f(3) \\
&= (\frac{1}{3})^{2}(\frac{2}{3}) \\
&= \frac{2}{27}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-a-probability-over-a-bounded-interval-lower-bound-is-zero"></a>
## Computing a Probability Over a Bounded Interval: Lower Bound is Zero

**Example:** Given $X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 37\right)$, compute $P(X < 5)$.

**Explanation**

If $X \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$, then $X$ has the following probability mass function:

$$
{(1 - p)^{x - 1}p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Here,

$$
X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 37\right)
$$

so the distribution of $X$ in this case is

$$
{(\frac{4}{7})^{x - 1}(\frac{3}{7}),\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X < 5) &= P(X ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}) \\
&= P(X = 1) + P(X = 2) + P(X = 3) + P(X = 4) \\
&= f(1) + f(2) + f(3) + f(4) \\
&= (\frac{4}{7})^{0}(\frac{3}{7}) + (\frac{4}{7})^{1}(\frac{3}{7}) + (\frac{4}{7})^{2}(\frac{3}{7}) + (\frac{4}{7})^{3}(\frac{3}{7}) \\
&\approx 0.893
\end{aligned}
$$

to $3$ decimal places.

## Computing a Probability Over a Bounded Interval: Lower Bound is Not Zero

**Example:** Given that $X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 15\right)$, compute $P(2 \leq X \leq 3)$.

**Explanation**

If $X \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$, then $X$ has the following probability mass function:

$$
{(1 - p)^{x - 1}p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Here,

$$
X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 15\right)
$$

so the distribution of $X$ in this case is

$$
{(\frac{4}{5})^{x - 1}(\frac{1}{5}),\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(2 \le X \le 3) &= P(X ∈ {2, 3}) \\
&= P(X = 2) + P(X = 3) \\
&= f(2) + f(3) \\
&= (\frac{4}{5})^{1}(\frac{1}{5}) + (\frac{4}{5})^{2}(\frac{1}{5}) \\
&= \frac{36}{125}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-a-probability-over-a-bounded-interval-using-the-complement"></a>
## Computing a Probability Over a Bounded Interval Using the Complement

**Example:** Given $X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 56\right)$, compute $P(X > 2)$.

**Explanation**

If $X \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$, then $X$ has the following probability mass function:

$$
{(1 - p)^{x - 1}p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3, … \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Here,

$$
X \sim \textrm{Geom}\left(\dfrac 56\right)
$$

so the distribution of $X$ in this case is

$$
{(\frac{1}{6})^{x - 1}(\frac{5}{6}),\begin{vmatrix}x = 1, 2, 3… \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

There are infinitely many values of $X$ such that $X > 2$. However, we can simplify the computation by using the complement instead:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X > 2) &= 1 - P(X \le 2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Computing the complement, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X \le 2) &= P(X ∈ {1, 2}) \\
&= P(X = 1) + P(X = 2) \\
&= f(1) + f(2) \\
&= (\frac{1}{6})^{0}(\frac{5}{6}) + (\frac{1}{6})^{1}(\frac{5}{6}) \\
&= \frac{35}{36}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X > 2) &= 1 - P(X \le 2) \\
&= 1 - \frac{35}{36} \\
&= \frac{1}{36}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="showing-that-a-geometric-distribution-is-a-probability-distribution"></a>
## Showing That a Geometric Distribution Is a Probability Distribution

You might be wondering where the geometric distribution gets its name. The answer is that the probability mass function forms a geometric sequence!

Suppose that $X\sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$. Then, the probability mass function for this geometric distribution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= (1 - p)^{x - 1}p \\
f(x) &= p(1 - p)^{x - 1} \\
f(x)_(⏟)_(a_{x}) &= p_(⏟)_(a_{1}) \cdot (1 - p_(⏟)_(r))^{x - 1} \\
a_{x} &= a_{1} \cdot r^{x - 1}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we see that the probability mass function can be interpreted as a geometric sequence where the first term is $a_1=p$, and the common ratio is $r=1-p$.

Moreover, notice that $f(x)$ satisfies all the criteria to be a probability mass function, namely

1. $0 \leq f(x) \leq 1$ for all $x$ in $S$
2. $\displaystyle \sum\limits_{x \in S} f(x) = 1$

Criterion 1 is clearly true because

$$
0 \leq p \leq 1
$$

To show that criterion 2 is true, we simply need to calculate the sum of this infinite geometric series, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∑_(x ∈ S)f(x) &= ∑_(x = 1)^(∞)p(1 - p)^{x - 1} \\
&= (p)/(1 - (1 - p)) \\
&= \frac{p}{p} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $f(x)$ is indeed a probability distribution.
