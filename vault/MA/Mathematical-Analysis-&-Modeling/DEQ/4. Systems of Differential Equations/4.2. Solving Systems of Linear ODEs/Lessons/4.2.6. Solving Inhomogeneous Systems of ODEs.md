## Introduction

Consider an **inhomogeneous** linear system of ordinary differential equations in component form:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a_{11}x_{1}(t) + a_{12}x_{2}(t) + f_{1}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = a_{21}x_{1}(t) + a_{22}x_{2}(t) + f_{2}(t)
$$

Here, we can package the unknown functions into vectors
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t) \\ x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}$

Then, the system can be written as
$\begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix}$

Often, the **forcing functions** $f_1(t), f_2(t)$ are linear combinations of a few basic functions (such as $e^{3t}$, $t^2$, or $\sin t$).
In that case, it is useful to write $\mathbf{g}(t)$ as a sum of those basic functions times constant vectors.

For example, let's write down the system of linear ODEs below in matrix form.

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = 4x_{1}(t) + 2x_{2}(t) + 8e^{3t}; x_{2}^{′}(t) =-3x_{1}(t) + 5x_{2}(t) + 10e^{4t}
$$

First, let's rewrite our system, highlighting the coefficients:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = 4 \cdot x_{1}(t) + 2 \cdot x_{2}(t) + e^{3t} \cdot 8 + e^{4t} \cdot 0; x_{2}^{′}(t) = (-3) \cdot x_{1}(t) + 5 \cdot x_{2}(t) + e^{3t} \cdot 0 + e^{4t} \cdot 10
$$

Now, we can write the system in matrix form:
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 2 \\ -3 & 5\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t) \\ x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="complementary-vs-particular-solutions"></a>
## Complementary vs. Particular Solutions

Recall that an inhomogeneous linear system has the form

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{g}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant matrix and $\mathbf{g}(t)$ is a known forcing term. The associated *homogeneous* system is

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)
$$

A *general solution* of the *homogeneous* system is called the **complementary solution** and is denoted by $\mathbf{x}_c(t)$.
A **particular solution** is any single function $\mathbf{x}_p(t)$ that satisfies the full inhomogeneous system.

These two pieces combine to give the *general solution* of the inhomogeneous system:

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_c(t)+\mathbf{x}_p(t)
$$

Indeed, notice that

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x_{c} + x_{p})^{′} &= x_{c}^{′} + x_{p}^{′} \\
&= (Ax_{c}) + (Ax_{p} + g(t)) \\
&= Ax_{c} + Ax_{p} + g(t) \\
&= A(x_{c} + x_{p}) + g(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
(\mathbf{x}_c+\mathbf{x}_p)' = A(\mathbf{x}_c+\mathbf{x}_p)+\mathbf{g}(t)
$$

, the sum is a solution of the inhomogeneous system.

Notice that it doesn't matter which particular solution we take.
Suppose we found two different particular solutions, $\mathbf{x}_p(t)$ and $\mathbf{y}_p(t)$. Let's examine their difference,

$$
\mathbf{w}(t) = \mathbf{y}_p(t) - \mathbf{x}_p(t)
$$

.

By definition, we know that

$$
\mathbf{y}_p' = A\mathbf{y}_p + \mathbf{g}(t)
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{x}_p' = A\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{g}(t)
$$

Now, let's find the derivative of the difference $\mathbf{w}(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w^{′} &= (y_{p} - x_{p})^{′} \\
&= y_{p}^{′} - x_{p}^{′} \\
&= (Ay_{p} + g(t)) - (Ax_{p} + g(t)) \\
&= Ay_{p} - Ax_{p} \\
&= A(y_{p} - x_{p}) \\
&= Aw
\end{aligned}
$$

The result

$$
\mathbf{w}' = A\mathbf{w}
$$

means that the difference between any two particular solutions is itself a solution to the *homogeneous* system.

This is why *any* particular solution is enough. Any other particular solution can be written as

$$
\mathbf{y}_{p} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{y}_{p}-\mathbf{x}_{p})}_{\text{homogeneous}} \:\:\: + \underbrace{\mathbf{x}_{p}}_{\text{particular}} \!\!\!\!\!
$$

The "variety" among particular solutions is already captured by the complementary solution $\mathbf{x}_c(t)$.

<a id="choosing-the-form-of-a-particular-solution"></a>
## Choosing the Form of a Particular Solution

To solve

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{g}(t)
$$

we often look for a particular solution $\mathbf{x}_p(t)$ whose *form matches the forcing term* $\mathbf{g}(t)$.

In each case below, the unknowns are constant vectors (to be determined by substitution).

- **Constant forcing.** If $\mathbf{g}(t)$ is a constant vector, then we try
$\mathbf{x}_p(t)=\mathbf{a}$
where $\mathbf{a}$ is a constant vector.
- **Polynomial forcing.** If $\mathbf{g}(t)$ is a polynomial of degree $n$, then we try
$\mathbf{x}_p(t) = \mathbf{a}_n t^n+\mathbf{a}_{n-1} t^{n-1}+\cdots+\mathbf{a}_1 t+\mathbf{a}_0$
where $\mathbf{a}_n,\mathbf{a}_{n-1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_0$ are constant vectors.
- **Exponential forcing.** If $\mathbf{g}(t)=e^{\alpha t}\mathbf{v}$ for some constant vector $\mathbf{v}$, then we try
$\mathbf{x}_p(t)=e^{\alpha t}\mathbf{a}$
where $\mathbf{a}$ is a constant vector.
- **Trigonometric forcing.** If $\mathbf{g}(t)=\cos(\beta t)\mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{g}(t)=\sin(\beta t)\mathbf{v}$ for some constant vector $\mathbf{v}$, then we try
$\mathbf{x}_p(t)=\cos(\beta t)\mathbf{a}+\sin(\beta t)\mathbf{b}$
where $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are constant vectors.

In the next step, we substitute the chosen $\mathbf{x}_p(t)$ into the differential equation to solve for the unknown vectors.

Let's see some concrete examples.

## Finding the Particular Solution of an Inhomogeneous System of Linear ODEs

**Example:** Consider the following inhomogeneous system of linear differential equations:
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
The complementary solution to this system is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
Find the general solution of the system.

**Explanation**

Recall that the general solution of an inhomogeneous system can be written as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{x}_c(t) + \mathbf{x}_p(t)
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_c(t)$ is the complementary solution (solution of the corresponding homogeneous system) and $\mathbf{x}_p(t)$ is a particular solution.

The forcing term
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -3\end{bmatrix}$
of the inhomogeneous system of ODEs is exponential. Therefore, we assume that the particular solution is also exponential, i.e.
$\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix}$
where $a,b$ are constants that are to be determined.

Calculating the derivative of $\mathbf{x}_p$ with respect to $t$ gives
$\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix}$

To find the unknown constants, we substitute $\mathbf{x}_p$ and $\mathbf{x}'_p$ into our system:

$$
\begin{aligned}
7e^{7t}\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot e^{7t}\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix} + e^{7t}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
7\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}7a \\ 7b\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3a + b \\ b\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}7a \\ 7b\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3a + b - 1 \\ b - 3\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Equating the corresponding components, we get the following system:

$$
{3a + b - 1 = 7a; b - 3 = 7b
$$

From the second equation, we get

$$
b - 7b = 3 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad b = -\dfrac12
$$

Substituting this into the first equation and solving for $a$, we have

$$
3a - \dfrac12 - 1 = 7a \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad a = -\dfrac38
$$

Therefore, the particular solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{3}{8} \\ -\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

And thus, the general solution is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{3}{8} \\ -\frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Solving an Initial Value Problem for an Inhomogeneous System of Linear ODEs

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
Find the missing coefficients in the solution to the initial value problem, shown above.

$\begin{bmatrix}e^{t} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We are told that the general solution to the system is
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{t} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
where $c_1,c_2$ are constants that are to be determined.

Next, we find the values of $c_1$ and $c_2$. Substituting $t = 0$ into the general solution, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(0) &= \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}e^{0} \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}0 \cdot e^{0} \\ e^{0}\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0^{2} - 0 + 4 \\ -0^{2} + 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}c_{1} + 4 \\ c_{2} + 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of linear equations:

$$
{c_{1} + 4 = 6; c_{2} + 2 = 1
$$

From the first equation, we get $c_1 = 2$. From the second equation, we have $c_2 = -1$.

Therefore, the solution to the initial value problem is
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{t} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
