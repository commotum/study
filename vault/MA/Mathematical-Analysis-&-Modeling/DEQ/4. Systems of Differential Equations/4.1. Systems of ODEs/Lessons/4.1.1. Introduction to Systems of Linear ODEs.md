## Introduction

A **first-order linear system of differential equations** with two dependent variables is a system of equations that takes the following *standard form*:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a(t)x_{1}(t) + b(t)x_{2}(t) + f(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = c(t)x_{1}(t) + d(t)x_{2}(t) + g(t)
$$

where

- $a(t)$, $b(t)$, $c(t)$, $d(t)$, $f(t)$, and $g(t)$ are *known* functions, and
- $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are *unknown* functions (dependent variables).

For example,

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = \ln (t)x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) =-2tx_{1}(t) + x_{2}(t) + 5t^{2} + 1
$$

is a first-order linear system of ODEs since it can be written in the standard form

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = 0 \cdot x_{1}(t) + \ln (t) \cdot x_{2}(t) + 0; x_{2}^{′}(t) =-2t \cdot x_{1}(t) + 1 \cdot x_{2}(t) + (5t^{2} + 1)
$$

where

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a(t) = 0, & b(t) = \ln (t), & f(t) = 0, \\ c(t) =-2t, & d(t) = 1, & g(t) = 5t^{2} + 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

On the other hand,

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = \ln (x_{1}(t)) + x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = tx_{1}(t) - t^{2}x_{2}(t) + \sin (t)
$$

is not a first-order linear system of ODEs since it contains the term $\ln(x_1(t))$.

Similar definitions apply to systems with three or more variables can be defined similarly.

## Identifying a Linear System of Differential Equations

**Example:** Which of the following systems of differential equations are linear?

${x_{1}^{′}(t) = \frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}(t) + 5x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = \sqrt{5}x_{1}(t) + 3\sin (x_{2}(t))$

**Explanation**

A first-order linear system of differential equations with two dependent variables is a system of equations that takes the following standard form:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a(t)x_{1}(t) + b(t)x_{2}(t) + f(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = c(t)x_{1}(t) + d(t)x_{2}(t) + g(t)
$$

where

- $a(t)$, $b(t)$, $c(t)$, $d(t)$, $f(t)$, and $g(t)$ are known functions, and
- $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are unknown functions (dependent variables).

With this in mind, let's inspect each system:

- System I is not a first-order linear system of ODEs since it contains the terms $x_1^2(t)$ and $\sin(x_2(t))$.
- System II is a first-order linear system of ODEs since it can be written in the standard form, where
$\begin{bmatrix}a(t) = 8t, & b(t) =-1, & f(t) = 1, \\ c(t) = 6, & d(t) =-2, & g(t) = t^{2}\end{bmatrix}$.

Therefore, only the second system is linear.

## Matrix Differential Equations

We can write any first-order linear system of differential equations in the form of a **matrix differential equation**

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{f}(t)
$$

For example, the system

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = 2x_{1}(t) - 2x_{2}(t) + 4; x_{2}^{′}(t) =-x_{1}(t) + 3x_{2}(t) - t^{2}
$$

can be written as
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{′}(t) \\ x_{2}^{′}(t)\end{bmatrix}$

If the coefficients next to the variables are constants (as in the example above), we say that the system has a constant matrix. Then, we can just use the notation $A$ instead of $A(t)$.

## Writing a Linear System of Differential Equations in Matrix Form

**Example:** If the following system of differential equations is written in the form $\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)$, then what is the matrix $A$?
${x_{1}^{′}(t) + 3x_{1}(t) - 4x_{2}(t) = 0; x_{2}^{′}(t) - \sqrt{2}x_{1}(t) + 2x_{2}(t) = 0$

**Explanation**

A first-order linear system of differential equations with two dependent variables is a system of equations that takes the following standard form:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a(t)x_{1}(t) + b(t)x_{2}(t) + f(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = c(t)x_{1}(t) + d(t)x_{2}(t) + g(t)
$$

where

- $a(t)$, $b(t)$, $c(t)$, $d(t)$, $f(t)$, and $g(t)$ are known functions, and
- $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are unknown functions (dependent variables).
Such a system can always be written in the form
$\mathbf x'(t) = A\mathbf x(t) + \mathbf f(t)$

where
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(t) \\ x_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}$

We have the following system.

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) + 3x_{1}(t) - 4x_{2}(t) = 0; x_{2}^{′}(t) - \sqrt{2}x_{1}(t) + 2x_{2}(t) = 0
$$

Writing our system in standard form, we get the following:

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) =-3x_{1}(t) + 4x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = \sqrt{2}x_{1}(t) - 2x_{2}(t)
$$

So, we can rewrite our system in matrix form as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 4 \\ \sqrt{2} & -2\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 4 \\ \sqrt{2} & -2\end{bmatrix}$

## Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous Systems

Recall that a first-order linear system of differential equations with two dependent variables is a system of the form

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a(t)x_{1}(t) + b(t)x_{2}(t) + f(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = c(t)x_{1}(t) + d(t)x_{2}(t) + g(t)
$$

If $f \equiv 0$ and $g \equiv 0$ (i.e., both $f$ and $g$ are zero functions), then the system is called **homogeneous**. Otherwise, if at least one of $f$ or $g$ is nonzero, the system is **inhomogeneous**.

In matrix form, the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A(t)\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{f}(t)
$$

is homogeneous when $\mathbf{f} \equiv \mathbf{0}$, and inhomogeneous when $\mathbf{f} \not\equiv \mathbf{0}$.

For example:

- The system below is homogeneous because $\mathbf{f}(t) = \mathbf{0}$:
${x_{1}^{′}(t) = 3x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) =-2x_{1}(t) + x_{2}(t)$
- The system below is inhomogeneous because of the term $5t^2$:
${x_{1}^{′}(t) = 3x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) =-2x_{1}(t) + x_{2}(t) + 5t^{2}$

<a id="identifying-homogeneous-and-inhomogeneous-systems"></a>
## Identifying Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous Systems

**Example:** Which of the following systems are homogeneous?

1. ${x_{1}^{′}(t) =-2x_{1}(t) + 3x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = 6x_{1}(t) + x_{2}(t)$
2. ${x_{1}^{′}(t) = x_{1}(t) + t^{3}; x_{2}^{′}(t) =-x_{2}(t) + 1$
3. ${x_{1}^{′}(t) = 5x_{1}(t) - x_{2}(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) =-2x_{1}(t) + 4x_{2}(t)$

**Explanation**

Recall that a first-order linear system of differential equations with two dependent variables is a system of the form

$$
{x_{1}^{′}(t) = a(t)x_{1}(t) + b(t)x_{2}(t) + f(t); x_{2}^{′}(t) = c(t)x_{1}(t) + d(t)x_{2}(t) + g(t)
$$

If $f \equiv 0$ and $g \equiv 0$ (i.e., both $f$ and $g$ are zero functions), then the system is *homogeneous*. Otherwise, if at least one of $f$ or $g$ is not identically zero, the system is *inhomogeneous*.

With this in mind, let's inspect each system:

- System I is homogeneous since it can be written in the standard form with $f \equiv 0$ and $g \equiv 0{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}a(t) =-2, & b(t) = 3, & f(t) = 0 \\ c(t) = 6, & d(t) = 1, & g(t) = 0\end{bmatrix}$
- System II is inhomogeneous since it can be written in the standard form with $f \not\equiv 0$ and $g \not\equiv 0{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}a(t) = 1, & b(t) = 0, & f(t) = t^{3} \\ c(t) = 0, & d(t) =-1, & g(t) = 1\end{bmatrix}$
- System III is homogeneous since it can be written in the standard form with $f \equiv 0$ and $g \equiv 0{:}$
$\begin{bmatrix}a(t) = 5, & b(t) =-1, & f(t) = 0 \\ c(t) =-2, & d(t) = 4, & g(t) = 0\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, only the first and third systems are homogeneous.

<a id="solutions-for-systems-of-odes"></a>
## Solutions for Systems of ODEs

A **solution of the system** is a vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$ that satisfies the equation

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{f}(t)
$$

For example, one solution to our linear system
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & -2 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix}$
is the vector $\begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix}$ We can check this by substituting the vector into the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{′}(t) &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -2 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix}x(t) \\
\begin{bmatrix}(2e^{t})^{′} \\ (e^{t})^{′}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -2 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}2 \cdot (2e^{t}) + (-2) \cdot (e^{t}) \\ (-1) \cdot (2e^{t}) + 3 \cdot (e^{t})\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}4e^{t} - 2e^{t} \\ -2e^{t} + 3e^{t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}2e^{t} \\ e^{t}\end{bmatrix}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="identifying-solutions-to-a-matrix-differential-equation"></a>
## Identifying Solutions to a Matrix Differential Equation

**Example:** Consider the matrix differential equation $\begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}$ Which of the following vectors are solutions to this equation?
$\begin{bmatrix}3e^{5t} \\ 5e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Here, we need to substitute each vector into the given matrix differential equation and check if it results in a true statement.

For $\mathbf{x}_1(t)$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}(3e^{5t})^{′} \\ (5e^{-3t})^{′}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3e^{5t} \\ 5e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}15e^{5t} \\ -15e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3e^{5t} \\ 5e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}15e^{5t} \\ -15e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}15e^{5t} \\ -15e^{-3t}\end{bmatrix}.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, for $\mathbf{x}_2(t)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}(t + 5)^{′} \\ (t^{2} - 3)^{′}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t + 5 \\ t^{2} - 3\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2t\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t + 5 \\ t^{2} - 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2t\end{bmatrix} \mid \ne \begin{bmatrix}5t + 25 \\ -3t^{2} + 9\end{bmatrix}.\cdot
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, for $\mathbf{x}_3(t)$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}(5e^{t})^{′} \\ (-3e^{t})^{′}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5e^{t} \\ -3e^{t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}5e^{t} \\ -3e^{t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5e^{t} \\ -3e^{t}\end{bmatrix} \\
\begin{bmatrix}5e^{t} \\ -3e^{t}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}25e^{t} \\ 9e^{t}\end{bmatrix}.\cdot
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that the correct answer is "$\mathbf{x}_1(t)$ only."
