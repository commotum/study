## Introduction

Matrix exponentials can be used for solving homogeneous systems of the form

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix.

For example, consider the system

$$
{x^{′}(t) = 5y(t); y^{′}(t) =-5x(t)
$$

Writing our system in matrix form, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 5 \\ -5 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$ So, the matrix of the system is $\begin{bmatrix}0 & 5 \\ -5 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Recall that given the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = A \mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix, the general solution of the system can be written as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi(t) \, \mathbf{c}
$$

where $\Phi(t)$ is a fundamental matrix of the system, and $\mathbf{c}$ is some constant vector.

Since we have already seen how to compute the standard fundamental matrix, we will not repeat the calculation here. So, computing the standard fundamental matrix $\Phi_0(t)$ of the system, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}\cos (5t) & \sin (5t) \\ -\sin (5t) & \cos (5t)\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the general solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= Φ_{0}(t)c \\
&= e^{At}c \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\cos (5t) & \sin (5t) \\ -\sin (5t) & \cos (5t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1} \\ c_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}\cos (5t) \\ -\sin (5t)\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}\sin (5t) \\ \cos (5t)\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Note:** Recall that for the general solution of the ordinary differential equation

$$
x'(t)=ax(t)
$$

we have

$$
x'(t)=ax(t) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad x(t) = c \, e^{at}, \quad c \in \mathbb{R}
$$

Now, for the general solution of the matrix differential equation

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)
$$

we obtain

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At} \, \mathbf{c}, \quad \mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

Let's see more examples.

## Finding the General Solution of a Homogeneous System Using a Given Matrix Exponential

**Example:** ${x^{′}(t) =-7x(t) + y(t); y^{′}(t) =-7y(t)$
Find the general solution to the system of ODEs above.

*Hint: $\begin{bmatrix}e^{-7t} & te^{-7t} \\ 0 & e^{-7t}\end{bmatrix}$ where $\begin{bmatrix}-7 & 1 \\ 0 & -7\end{bmatrix}$*

**Explanation**

Writing our system in matrix form, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-7 & 1 \\ 0 & -7\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$ So, the matrix of the system is $\begin{bmatrix}-7 & 1 \\ 0 & -7\end{bmatrix}$

Given the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = A \mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix, the general solution of the system can be written as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi(t) \, \mathbf{c}
$$

where $\Phi(t)$ is a fundamental matrix of the system, and $\mathbf{c}$ is a constant vector.

Now, using the given information, the standard fundamental matrix $\Phi_0(t)$ of the system is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{-7t} & te^{-7t} \\ 0 & e^{-7t}\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the general solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= Φ_{0}(t)c \\
&= e^{At}c \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}e^{-7t} & te^{-7t} \\ 0 & e^{-7t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1} \\ c_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-7t} \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}te^{-7t} \\ e^{-7t}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}e^{-7t} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}t \\ 1\end{bmatrix}e^{-7t}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

## Solving Initial Value Problems

Now, let's find the solution to the initial value problem below

$$
{x^{′}(t) = 7x(t) + y(t); y^{′}(t) = 7y(t)
$$

Writing our system in matrix form, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}7 & 1 \\ 0 & 7\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$ So, the matrix of the system is $\begin{bmatrix}7 & 1 \\ 0 & 7\end{bmatrix}$

Recall that the general solution of the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = A \mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix, can be written as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi_0(t) \, \mathbf{c}
$$

where

$$
\Phi_0(t) = e^{At}
$$

is the standard fundamental matrix of the system, and $\mathbf{c}$ is some constant vector.

Evaluating both sides of

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At} \mathbf{c}
$$

at $t=0$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(0) &= Φ_{0}(0)c \\
x(0) &= e^{A(0)}c \\
x(0) &= Ic \\
c &= x(0)
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, the solution to our initial value problem is

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi_0(t) \, \mathbf{x}(0)
$$

Computing the standard fundamental matrix $\Phi_0(t)$ of the system, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}e^{7t} & te^{7t} \\ 0 & e^{7t}\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= e^{At}x(0) \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}e^{7t} & te^{7t} \\ 0 & e^{7t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}(3 - 2t)e^{7t} \\ -2e^{7t}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Solving Initial Value Problems With Shifted Conditions

Next, let's find the solution to the initial value problem

$$
{x^{′}(t) =-7y(t); y^{′}(t) = 7x(t)
$$

given that $\begin{bmatrix}\cos (7t) & -\sin (7t) \\ \sin (7t) & \cos (7t)\end{bmatrix}$ where $\begin{bmatrix}0 & -7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Writing our system in matrix form, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & -7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$ So, the matrix of the system is $\begin{bmatrix}0 & -7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Given the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = A \mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix, the general solution is

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At} \, \mathbf{c}
$$

We can determine the constant vector $\mathbf{c}$ using the initial condition at time $t=t_0{:}$

$$
\mathbf{x}(t_0) = e^{At_0} \, \mathbf{c} \implies \mathbf{c} = e^{-At_0} \, \mathbf{x}(t_0)
$$

since the inverse of $e^{At_0}$ is $e^{-At_0}$. Plugging this back into our general solution gives the formula

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= e^{At}e^{-At_{0}}x(t_{0}) \\
&= e^{A(t - t_{0})}x(t_{0})
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solution to our specific problem (with $t_0=1$) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= e^{A(t - 1)}x(1) \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\cos (7(t - 1)) & -\sin (7(t - 1)) \\ \sin (7(t - 1)) & \cos (7(t - 1))\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4\sin (7(t - 1)) \\ -4\cos (7(t - 1))\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Solving a Homogeneous Initial Value Problem Using a Given Matrix Exponential: Shifted Initial Conditions

**Example:** ${x^{′}(t) = 7y(t); y^{′}(t) = 7x(t)$,
Find the solution to the initial value problem above.

*Hint: $\begin{bmatrix}\cosh (7t) & \sinh (7t) \\ \sinh (7t) & \cosh (7t)\end{bmatrix}$ where $\begin{bmatrix}0 & 7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$*

**Explanation**

Writing our system in matrix form, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$ So, the matrix of the system is $\begin{bmatrix}0 & 7 \\ 7 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

Given the system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = A \mathbf{x}(t)
$$

where $A$ is a constant square matrix, the general solution of the system can be written as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi(t) \, \mathbf{c}
$$

where $\Phi(t)$ is a fundamental matrix of the system, and $\mathbf{c}$ is a constant vector.

So, we can write our general solution as

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi_0(t) \mathbf{c} =e^{At} \, \mathbf{c}
$$

where

$$
\Phi_0(t) = e^{At}
$$

is the standard fundamental matrix of the system. Substituting $t = t_0$, we have

$$
\mathbf{x}(t_0) = e^{At_0} \, \mathbf{c}
$$

and since $e^{At}$ is invertible for all $t$, we have

$$
\mathbf{c} = e^{-At_0} \, \mathbf{x}(t_0)
$$

Plugging the above back into our expression for $\mathbf{x}(t)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= e^{At}e^{-At_{0}}x(t_{0}) \\
&= e^{A(t - t_{0})}x(t_{0})
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x(t) &= e^{A(t - 1)}x(1) \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\cosh (7(t - 1)) & \sinh (7(t - 1)) \\ \sinh (7(t - 1)) & \cosh (7(t - 1))\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3\sinh (7(t - 1)) \\ 3\cosh (7(t - 1))\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Reminder: Properties of Matrix Exponentials

Before proceeding with the next example, let's recall some important properties of matrix exponentials:

1. *Law of Exponents:* If $A$ is a constant square matrix, then
$e^{A(t+s)} = e^{At}\,e^{As}$.
2. *Inverse:* For any $t$, the matrix $e^{At}$ is invertible and
$(e^{At})^{-1} = e^{-At}$.
3. *Identity:* For any square matrix $A$,
$e^{A\cdot 0} = I$.
4. *Product of exponentials:* If $A$ and $B$ commute (i.e., $AB = BA$), then
$e^{A}e^{B} = e^{A+B}$.
**Watch out!** In general, this identity is *not true* unless $A$ and $B$ commute.

Let's see how these properties can be used in practice.

## Proving the Matrix Exponential Formula for IVP Solution: Homogeneous Systems

**Example:** Consider the homogeneous system $\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t)$, where $A$ is a constant square matrix. You're given that the standard fundamental matrix $\Phi_0(t)$ is the unique solution to the initial value problem
$\Phi_0'(t) = A\Phi_0(t), \qquad \Phi_0(0) = I_n$.

A derivation of the formula for the solution $\mathbf{x}(t)$ passing through $\mathbf{x}(t_0)$ is presented below.

> $\textrm{L1}{:}\;$ Let $\Phi_0(t)=e^{At}$
> $\textrm{L2}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t)=\Phi_0(t)\,\mathbf{c}$ for some constant vector $\mathbf{c}$
> $\textrm{L3}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{At}\,\mathbf{c}$
> $\textrm{L4}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t_0)=e^{A t_0} \, \mathbf{c}$
> $\textrm{L5}{:}\;$ $(e^{A t_0})^{-1} \mathbf{x}(t_0)=(e^{At_0})^{-1} e^{A t_0} \, \mathbf{c}$
> $\textrm{L6}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{c} = e^{-A t_0} \mathbf{x}(t_0) \,$
> $\textrm{L7}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At} e^{-At_0} \mathbf{x}(t_0) \,$
> $\textrm{L8}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{At - At_0} \mathbf{x}(t_0) \,$
> $\textrm{L9}{:}\;$ $\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{A(t-t_0)} \mathbf{x}(t_0) \,$

Fill in the blanks to justify each step of the following reasoning.

$\qquad$ Line $\textrm{L5}$ follows from $\textrm{L4}$ by $AAAAAAAAAAAAAAA$

$\qquad$ The property of the matrix exponential used in $\textrm{L8}$ is $AAAAAAAAAAAAAAA$

$\qquad$ Line $\textrm{L9}$ follows from $\textrm{L8}$ by the fact that $AAAAAAAAAAAAAAA$

**Explanation**

Let's justify each of the selected steps.

- We first consider lines $\textrm{L4}$ and $\textrm{L5}$. Starting from $\textrm{L4}$,
$\mathbf{x}(t_0)=e^{A t_0} \, \mathbf{c}$,
we multiply both sides on the left by $(e^{At_0})^{-1}$ to get
$(e^{A t_0})^{-1}\mathbf{x}(t_0)=(e^{At_0})^{-1}e^{A t_0}\,\mathbf{c}$,
which is line $\textrm{L5}$. Therefore, $\textrm{L5}$ follows from $\textrm{L4}$ by $(e^{At_{0}})^{-1}$
- Next, we justify the step $\textrm{L7}$ to $\textrm{L8}$. Since $At$ and $-At_0$ commute, we may combine exponentials:
$e^{At}e^{-At_0}=e^{At-At_0}$.
Substituting this into line $\textrm{L7}$ produces line $\textrm{L8}$. Hence, the property of the matrix exponential used in $\textrm{L8}$ is $X$
- Finally, we consider lines $\textrm{L8}$ and $\textrm{L9}$. Starting from $\textrm{L8}$,
$\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{At-At_0}\mathbf{x}(t_0)$,
we rewrite the exponent using the algebraic identity $At-At_0=A(t-t_0)$ to get
$\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{A(t-t_0)}\mathbf{x}(t_0)$,
which is line $\textrm{L9}$. Thus, $\textrm{L9}$ follows from $\textrm{L8}$ by the fact that $\bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt lightgray]{At-At_0=A(t-t_0)\text{for any}t\text{and}t_0}$.
