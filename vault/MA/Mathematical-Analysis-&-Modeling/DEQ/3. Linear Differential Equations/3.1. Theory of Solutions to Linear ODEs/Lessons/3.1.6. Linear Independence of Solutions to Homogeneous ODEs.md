## Introduction

Suppose $\{f_1(x), f_2(x), \ldots, f_n(x) \}$ is a set of functions defined on an interval $I\subseteq R$.

- The set is called **linearly dependent** on $I$ if there exist real numbers $k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_n$, *not all zero*, such that
$k_1 f_1(x) + k_2 f_2(x) + \cdots + k_n f_n(x) = 0$
for all $x \in I$. Intuitively, at least one of the functions can be written as a linear combination of the others.

- The set is called **linearly independent** on $I$ if the *only* way the linear combination
$k_1 f_1(x) + k_2 f_2(x) + \cdots + k_n f_n(x)$
can equal $0$ for all $x \in I$ is by taking
$k_1 = k_2 = \cdots = k_n = 0$.

Notice that these definitions closely parallel the notions of linear dependence and independence for vectors in a vector space.

For example, let's consider the set of functions
$\{\sin x, \, \cos x, \, 2\sin x \}$
defined on $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Notice that for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we have that

$$
2 \cdot \sin x + 0 \cdot \cos x + (-1) \cdot 2\sin x = 0
$$

Therefore, since not all the scalar coefficients in the linear combination are zero, the given set of functions is linearly dependent.

Let's see another example.

<a id="identifying-pairs-of-linearly-dependent-and-linearly-independent-functions"></a>
## Identifying Pairs of Linearly Dependent and Linearly Independent Functions

**Example:** Given the set of functions $\{e^x, \, \sin x \}$ defined on $x \in \mathbb{R}$, explain why they are linearly independent.

**Explanation**

Recall that the set of functions $\{f_1(x), f_2(x), \ldots, f_n(x) \}$, which are defined on some interval $I$, is *linearly dependent* if there exist real scalars $k_1,k_2,\ldots,k_n$ not all zero such that the linear combination $k_1 \cdot f_1 + k_2 \cdot f_2 + \ldots + k_2 \cdot f_2$ is identically zero, i.e,

$$
k_1 \cdot f_1(x) + k_2 \cdot f_2(x) + \ldots + k_2 \cdot f_2(x) = 0
$$

for all $x \in I$. Otherwise, if

$$
k_1 = k_2 = \cdots = k_n = 0
$$

is the only solution, the set is *linearly independent*.

Now, suppose for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we have that

$$
k_1 e^x + k_2 \sin x = 0
$$

For example:

- If $x=0$, we get
$k_{1}(e^{0}) + k_{2}(\sin 0) = 0; k_{1} \cdot 1 + k_{2} \cdot 0 = 0⇒k_{1} = 0$.
- If $x=1$, we get
$k_{1}(e^{1}) + k_{2}(\sin 1) = 0; ek_{1} + (\sin 1)k_{2} = 0⇒ek_{1} + (\sin 1)k_{2} = 0$.

Thus, we obtain the following system of linear equations:

$$
{k_{1} = 0; ek_{1} + (\sin 1)k_{2} = 0
$$

The only solution for this system is

$$
\bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt gainsboro]{k_1=k_2=0}
$$

Therefore, since $\bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{all}}$ the scalar coefficients in the linear combination must be zero, the given set of functions is linearly $\bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{independent}}$.

## The Wronskian

Suppose $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are differentiable functions defined on some interval $I$. Then, the **Wronskian** is defined as
$\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}$
and it has the following important properties:

> If $W \neq 0$ for at least one $x \in I$, then $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent.

> If $W = 0$ and it’s known that $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions to the same second-order linear differential equation, then $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly dependent.

**Watch out!** We cannot deduce any information from $W = 0$ in cases where $y_1$ and $y_2$ are not known to be solutions to the same second-order linear differential equation.

---

<a id="calculating-the-wronskian-of-two-functions"></a>
## Calculating the Wronskian of Two Functions

**Example:** Compute the Wronskian $W(y_1, y_2)$ for $y_1(x) =2x$ and $y_2(x) = x^2$.

**Explanation**

The Wronskian $W(y_1,y_2)$ of two functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ is defined as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{bmatrix}
$$

In our case, we have $y_1(x) = 2x$ and

$$
y_2(x) = x^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= 2x(x^{2})^{′} - x^{2}(2x)^{′} \\
&= 4x^{2} - 2x^{2} \\
&= 2x^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="identifying-pairs-of-linearly-independent-functions-using-the-wronskian"></a>
## Identifying Pairs of Linearly Independent Functions Using the Wronskian

**Example:** $y_1(x) = x^2 + 1, \qquad y_2(x) = 2x, \qquad x \in \mathbb R$
Consider the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ above. Given that $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions of the same second-order linear differential equation, use the Wronskian to explain why the set $\{y_1(x), y_2(x) \}$ is linearly independent.

**Explanation**

Suppose the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are defined on some interval $I$.
The Wronskian $W(y_1,y_2)$ of these functions is defined as
$\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}$

Recall the following:

- If $W \neq 0$ for at least one $x \in I$, then $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent.
- If $W = 0$ and it’s known that $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions to the same second-order linear differential equation, then $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly dependent.

In our case, the Wronskian is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}x^{2} + 1 & 2x \\ (x^{2} + 1)^{′} & (2x)^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}x^{2} + 1 & 2x \\ 2x & 2\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (x^{2} + 1) \cdot 2 - (2x) \cdot (2x) \\
&= 2(x^{2} + 1) - 4x^{2} \\
&= 2x^{2} + 2 - 4x^{2} \\
&= 2 - 2x^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is nonzero for at least one $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Therefore, the set

$$
\left\{y_1(x), y_2(x) \right\}
$$

is linearly independent on $\mathbb R$.

## Sets of Fundamental Solutions

A **fundamental set of solutions** to a homogeneous, linear differential equation of degree $n$ is a set of precisely $n$ linearly independent solutions.

For example, consider the second-order homogeneous differential equation

$$
y''(x) - 4y(x) = 0
$$

Using direct substitution, it's straightforward to show that

$$
y_1(x) = e^{2x}
$$

is a solution to the equation.

We can then use our solution $y_1(x)$ and the reduction of order method to find a second solution to the equation. In this case, we'd find that

$$
y_2(x) = e^{-2x}
$$

is also a solution to the equation.

So, we now have two solutions to the equation. Let's keep track of our solutions by putting them into a set $S$, as follows:

$$
S = \left\{e^{2x}, e^{-2x}\right\}
$$

Computing the Wronskian of our solutions, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{1}^{′}y_{2} \\
&= (e^{2x})(e^{-2x})^{′} - (e^{2x})^{′}(e^{-2x}) \\
&= (e^{2x})(-2e^{-2x}) - (2e^{2x})(e^{-2x}) \\
&=-2 - 2 \\
&=-4
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the Wronskian is not zero, the solutions $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent.

Because the original equation is of degree two, and since the set $S$ contains *precisely* two linearly independent functions, we say that the set $S$ is a fundamental set of solutions to the differential equation.

**Important:** The fundamental set of solutions is not unique. For example, the set $S'$, given by

$$
S' = \left\{4e^{2x}, e^{-2x}\right\}
$$

is also a fundamental set of solutions.

As a general rule, we tend to pick the simplest set of linearly independent functions when constructing our fundamental set of solutions.

<a id="identifying-fundamental-sets-of-solutions"></a>
## Identifying Fundamental Sets of Solutions

**Example:** Consider the differential equation $x^2y'' - 5x y' + 9y=0$. Which of the following statements are true?

1. $y_1(x) = x^3$ is a solution to the equation for $x\in \mathbb R$.
2. $y_2(x) = x^3 \ln x$ is a solution to the equation for $x > 0$.
3. The functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ form a set of fundamental solutions to the equation on $(0, \infty)$.

**Explanation**

Recall that to determine whether $y(x)$ is a solution to the differential equation, we substitute $y$ and its derivatives into the equation.

Let's check each statement in turn.

- Statement I is true. First, we find the first and second derivatives of $y_1(x){:}$
$y_{1}^{′}(x)|= 3x^{2}; y_{1}^{″}(x)|= 6x$
Now, let's substitute $y_1(x)$ and its derivatives into the equation:
$x^{2} \cdot 6x - 5x \cdot 3x^{2} + 9x^{3}|= 0; 6x^{3} - 15x^{3} + 9x^{3}|= 0; 0|= 0✓$
So, $y_1(x)$ is a solution to the equation.
- Statement II is true. First, we find the first and second derivatives of $y_2(x){:}$
$y_{2}^{′}(x)|= 3x^{2}\ln x + x^{2}; y_{2}^{″}(x)|= 6x\ln x + 5x$
Now, let's substitute $y_2(x)$ and its derivatives into the equation:
$x^{2}(6x\ln x + 5x) - 5x(3x^{2}\ln x + x^{2}) + 9x^{3}\ln x|= 0; (6x^{3}\ln x + 5x^{3} - 15x^{3}\ln x - 5x^{3} + 9x^{3}\ln x)|= 0; 0|= 0✓$
So, $y_2(x)$ is a solution to the equation.
- Statement III is true. Both $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions of the equation. To check if these functions are linearly independent, we compute the Wronskian:
$W(y_{1}, y_{2})|=\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}; =\begin{vmatrix}x^{3} & x^{3}\ln x \\ 3x^{2} & 3x^{2}\ln x + x^{2}\end{vmatrix}; = x^{3}(3x^{2}\ln x + x^{2}) - x^{3}\ln x \cdot 3x^{2}; = 3x^{5}\ln x + x^{5} - 3x^{5}\ln x; = x^{5}$
Since the Wronskian is not identically $0$ on $(0, \infty)$, the functions are linearly independent. Hence, $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ form a set of fundamental solutions to the equation on $(0, \infty)$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I, II, and III".
