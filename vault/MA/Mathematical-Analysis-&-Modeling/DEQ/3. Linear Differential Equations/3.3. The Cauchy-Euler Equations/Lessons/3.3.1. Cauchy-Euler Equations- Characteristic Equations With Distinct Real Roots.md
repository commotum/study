## Introduction

The second-order Cauchy-Euler equation has the form

$$
ax^2y''+bxy'+cy=f(x)
$$

where $a,b$, and $c$ are constants.

Cauchy-Euler equations can be solved using a trial solution

$$
y=x^\lambda
$$

where $\lambda$ is a constant to be found. To illustrate, let's solve the following Cauchy-Euler equation:

$$
x^2y''+xy'-9y=0
$$

Differentiating the trial solution

$$
y = x^\lambda
$$

with respect to $x$ gives

$$
y' = \lambda x^{\lambda -1}, \qquad y'' = \lambda(\lambda-1)x^{\lambda-2}
$$

Substituting the above to our differential equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2}λ(λ - 1)x^{λ - 2} + xλx^{λ - 1} - 9x^{λ} &= 0 \\
λ(λ - 1)x^{λ} + λx^{λ} - 9x^{λ} &= 0 \\
x^{λ}(λ(λ - 1) + λ - 9) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following characteristic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
λ(λ - 1) + λ - 9 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 9 &= 0 \\
λ &= ± 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the general solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(x) &= c_{1}x^{3} + c_{2}x^{-3} \\
&= c_{1}x^{3} + \frac{c_{2}}{x^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying Instances of the Cauchy-Euler Equation

**Example:** Which of the following equations is an instance of the Cauchy-Euler equation?

1. $-2x^2y''+6xy'-5y=0$
2. $xy''+2y'-3xy=0$
3. $y''-3x^2y=\sqrt{x}$

**Explanation**

The second-order Cauchy-Euler equation has the form

$$
ax^2y''+bxy'+cy=f(x)
$$

where $a,b$, and $c$ are constants. With that in mind, let's inspect each of the given equations.

- Equation I is a Cauchy-Euler equation with $a=-2$, $b=6$, $c=-5$, and $f(x)=0$.
- Equation II is *not* a Cauchy-Euler equation because the $y''$ is not multiplied by an $x^2$. Instead, it is multiplied by an $x$.
- Equation III is *not* a Cauchy-Euler equation because the $y''$ term is not multiplied by an $x^2$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only".

## Solving an Instance of the Cauchy-Euler Equation

**Example:** Find the general solution to the equation
$3t^2 y''+8ty' -2y = 0, \quad t\gt 0$.

**Explanation**

This is an instance of the Cauchy-Euler equation. So, we assume the solutions take the form

$$
y=t^\lambda
$$

Differentiating $y$ with respect to $t$ gives

$$
y' = \lambda t^{\lambda -1}, \qquad y'' = \lambda(\lambda-1)t^{\lambda-2}
$$

Substituting the above into our differential equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
3t^{2} \cdot λ(λ - 1)t^{λ - 2} + 8t \cdot λt^{λ - 1} - 2t^{λ} &= 0 \\
3λ(λ - 1)t^{λ} + 8λt^{λ} - 2t^{λ} &= 0 \\
t^{λ}[3λ(λ - 1) + 8λ - 2] &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following characteristic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
3λ(λ - 1) + 8λ - 2 &= 0 \\
3λ^{2} + 5λ - 2 &= 0 \\
(λ + 2)(3λ - 1) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The roots of the characteristic equation are

$$
\lambda = -2
$$

and

$$
\lambda = \dfrac{1}{3}
$$

Therefore, the general solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(t) &= c_{1}t^{-2} + c_{2}t^{1/3} \\
&= \frac{c_{1}}{t^{2}} + c_{2}t^{1/3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Solving an Initial Value Problem Involving a Cauchy-Euler Equation

**Example:** Given that the differential equation

$x^2 y'' +2 xy' -6 y= 0$

has the general solution

$y=\dfrac{c_1}{x^3}+ c_2x^{2}$,

find the solution to the initial value problem

$x^2 y'' +2 xy' -6 y= 0, \quad y(1)= 0, \quad y'(1) = 5, \quad x \gt 0$.

**Explanation**

We find the constants $c_1$ and $c_2$ using the initial conditions.

- First, we apply the condition $y(1)=0$. Substituting $y(1)= 0$ into the general solution gives
$0 = c_1+c_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad c_2 = -c_1$.
- Then, we apply the condition $y'(1) = 5$. Differentiating $y$, we get
$y^{′}|=-3\frac{c_{1}}{x^{4}} + 2c_{2}x$.
Substituting $y'(1)=5$ and $c_2=-c_1$ into the above gives
$5|=-3c_{1} + 2c_{2}; 5|=-3c_{1} + 2(-c_{1}); 5|=-5c_{1}; c_{1}|=-1⇒c_{2} = 1$.

Therefore, the solution to the initial value problem is

$$
y(x)=x^{2}-\dfrac{1}{x^3}
$$
