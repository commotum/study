## Introduction

In the previous lesson, we focused on dilution problems in which the incoming solution contained none of the substance being modeled. In those cases, the rate at which the substance entered the container was zero.

In many practical situations, however, the incoming solution *does* contain some amount of the substance. When this happens,

- the rate of entry is no longer zero, and
- the resulting differential equation takes the form of a first-order linear differential equation.

For example, suppose a storage tank initially holds $650\,\textrm{L}$ of water containing $1\,300\,\textrm{g}$ of dissolved chlorine.

- A solution containing $20\,\textrm{g/L}$ of dissolved chlorine is pumped into the tank at a rate of $5\,\textrm{L/min}$.
- At the same time, the completely mixed water is drained out at a rate of $5\,\textrm{L/min}$.

To construct the model for the amount of chlorine $Q$ in the tank, we compare the rates of chlorine entering and leaving. The derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$. This is equal to the rate at which chlorine enters the tank minus the rate at which chlorine leaves the tank.

$$
\bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}}
$$

We compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which chlorine enters the tank.

The rate of chlorine entering is the concentration times the flow rate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (20\frac{g}{L}) \cdot (5\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= (20\frac{g}{L}) \cdot (5\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= 100\frac{g}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which chlorine exits the tank.

Since the volume remains constant at $650\,\textrm{L}$, the concentration in the tank is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{650}\,\dfrac{\textrm{g}}{\textrm{L}}
$$

So, the rate of chlorine exiting is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (5\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{650}\frac{g}{L}) \\
&= (5\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{650}\frac{g}{L}) \\
&= \frac{Q}{130}\frac{g}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The net rate of change

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_\text{in} - Q'_\text{out}
$$

is the difference between the rate in and the rate out. Combining this with the initial condition

$$
Q(0) = 1\,300\,\textrm{g}
$$

we can state the complete initial value problem:

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = 100 - \dfrac{1}{130}Q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dfrac{1}{130}Q = 100, \quad Q(0) = 1\,300\,\textrm{g}}
$$

## Constructing and Solving a Dilution Model: Inflow Equals Outflow

**Example:** A fertilizer tank initially holds $800\,\textrm{L}$ of liquid containing $7\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved nutrient.
A solution containing $0.01\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of dissolved nutrient is pumped into the tank at a rate of $40\,\textrm{L/min}$, while the completely mixed liquid is drained out at a rate of $40\,\textrm{L/min}$. Which expression gives the amount of nutrient $Q(t)$, in kilograms, in the tank $t$ minutes after pumping begins?

**Explanation**

Let $Q$ be the amount of nutrient in the tank at time $t$, in minutes. Then, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$. This is equal to the rate at which nutrient enters the tank minus the rate at which nutrient leaves the tank.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{kg}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which additives enter the tank.

Note that a feed solution containing $0.01\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of the nutrient enters the tank at a rate of $40\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, the rate at which the *nutrient* enters the tank is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0.01\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (40\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= (0.01\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (40\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= 0.4\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which additives exit the tank.

Next, note that the rate of flow in equals the rate of flow out, so the volume in the tank remains $800\,\textrm{L}$. Then, since $Q$ is the amount of nutrient in the tank, the *concentration* of the solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{800}\,\textrm{kg/L}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed liquid leaves at a rate of $40\,\textrm{L/min}$, *nutrient* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (40\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{800}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= (40\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{800}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= \frac{40Q}{800}\frac{kg}{min} \\
&= \frac{Q}{20}\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 0.4 - \frac{Q}{20}
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives the equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dfrac{1}{20}Q = 0.4
$$

**Step 4**: Integrate the equation.

This is a first-order linear equation with constant coefficients. To find the general solution, we must sum the complementary and particular solutions.

- To find the complementary solution $Q_c$, we solve the corresponding homogeneous equation, given by
$\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q_c}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dfrac{1}{20}Q_c = 0$.
Solving using an integration technique, such as separation of variables, we obtain the complementary solution
$Q_c(t) = Ae^{-t/20}$,
where $A$ is a constant of integration.
- Next, we find the particular solution. Note that the right-hand side of the inhomogeneous equation is a polynomial of degree $0$. So, we assume that the particular solution is also a polynomial of degree $0$, i.e.,
$Q_p(t) = \alpha$.
To find the value of $\alpha$, we substitute $Q_p = \alpha$ and $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = (\alpha)' = 0$ into the inhomogeneous equation:
$0 + \dfrac{1}{20}\alpha = 0.4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = 8$.
Therefore, the particular solution is $Q_p(t) = 8$.

Now, since the general solution is given by the sum of the complementary solution and the particular solution, the general solution in our case is

$$
Q(t) = Q_c(t) + Q_p(t) = Ae^{-t/20} + 8
$$

We're told that the tank initially contains $7\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved nutrient. So, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0) = 7$, and solve for $A{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 7 \\
Ae^{-0/20} + 8 &= 7 \\
A &= -1
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
Q(t) = 8 - e^{-t/20}
$$

## Constructing and Solving a Dilution Model

**Example:** A water-treatment holding tank initially holds $700\,\textrm{L}$ of liquid containing $12\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved coagulant.
A feed solution containing $0.05\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of the coagulant is pumped into the tank at a rate of $30\,\textrm{L/min}$, while the completely mixed liquid is drained out at a rate of $20\,\textrm{L/min}$.
Find, rounded to two decimal places, the amount of coagulant in the tank $8$ minutes after pumping begins.

**Explanation**

Let $Q$ be the amount of coagulant in the tank at time $t$, in minutes. Then, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$. This is equal to the rate at which coagulant enters the tank minus the rate at which coagulant leaves the tank.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{kg}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which coagulant enters the tank.

Note that a feed solution containing $0.05\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of the coagulant enters the tank at a rate of $30\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, the rate at which the *coagulant* enters the tank is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0.05\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (30\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= (0.05\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (30\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= 1.5\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which coagulant exits the tank.

To find the rate at which coagulant leaves the tank, we must first determine the volume of liquid in the tank at time $t$.

The tank initially contains $700\,\textrm{L}$ of liquid; a solution enters at $30\,\textrm{L/min}$ and leaves at $20\,\textrm{L/min}$.
As a result, the volume of liquid in the tank after $t$ minutes is

$$
700 + 30t - 20t = (700+10t)\,\textrm{L}
$$

Since $Q$ is the amount of coagulant in the tank, the *concentration* of the mixed solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{700+10t}\,\textrm{kg/L}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed liquid leaves at a rate of $20\,\textrm{L/min}$, *coagulant* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (20\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{700 + 10t}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= (20\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{700 + 10t}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= \frac{20Q}{700 + 10t}\frac{kg}{min} \\
&= \frac{2Q}{70 + t}\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 1.5 - \frac{2Q}{70 + t}
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives the equation

$$
\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{2}{70 + t}Q = 1.5
$$

**Step 4**: Integrate the equation.

This is a first-order linear equation with variable coefficients. So we find the general solution using an integrating factor:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(t) &= e^{∫2/(70 + t)dt} \\
&= e^{2\ln \mid 70 + t \mid} \\
&= e^{\ln (70 + t)^{2}} \\
&= (70 + t)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying both sides of the differential equation by

$$
I(t) = (70+t)^2
$$

yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
(70 + t)^{2} \cdot (\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{2}{70 + t}Q) &= (70 + t)^{2} \cdot 1.5 \\
(70 + t)^{2}\frac{dQ}{dt} + 2(70 + t)Q &= 1.5(70 + t)^{2} \\
\frac{d}{dt}(Q(70 + t)^{2}) &= 1.5(70 + t)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we integrate with respect to $t{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{d}{dt}(Q(70 + t)^{2})dt &= ∫1.5(70 + t)^{2}dt \\
Q(70 + t)^{2} &= 1.5 \cdot \frac{1}{3}(70 + t)^{3} + C \\
Q(t) &= 0.5(70 + t) + (C)/((70 + t)^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that $C$ is a constant of integration.

We're told that the tank initially contains $12\,\textrm{kg}$ of coagulant. So, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0) = 12$, and solve for $C{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 12 \\
0.5(70 + 0) + (C)/((70 + 0)^{2}) &= 12 \\
35 + \frac{C}{70^{2}} &= 12 \\
\frac{C}{70^{2}} &= -23 \\
C &= -23(70)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(t) &= 0.5(70 + t) - (23(70)^{2})/((70 + t)^{2}) \\
&= 0.5(70 + t) - 23(\frac{70}{70 + t})^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the amount of coagulant in the tank $8$ minutes after pumping begins is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(8) &= 0.5(70 + 8) - 23(\frac{70}{70 + 8})^{2} \\
&= 39 - 23(\frac{70}{78})^{2} \\
&\approx 20.48kg
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to two decimal places.

## Determining an Amount at an Overflow Point

**Example:** A syrup mixing tank in a confectionery factory can hold up to $240\,\textrm{gal}$ of liquid. Initially, it contains $96\,\textrm{gal}$ of syrup solution with $32\,\textrm{lb}$ of dissolved sugar. A more concentrated syrup with concentration $1.5\,\textrm{lb/gal}$ flows into the tank at a rate of $24\,\textrm{gal/min}$, while the completely mixed solution drains out at a rate of $16\,\textrm{gal/min}$. Rounded to one decimal place, find the amount of sugar in the tank at the moment it begins to overflow.

**Explanation**

Since $Q$ is the amount of sugar in the tank at time $t$, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$, in minutes. This is equal to the rate at which sugar enters the tank minus the rate at which sugar leaves the tank.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{lb}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which sugar enters the tank.

First, note that a solution containing $1.5\,\textrm{lb/gal}$ of sugar enters the tank at a rate of $24\,\textrm{gal/min}$. So, the rate at which the *sugar* enters the tank is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (1.5\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (24\frac{gal}{min}) \\
&= (1.5\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (24\frac{gal}{min}) \\
&= 36\frac{lb}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which sugar exits the tank.

To find the rate at which sugar leaves the tank, we must first determine the volume of solution in the tank at time $t$.

The tank initially contains $96\,\textrm{gal}$ of solution; solution enters at $24\,\textrm{gal/min}$ and mixed solution leaves at $16\,\textrm{gal/min}$.
As a result, the amount of solution in the tank after $t$ minutes is

$$
V(t) = 96 + 24t - 16t = 8(12+t)\,\textrm{gal}
$$

Note that $V(t)$ is an increasing function, so the tank is filling.

Since $Q$ is the amount of sugar in the tank, the concentration of the solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{8(12 + t)}\,\textrm{lb/gal}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed solution leaves at a rate of $16\,\textrm{gal/min}$, *sugar* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (16\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot ((Q)/(8(12 + t))\frac{lb}{gal}) \\
&= (16\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot ((Q)/(8(12 + t))\frac{lb}{gal}) \\
&= (16Q)/(8(12 + t))\frac{lb}{min} \\
&= \frac{2Q}{12 + t}\frac{lb}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 36 - \frac{2Q}{12 + t}
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives the equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dfrac{2}{12 + t}\,Q = 36
$$

**Step 4**: Integrate the equation.

This is a first-order linear equation with variable coefficients. So we find the general solution using an integrating factor:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(t) &= e^{∫2/(12 + t)dt} \\
&= e^{2\ln \mid 12 + t \mid} \\
&= e^{\ln (12 + t)^{2}} \\
&= (12 + t)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying both sides of the differential equation by

$$
I(t) = (12+t)^2
$$

yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
(12 + t)^{2} \cdot (\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{2}{12 + t}Q) &= (12 + t)^{2} \cdot 36 \\
(12 + t)^{2}\frac{dQ}{dt} + 2(12 + t)Q &= 36(12 + t)^{2} \\
\frac{d}{dt}(Q(12 + t)^{2}) &= 36(12 + t)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we integrate with respect to $t{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{d}{dt}(Q(12 + t)^{2})dt &= ∫36(12 + t)^{2}dt \\
Q(12 + t)^{2} &= 36 \cdot \frac{1}{3}(12 + t)^{3} + C \\
Q(t) &= 12(12 + t) + (C)/((12 + t)^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

We're told that the tank initially contains $32\,\textrm{lb}$ of sugar. So, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0) = 32$, and solve for $C{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 32 \\
12(12 + 0) + (C)/((12 + 0)^{2}) &= 32 \\
12 \cdot 12 + (C)/((12)^{2}) &= 32 \\
144 + \frac{C}{12^{2}} &= 32 \\
\frac{C}{12^{2}} &= -112 \\
C &= -112(12)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(t) &= 12(12 + t) - (112(12)^{2})/((12 + t)^{2}) \\
&= 12(12 + t) - 112(\frac{12}{12 + t})^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let $t_o$ be the time when the tank begins to overflow. The tank has a capacity of $240\,\textrm{gal}$, so we can use the condition

$$
V(t_o) = 240
$$

to solve for $t_o{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(t_{o}) &= 240 \\
96 + 24t_{o} - 16t_{o} &= 240 \\
96 + 8t_{o} &= 240 \\
8t_{o} &= 144 \\
t_{o} &= 18min
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, rounded to one decimal place, the amount of sugar in the tank at the moment it begins to overflow is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(18) &= 12(12 + 18) - 112(\frac{12}{12 + 18})^{2} \\
&= 360 - 112(\frac{12}{30})^{2} \\
&\approx 342.1lb
\end{aligned}
$$
