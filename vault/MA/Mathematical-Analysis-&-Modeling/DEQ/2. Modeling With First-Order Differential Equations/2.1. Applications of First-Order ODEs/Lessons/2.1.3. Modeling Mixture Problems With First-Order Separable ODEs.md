## Introduction

Consider a container which initially holds $V_0\,\textrm{gal}$ of solution containing $a\,\textrm{lb}$ of a substance.

- Another solution, containing $b\,\textrm{lb}$ of the substance per gallon, flows *into* the container at the rate $e\,\textrm{gal}/\textrm{min}$.
- At the same time, the completely mixed solution flows *out* of the container at the rate $f\,\textrm{gal}/\text{min}$.

![](<../Source/2.1.3. Modeling Mixture Problems With First-Order Separable ODEs/Images/4eb6296a4070b27a329bd17f0b81df1e.png>)

How can we model the amount $Q$ (in pounds) of substance in the container at time $t$?

To model $Q(t)$, we can analyze how it changes over time.
The rate of change of the amount of substance in the container is given by

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_\text{in} - Q'_\text{out}
$$

where:

- $Q'_\text{in}$ is the rate at which the substance *enters* the container. A solution containing $b\,\textrm{lb}/\textrm{gal}$ of the substance enters the container at a rate of $e\,\textrm{gal}/\textrm{min}$. So, we have
$Q_{in}^{′}|= (b\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (e\frac{gal}{min}); = (b\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (e\frac{gal}{min}); = be\frac{lb}{min}$.
- $Q'_\text{out}$ is the rate at which the substance *leaves* the container. To determine the rate of exit, we first find the volume of solution in the container at time $t$.Starting from the initial volume $V_0$, we add the volume that enters, $et$, and subtract the volume that leaves, $ft$. Thus, the volume of solution at time $t$ is
$(V_0 + et - ft)\,\textrm{gal}$.
Since $Q$ is the mass of substance in the container, the concentration of the solution at time $t$ is $\dfrac{Q}{V_0 + et - ft}\,\textrm{lb}/\textrm{gal}$. So, as the completely mixed solution leaves at a rate of $f\,\textrm{gal}/\textrm{min}$, the substance leaves the container at the rate of
$Q_{out}^{′}|= (f\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{V_{0} + et - ft}\frac{lb}{gal}); = (f\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{V_{0} + et - ft}\frac{lb}{gal}); = f(\frac{Q}{V_{0} + et - ft})\frac{lb}{min}$.

Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = be - f\left(\dfrac{Q}{V_0 + et - ft}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + f\left(\dfrac{Q}{V_0 + et - ft}\right) = be}
$$

Let's see this in a more concrete example.

## A Worked Example

Suppose that a tank initially holds $450\,\textrm{L}$ of water containing $6\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved contaminant.
A solution containing no contaminant is pumped into the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$, while the completely mixed solution leaves the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$.

Let's find a differential equation that describes the amount of dissolved contaminant $Q$, in kilograms, in the tank $t$ minutes after the solution begins to be added.

Since $Q$ is the amount of dissolved contaminant in the tank at time $t$, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

represents the rate at which this amount changes. This is equal to the rate at which dissolved contaminant enters the tank minus the rate at which dissolved contaminant leaves the tank:

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{kg}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which dissolved contaminant enters the tank.

First, note that a solution containing $0\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of dissolved contaminant enters the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, the rate at which the *dissolved contaminant* enters the tank is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (8\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= (0\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (8\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= 0\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which dissolved contaminant exits the tank.

Now, to find the rate at which dissolved contaminant leaves the tank, we must first determine the volume of solution in the tank at time $t$.

- The tank initially contains $450\,\textrm{L}$ of solution.
- Solution enters the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, after $t$ minutes, $8t\,\textrm{L}$ have entered the tank.
- The completely mixed solution leaves the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, after $t$ minutes, $8t\,\textrm{L}$ have left the tank.

As a result, the volume of solution in the tank after $t$ minutes is

$$
450 + 8t - 8t = 450\,\textrm{L}
$$

Since $Q$ is the mass of dissolved contaminant in the tank, the *concentration* of the solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{450}\,\dfrac{\textrm{kg}}{\textrm{L}}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed solution leaves at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$, *dissolved contaminant* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (8\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{450}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= (8\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{450}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= \frac{8Q}{450}\frac{kg}{min} \\
&= \frac{4Q}{225}\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

Finally, the rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 0 - \frac{4Q}{225}
\end{aligned}
$$

which finally gives

$$
d Q d t + 4 225 Q = 0
$$

## Constructing a Dilution Model

**Example:** A vat initially holds $360\,\textrm{L}$ of a sugar solution containing $18\,\textrm{kg}$ of sugar.
A sugar solution containing $0.30\,\textrm{kg}$ of sugar per liter is poured into the vat at a rate of $18\,\textrm{L/min}$, while the completely mixed solution leaves the vat at a rate of $30\,\textrm{L/min}$. What differential equation describes the amount of sugar $Q$, in kilograms, in the vat $t$ minutes after the inflow begins?

**Explanation**

Since $Q$ is the amount of sugar in the vat at time $t$, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$, in minutes. This is equal to the rate at which sugar enters the vat minus the rate at which sugar leaves the vat.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{kg}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which sugar enters the vat.

First, note that a sugar solution containing $0.30\,\textrm{kg/L}$ of sugar enters the vat at a rate of $18\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, the rate at which the *sugar* enters the vat is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0.30\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (18\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= (0.30\frac{kg}{L}) \cdot (18\frac{L}{min}) \\
&= 5.4\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which sugar exits the vat.

Now, to find the rate at which sugar leaves the vat, we must first determine the volume of solution in the vat at time $t$.

- The vat initially contains $360\,\textrm{L}$ of solution.
- Sugar solution enters the vat at a rate of $18\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, after $t$ minutes, $18t\,\textrm{L}$ have entered the vat.
- The completely mixed solution leaves the vat at a rate of $30\,\textrm{L/min}$. So, after $t$ minutes, $30t\,\textrm{L}$ have left the vat.

As a result, the volume of solution in the vat after $t$ minutes is

$$
360 + 18t - 30t = (360-12t)\,\textrm{L}
$$

Since $Q$ is the mass of sugar in the vat, the *concentration* of the solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{360 - 12t}\,\dfrac{\textrm{kg}}{\textrm{L}}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed solution leaves at a rate of $30\,\textrm{L/min}$, *sugar* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (30\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{360 - 12t}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= (30\frac{L}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{360 - 12t}\frac{kg}{L}) \\
&= \frac{30Q}{360 - 12t}\frac{kg}{min} \\
&= \frac{5Q}{60 - 2t}\frac{kg}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

Finally, the rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 5.4 - \frac{5Q}{60 - 2t}
\end{aligned}
$$

which finally gives

$$
\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{5}{60 - 2t}Q = 5.4
$$

<a id="solving-a-dilution-model-where-the-input-has-a-concentration-of-zero"></a>
## Solving a Dilution Model Where the Input Has a Concentration of Zero

So far, we have seen that if a container initially holds $V_0\,\textrm{gal}$ of solution containing $a\,\textrm{lb}$ of a substance, and

- a solution with concentration $b\,\textrm{lb}/\textrm{gal}$ flows into the container at the rate $e\,\textrm{gal}/\textrm{min}$, while
- the completely mixed solution flows out at the rate $f\,\textrm{gal}/\text{min}$,

then the amount $Q(t)$ of the substance in the container satisfies the differential equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + f\left(\dfrac{Q}{V_0 + (e-f)t}\right) = be
$$

For the rest of this lesson, we'll focus on the special case where the input solution contains *no substance*, that is, where $b=0$. In this case, the right-hand side vanishes, and the differential equation becomes *separable*:

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + f\left(\dfrac{Q}{V_0 + (e-f)t}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac1Q\cdot\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} =-\dfrac{f}{V_0 + (e-f)t}
$$

Notice that the right-hand side is negative, meaning the amount of substance in the container decreases over time. We can now solve the differential equation directly by separation of variables.

To demonstrate, consider our previous example: a tank initially holds $450\,\textrm{L}$ of water containing $6\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved contaminant, and a solution containing no contaminant is pumped into the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$, while the completely mixed solution leaves the tank at a rate of $8\,\textrm{L/min}$.

We found that the amount of dissolved contaminant $Q(t)$, in kilograms, in the tank after $t$ minutes is modeled by the differential equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dfrac{4}{225}Q = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{1}{Q} \cdot \dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dfrac{4}{225}
$$

We solve this equation by integrating both sides with respect to $t{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dt}dt &= ∫(-\frac{4}{225})dt \\
∫\frac{1}{Q}dQ &= -\frac{4}{225}∫dt \\
\ln \mid Q \mid &= -\frac{4t}{225} + C \\
Q &= e^{-4t/225 + C} \\
Q(t) &= Ae^{-4t/225}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that $A = \pm e^C$ is a constant of integration.

Finally, since the tank initially contains $6\,\textrm{kg}$ of dissolved contaminant, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0)=6$, and solve for $A{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 6 \\
Ae^{-4(0)/225} &= 6 \\
A &= 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
\bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{Q(t) = 6e^{-4t/225}}
$$

## Constructing and Solving a Dilution Model

**Example:** A reactor initially holds $300\,\textrm{gal}$ of a chemical solution containing $18\,\textrm{lb}$ of dissolved reactant.
Pure solvent is pumped into the reactor at a rate of $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$, while the completely mixed chemical solution leaves the reactor at a rate of $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$.
Find, rounded to two decimal places, the amount of reactant in the reactor $25$ minutes after the solvent pumping begins.

**Explanation**

Let $Q$ be the amount of reactant in the reactor at time $t$. Then, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$, in minutes. This is equal to the rate at which reactant enters the reactor minus the rate at which reactant leaves the reactor.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{lb}/\textrm{min}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which reactant enters the reactor.

Note that pure solvent (i.e., a solution containing $0\,\textrm{lb/gal}$ of reactant) enters the reactor at a rate of $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$. So, the rate at which the *reactant* enters the reactor is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (14\frac{gal}{min}) \\
&= (0\frac{lb}{gal}) \cdot (14\frac{gal}{min}) \\
&= 0\frac{lb}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which reactant exits the reactor.

To find the rate at which reactant leaves the reactor, we first determine the volume of solution in the reactor at time $t$.

The reactor initially contains $300\,\textrm{gal}$ of chemical solution; solvent enters at $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$ and drains out at $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$.
As a result, the amount of chemical solution in the reactor after $t$ minutes is

$$
300 + 14t - 14t = 300\,\textrm{gal}
$$

Since $Q$ is the amount of reactant in the reactor, the *concentration* of the solution at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{300}\,\dfrac{\textrm{lb}}{\textrm{gal}}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed chemical solution leaves at a rate of $14\,\textrm{gal/min}$, *reactant* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (14\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{300}\frac{lb}{gal}) \\
&= (14\frac{gal}{min}) \cdot (\frac{Q}{300}\frac{lb}{gal}) \\
&= \frac{14Q}{300}\frac{lb}{min} \\
&= \frac{7Q}{150}\frac{lb}{min}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 0 - \frac{7Q}{150}
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives the equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dfrac{7}{150}\,Q
$$

**Step 4**: Integrate the equation.

Separating variables and integrating both sides with respect to $t$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dt} &= -\frac{7}{150} \\
∫\frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dt}dt &= ∫(-\frac{7}{150})dt \\
∫\frac{1}{Q} \cdot dQ &= -\frac{7}{150}∫1dt \\
\ln \mid Q \mid &= -\frac{7t}{150} + C \\
\mid Q \mid &= e^{-\frac{7t}{150} + C} \\
Q(t) &= Ae^{-7t/150}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $A = \pm e^C$ is a constant of integration.

We're told that the reactor initially contains $18\,\textrm{lb}$ of reactant. So, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0) = 18$, and solve for $A{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 18 \\
Ae^{-7 \cdot 0/150} &= 18 \\
A &= 18
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
Q(t) = 18e^{-7t/150}
$$

Finally, the amount of reactant in the reactor $25$ minutes after the solvent is first pumped in is

$$
Q(25) = 18e^{-175/150} \approx 5.61\,\textrm{lb}
$$

rounded to two decimal places.

<a id="points-of-overflow-and-emptying"></a>
## Points of Overflow and Emptying

Previously, we saw that the volume $V$ of solution at time $t$ in a container with initial volume $V_0$, input rate $e$, and output rate $f$ is given by

$$
V(t) = V_0 + (e - f)t
$$

There are three possible behaviors for the volume depending on the rates:

- The volume *remains constant* when the input rate equals the output rate, $e=f$. In this case, $V(t) = V_0$.
- The volume *decreases* when the output rate is greater than the input rate, $f > e > 0$, and the container begins to empty. Let $t_e$ be the time when the container empties: $V(t_e) = 0$. Solving for $t_e$, we get
$V(t_{e})|= V_{0} + (e - f)t_{e}; 0|= V_{0} + (e - f)t_{e}$.
Solving this equation, we find that the container empties at time $\bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{t_e = \dfrac{V_0}{f - e}}$.

- The volume *increases* when the input rate is greater than the output rate, $e > f > 0$, and the container begins filling until it reaches its capacity $V_\text{cap}$. Let $t_o$ be the time when the container reaches capacity: $V(t_o) = V_\text{cap}$. Solving for $t_o$, we get
$V(t_{o})|= V_{cap}; V_{0} + (e - f)t_{o}|= V_{cap}$.
Solving this equaiton, we find that the container overflows at time $\bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{t_o = \dfrac{V_\text{cap} - V_0}{e - f}}$.

Now, let's apply one of these formulas in a concrete example.

<a id="determining-an-amount-at-an-overflow-point"></a>
## Determining an Amount at an Overflow Point

**Example:** A $12\,000\,\textrm{mL}$-capacity tank initially holds $8\,000\,\textrm{mL}$ of water containing $320\,\textrm{g}$ of dissolved contaminant. Clean water begins to flow into the tank at a rate of $100\,\textrm{mL/s}$, while the completely mixed water flows out at a rate of $60\,\textrm{mL/s}$. Find, rounded to two decimal places, the amount of contaminant in the tank at the moment it begins to overflow.

**Explanation**

Let $Q$ be the amount of contaminant in the tank at time $t$. Then, the derivative

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}
$$

describes how this amount changes with respect to time $t$, in seconds. This is equal to the rate at which contaminant enters the tank minus the rate at which contaminant leaves the tank.

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q'_{\textrm{in}} - Q'_{\textrm{out}}
$$

Note that the units of

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}, Q'_{\textrm{in}}
$$

and $Q'_{\textrm{out}}$ are $\textrm{g}/\textrm{s}$. Now, let's compute the required rates of change in steps.

**Step 1**: Calculating the rate at which contaminant enters the tank.

First, note that clean water (i.e., water containing $0\,\textrm{g/mL}$ of contaminant) enters the tank at a rate of $100\,\textrm{mL/s}$. So, the rate at which the *contaminant* enters the tank is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{in}^{′} &= (0\frac{g}{mL}) \cdot (100\frac{mL}{s}) \\
&= (0\frac{g}{mL}) \cdot (100\frac{mL}{s}) \\
&= 0\frac{g}{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2**: Calculating the rate at which contaminant exits the tank.

To find the rate at which contaminant leaves the tank, we must first determine the volume of water in the tank at time $t$.

The tank initially contains $8\,000\,\textrm{mL}$ of water; clean water enters at $100\,\textrm{mL/s}$, and mixed water leaves at $60\,\textrm{mL/s}$.
As a result, the amount of water in the tank after $t$ seconds is

$$
V(t) = 8\,000 + 100t - 60t = 40(200+t)\,\textrm{mL}
$$

Note that $V(t)$ is an increasing function, so the tank is filling.

Since $Q$ is the amount of contaminant in the tank, the concentration of the water at time $t$ is

$$
\dfrac{Q}{40(200 + t)}\,\dfrac{\textrm{g}}{\textrm{mL}}
$$

Therefore, as the completely mixed water leaves at a rate of $60\,\textrm{mL/s}$, *contaminant* leaves at a rate of

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{out}^{′} &= (60\frac{mL}{s}) \cdot ((Q)/(40(200 + t))\frac{g}{mL}) \\
&= (60\frac{mL}{s}) \cdot ((Q)/(40(200 + t))\frac{g}{mL}) \\
&= (3Q)/(2(200 + t))\frac{g}{s}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3**: Calculate the overall rate of change.

The rate of change equals the rate of entry minus the rate of exit. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dQ}{dt} &= Q_{in}^{′} - Q_{out}^{′} \\
&= 0 - (3Q)/(2(200 + t))
\end{aligned}
$$

which gives the equation

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\dfrac{3}{2(200 + t)}\,Q
$$

**Step 4**: Integrate the equation

Separating variables and integrating both sides with respect to $t$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dt} &= - (3)/(2(200 + t)) \\
∫\frac{1}{Q} \cdot \frac{dQ}{dt}dt &= ∫(- (3)/(2(200 + t)))dt \\
∫\frac{1}{Q} \cdot dQ &= -\frac{3}{2}∫\frac{1}{200 + t}dt \\
\ln\begin{vmatrix}Q & &= -\frac{3}{2}(\ln \mid 200 + t \mid) + C \\ \ln & Q\end{vmatrix}|= \ln \mid (200 + t)^{-3/2} \mid + C \\
\begin{vmatrix}Q & &= e^{\ln \mid (200 + t)^{-3/2}\end{vmatrix}+ C} \\
Q(t) &= (A)/((200 + t)^{3/2})
\end{aligned}
$$

where $A = \pm e^C$ is a constant of integration.

We're told that the tank initially contains $320\,\textrm{g}$ of contaminant. So, we can apply the initial condition $Q(0) = 320$, and solve for $A{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(0) &= 320 \\
(A)/((200 + 0)^{3/2}) &= 320 \\
A &= 320(200)^{3/2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we obtain the following expression for $Q(t){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(t) &= (320(200)^{3/2})/((200 + t)^{3/2}) \\
&= 320(\frac{200}{200 + t})^{3/2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let $t_o$ be the time when the tank begins to overflow. The tank has a capacity of $12\,000\,\textrm{mL}$, so we can use the condition

$$
V(t_o) = 12\,000
$$

to solve for $t_o{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(t_{o}) &= 12000 \\
40(200 + t_{o}) &= 12000 \\
200 + t_{o} &= 300 \\
t_{o} &= 100s
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the amount of contaminant in the tank at the moment it begins to overflow is

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(100) &= 320(\frac{200}{200 + 100})^{3/2} \\
&= 320(\frac{2}{3})^{3/2} \\
&\approx 174.19g
\end{aligned}
$$
