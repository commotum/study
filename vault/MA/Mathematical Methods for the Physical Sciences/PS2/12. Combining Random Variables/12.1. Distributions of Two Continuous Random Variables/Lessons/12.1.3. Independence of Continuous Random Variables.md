## Introduction

Let $X$ and $Y$ be continuous random variables. We say that $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if, for all $A\subseteq \mathbb R$ and $B\subseteq \mathbb R$, we have

$$
P(X\in A, Y\in B)=P(X\in A)\cdot P(Y\in B)
$$

In practice, this is not a very helpful definition when checking for independence since it involves verifying that it's true for all possible subsets $A$ and $B$!

Now suppose that $X$ and $Y$ have the joint probability density function $f(x,y)$, and $f_X(x)$ and $f_Y(y)$ are the marginal density functions of $X$ and $Y$, respectively. Then, it can be shown that $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if

$$
f(x, y) = f_X(x)\cdot f_Y (y)
$$

for all values of $x$ and $y$. Notice that this has a direct analogy with the case of independence for discrete random variables.

## Finding the Joint PDF of Two Independent Random Variables Using Marginal PDFs

**Example:** Let $X$ and $Y$ be two independent continuous random variables whose marginal density functions are as follows:

${\frac{2}{9}x,\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 3, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$

Find the joint probability density function $f(x,y)$.

**Explanation**

Two continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint probability density function $f(x,y)$ are independent if and only if

$$
f(x, y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y (y)
$$

for all possible values $x$ and $y$, where $f_X(x)$ and $f_Y (y)$ are the marginal probability density functions for $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

Since our random variables are independent, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x, y) &= f_{X}(x) \cdot f_{Y}(y) \\
&= \frac{2}{9}x \cdot \frac{1}{4}y^{3} \\
&= \frac{1}{18}xy^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $(x,y) \in [0, 3] \times [0, 2]$. Therefore, the full expression for $f(x,y)$ is

$$
{\frac{1}{18}xy^{3},\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 3, 0 \le y \le 2 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

## Finding a Joint Probability for Some Independent Random Variables Using Marginal PDFs

**Example:** Let $X$ and $Y$ be two independent continuous random variables whose marginal density functions are as follows:

${\frac{10}{3x^{2}},\begin{vmatrix}2 \le x \le 5, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$

Find $P(X \geq 3, Y \leq 2)$.

**Explanation**

Two continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint probability density function $f(x,y)$ are independent if and only if

$$
f(x, y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y (y)
$$

for all possible values $x$ and $y$, where $f_X(x)$ and $f_Y (y)$ are the marginal probability density functions for $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

Since our random variables are independent, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x, y) &= f_{X}(x) \cdot f_{Y}(y) \\
&= \frac{10}{3x^{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{4}y \\
&= \frac{5y}{6x^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
(x,y) \in \left[2,5\right]\times \left[1,3\right]
$$

Therefore, the full expression for $f(x,y)$ is

$$
{\frac{5y}{6x^{2}},\begin{vmatrix}2 \le x \le 5, 1 \le y \le 3 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Finally, the required probability is

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X \ge 3, Y \le 2) &= ∫_{3}^{5}[∫_{1}^{2}\frac{5y}{6x^{2}}dy]dx \\
&= ∫_{3}^{5}\frac{5}{6x^{2}}[\frac{y^{2}}{2}]_{1}^{2}dx \\
&= ∫_{3}^{5}\frac{5}{6x^{2}}(\frac{3}{2})dx \\
&= ∫_{3}^{5}\frac{5}{4x^{2}}dx \\
&= [- \frac{5}{4x}]_{3}^{5} \\
&=-\frac{5}{4}(\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{3}) \\
&= \frac{1}{6}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Determining Whether Two Continuous Random Variables are Independent

**Example:** The joint probability density function for the continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given by

${\frac{1}{4}xe^{-y/2},\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 2, y \ge 0, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$.

Which of the following statements are true?

1. $f_Y(y) = \dfrac{1}{2}e^{-y/2}$ for $[MATH: y\in \left[0,\infty\right)]$
2. $f(x, y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y (y)$ for all possible $x$ and $y$
3. $X$ and $Y$ are independent

**Explanation**

Two continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ with a joint probability density function $f(x,y)$ are independent if and only if

$$
f(x, y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y (y)
$$

for all possible values $x$ and $y$, where $f_X(x)$ and $f_Y (y)$ are the marginal probability density functions for $X$ and $Y$, respectively.

With that in mind, let's examine our statements.

- Statement I is true. Computing the marginal density function for $Y$, we get
$f_{Y}(y)|= ∫_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{4}xe^{-y/2}dx; = \frac{1}{4}e^{-y/2}[\frac{x^{2}}{2}]_{0}^{2}; = \frac{1}{2}e^{-y/2}$,
where $[MATH: y \in \left[0,\infty\right).]$
- Statement II is true. Computing the marginal density function for $X$, we get
$f_{X}(x)|= ∫_{0}^{∞}\frac{1}{4}xe^{-y/2}dy; = \frac{1}{4}xlim_(b → ∞)∫_{0}^{b}e^{-y/2}dy; = \frac{1}{4}xlim_(b → ∞)[- 2e^{-y/2}]_{0}^{b}; = \frac{1}{4}xlim_(b → ∞)[- 2e^{-b/2} + 2]; = \frac{x}{2}$,
where $x \in \left[0,2\right]$. So, when $x \in \left[0,2\right]$ and $[MATH: y\in \left[0,\infty\right),]$ we obtain that
$\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{4}xe^{-y/2}}_{\large f(x,y)} = \underbrace{\Big(\dfrac{x}{2}\Big)}_{\large f_X(x)} \cdot \underbrace{\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}e^{-y/2}\Big)}_{\large f_Y(y)}$.
- Statement III is true. Since statement II is true, our random variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I, II, and III."

## Independence When the Joint Support Is Rectangular

For continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ whose joint support $S$ is rectangular, we have the following theorem:

> *Suppose that the joint support of two continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ with a joint probability density function $f(x,y)$ is rectangular. If
> $f(x,y) = g(x)\cdot h(y)$
> for some two functions $g$ and $h$ (not necessarily probability density functions), then $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Note that the rectangular joint support $S$ does not need to be finite.*

For example, let's consider the random variables $X$ and $Y$ with the following joint PDF:

$$
{\frac{1}{4}xe^{-y/2},\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 2, y \ge 0, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

The joint support $[MATH: [0,2]\times [0, \infty)]$ is rectangular. Also, we can write

$$
f(x,y) = g(x)\cdot h(y)
$$

where

$$
g(x) = \dfrac14 x, \qquad h(y) = e^{-y/2}
$$

Therefore, $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
