## Introduction

A set of vectors

$$
{U}=\{\mathbf{u}_1, \cdots, \mathbf{u}_p \}
$$

in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an **orthogonal set** if each pair of distinct vectors from the set is orthogonal.

For example, let's consider the following set:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

If we denote the vectors in our set ${W}$ as $\{\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \mathbf{w}_3 \}$, we can check that ${W}$ is orthogonal by showing that

$$
{w_{1} \cdot w_{2} = 0; w_{1} \cdot w_{3} = 0; w_{2} \cdot w_{3} = 0
$$

Let's compute each dot product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}w_{1} \cdot w_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 = 0 & ✓ \\ w_{1} \cdot w_{3}|= \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -4 \\ 7\end{bmatrix} = 3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot (-4) + 1 \cdot 7 = 0 & ✓ \\ w_{2} \cdot w_{3}|= \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -4 \\ 7\end{bmatrix} = (-1) \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot (-4) + 1 \cdot 7 = 0 & ✓\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So indeed, ${W}$ is an orthogonal set.

If an orthogonal set is also a basis, then we call it an **orthogonal basis**. Here, $W$ is an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, because it contains three linearly independent (you can check this) orthogonal vectors.

## Determining Whether Sets are Orthogonal

**Example:** Determine whether the following set is orthogonal:
$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

If we denote the vectors in our set ${X}$ as $\{\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_3 \}$, we can check that ${X}$ is orthogonal by showing that

$$
{x_{1} \cdot x_{2} = 0; x_{1} \cdot x_{3} = 0; x_{2} \cdot x_{3} = 0
$$

Let's compute each dot product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \cdot x_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = 2 \cdot (-1) + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 2 = 0 & ✓ \\ x_{1} \cdot x_{3}|= \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = 2 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-2) + 1 \cdot 0 = 2 \ne 0 & \cdot \\ x_{2} \cdot x_{3}|= \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = (-1) \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 0 =-1 \ne 0 & \cdot\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So ${X}$ is *not* an orthogonal set.

## Orthonormal Sets and Orthonormal Bases

A set of vectors

$$
{U}=\{\mathbf{u}_1, \cdots, \mathbf{u}_p \}
$$

is an **orthonormal set** if it is an orthogonal set of *unit* vectors. Remember that $\mathbf u_i$ is a unit vector if

$$
\Vert \mathbf u_i \Vert = 1
$$

or equivalently,

$$
\mathbf u_i \cdot \mathbf u_i = 1
$$

An orthonormal set that is also a basis, like the standard basis, is called an **orthonormal basis**. For instance, the standard basis $\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \mathbf{e}_3\}$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is an orthonormal basis because the vectors are orthogonal to each other and each vector in the set is a unit vector.

There are two more things to note about orthogonal sets:

- Any orthogonal set of non-zero vectors is linearly independent.
- An orthogonal set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ cannot contain more than $n$ vectors. More precisely, if $V = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p\}$ is an orthogonal set of non-zero vectors and $\mathbf{v}_i\in\mathbb{R}^n$, then we must have that $p\leq n$.

**Note:** To remember that orthogonal set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ cannot contain more than $n$ vectors, you can use the following intuitive mnemonic. There are only $n$ dimensions in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and each orthogonal vector has a $1$-dimensional span, meaning that it "takes up" $1$ dimension of $\mathbb{R}^n$. So, you can't "fit" more than $n$ orthogonal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

<a id="determining-whether-sets-are-orthonormal"></a>
## Determining Whether Sets are Orthonormal

**Example:** Determine whether the following set is orthonormal:
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of the given set as $\{\mathbf{w}_1,\mathbf{w}_2\}$, respectively.

The set $W$ is orthonormal if the following conditions hold:

$$
{w_{1} \cdot w_{1} = 1; w_{2} \cdot w_{2} = 1
$$

With that in mind, let's examine our set:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}w_{1} \cdot w_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 0 & ✓ \\ w_{1} \cdot w_{1}|= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1 & ✓ \\ w_{2} \cdot w_{2}|= \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} = 16 + 16 = 32 \ne 1 & \cdot\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

We see that $\mathbf w_1$ and $\mathbf w_2$ are orthogonal, and $\mathbf w_1$ is a unit vector, but $\mathbf w_2$ is *not* a unit vector.

Therefore, ${W}$ is *not* an orthonormal set.

<a id="extending-a-set-to-form-an-orthogonal-basis"></a>
## Extending a Set to Form an Orthogonal Basis

**Example:** Consider the orthogonal set $U$ below. By adding a new vector $\mathbf x = [x_1,\,x_2,\,x_3]^T$, extend the set so that it forms an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of ${U}$ as $\mathbf{u}_1,\, \mathbf{u}_2$. To extend the given set to an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, we need to find a third vector $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$ such that

$$
{u_{1} \cdot x = 0; u_{2} \cdot x = 0
$$

So, we consider the augmented matrix $M$ of the system above and reduce it to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, there are two pivot columns ($1$st and $3$rd). This means $x_2$ is a free variable. From the second equation, we get $x_3=0$. Substituting this into the first equation, we obtain

$$
-x_{1} - x_{2} + 2(0) = 0⟹x_{1} =-x_{2}
$$

So, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}-x_{2} \\ x_{2} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

Setting $x_2=1$, we obtain the vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

## Extending a Set to Form an Orthonormal Basis

**Example:** Consider the **orthonormal** set $U$ below. By adding a new vector $\mathbf x = [x_1,\,x_2,\,x_3]^T$, extend the set so that it forms an **orthonormal** basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
$\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of ${U}$ as $\mathbf{u}_1,\, \mathbf{u}_2$. To extend the given set to an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, we need to find a third vector $\mathbf{x}$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\| \mathbf{x} \ &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
{u_{1} \cdot x = 0; u_{2} \cdot x = 0
$$

So, we consider the augmented matrix $M$ of the system above and reduce it to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{2}{\sqrt{6}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{1}:=\sqrt{2} \cdot R_{1} \\ R_{2}:=\sqrt{6} \cdot R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, there are two pivot columns ($1$st and $2$nd). This means $x_3$ is a free variable. From the second equation, we get $x_2=-x_3$, and from the first equation, we obtain $x_1=x_3$.

So, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{3} \\ -x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Setting $x_3=1$, we obtain the vector
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Now, we need to normalize $\mathbf{x}$. Notice that

$$
\| \mathbf{x} \| = \sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2+1^2} = \sqrt{3}
$$

So, we normalize $\mathbf x$ as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}
$$
