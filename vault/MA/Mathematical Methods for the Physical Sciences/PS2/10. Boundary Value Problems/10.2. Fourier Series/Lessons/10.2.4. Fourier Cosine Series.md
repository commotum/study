## Introduction

In the earlier topics, we saw that *symmetry* can force many Fourier coefficients to be zero automatically.
In particular, if a $2\pi$-periodic function is *even*, then its Fourier series has *cosine terms only*.

A **Fourier cosine series** uses this idea when a function is initially given only on the half-interval $[MATH: [0,\pi),]$ but we want a full Fourier series on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ (and then a $2\pi$-periodic extension to all real $x$).

To do this, we start with a function $F(x)$ on $[MATH: [0,\pi)]$ and define its **even extension** $f(x)$ to $(-\pi,\pi)$ by
$[MATH: {F(x), \mid x ∈ [0, π),; F(-x), \mid x ∈ (- π, 0).]$

For example, a function $F(x)$, defined on $[MATH: x \in [0,1),]$ and its even extension $f(x)$ to $x \in (-1,1)$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/10.2.4. Fourier Cosine Series/Images/a60be008a552f0c8de9c07a97fc1b3be.png>)

By construction, $f$ is an *even* function, meaning

$$
f(-x)=f(x)
$$

. Because $f$ is even, its Fourier series on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ has *cosine terms only*:

$$
f(x)\sim \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\cos(nx)
$$

Since $f(x)$ is even and $\cos(nx)$ is even for any

$$
n\ge 1
$$

the product $f(x)\cos(nx)$ is even too. Therefore, the cosine coefficients can be computed using the *half-range formulas*:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{0} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{- π}^{π}f(x)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{π} \cdot 2\int_{0}^{π}f(x)dx \\
&= \frac{2}{π}\int_{0}^{π}f(x)dx
\end{aligned}
$$

and for

$$
n\ge 1
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{- π}^{π}f(x)\cos (nx)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{π} \cdot 2\int_{0}^{π}f(x)\cos (nx)dx \\
&= \frac{2}{π}\int_{0}^{π}f(x)\cos (nx)dx
\end{aligned}
$$

So in practice, the workflow is the following:

1. Start with $F$ on $[MATH: [0,\pi)]$ and form the even extension $f$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$.
2. Compute $a_0$ and $a_n$ from the half-range integrals on $[MATH: [0,\pi).]$
3. Write the Fourier cosine series of $f$.

Let's see some concrete examples.

## Understanding the Even Extension of a Function

**Example:** Let $F(x)=x^3 - x + 5\cos x$ for $[MATH: x\in[0,\pi),]$ and let $f(x)$ be the **even** extension of $F(x)$ to $x \in (-\pi,\pi)$. What is $f(x)$ for $x\in(-\pi,0)$?

**Explanation**

If $F(x)$ is defined on $[MATH: x\in[0,\pi),]$ the even extension of $F$ is the function $f$ such that
$[MATH: {F(x), \mid x ∈ [0, π),; F(-x), \mid x ∈ (- π, 0).]$

In our cases, for $x\in(-\pi,0)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= F(-x) \\
&= (-x)^{3} - (-x) + 5\cos (-x) \\
&=-x^{3} + x + 5\cos x
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Constant Term From the Half-Range Formula

**Example:** $f(x) \sim \dfrac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n \cos(nx)$
Let $F(x)=\sin\left(\dfrac{3x}{4}\right)$ for $[MATH: x\in[0,\pi).]$ The Fourier cosine series of the **even** $2\pi$-periodic extension $f(x)$ of $F(x)$ is shown above. Find the expression for $a_0$.

**Explanation**

For our Fourier cosine series, the constant term is given by

$$
a_0=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi f(x)\,\text{d}x
$$

In our case,

$$
f(x)=\sin\left(\dfrac{3x}{4}\right)
$$

for $x \in (0,\pi)$. So, substituting into the formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{0} &= \frac{2}{π}\int_{0}^{π}\sin (\frac{3x}{4})dx \\
&= \frac{2}{π}[- \frac{4}{3}\cos (\frac{3x}{4})]_{0}^{π} \\
&=-\frac{8}{3π}(\cos (\frac{3π}{4}) - \cos (0)) \\
&=-\frac{8}{3π}(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - 1) \\
&= 4 3 π (2 + 2)
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Cosine Coefficients From the Half-Range Formula

**Example:** Let $F(x)=-6x-2$ for $[MATH: x\in[0,\pi).]$ Find the missing parts in the Fourier cosine series of the **even** $2\pi$-periodic extension $f(x)$ of the function $F(x)$.

$XX_{X}^{X}$

**Explanation**

For our Fourier cosine series, the coefficients are given by

$$
a_n=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi f(x)\cos(nx)\,\text{d}x
$$

In our case,

$$
f(x)=-6x-2
$$

for $x \in (0,\pi)$. So, substituting into the formula, we get

$$
a_n = \dfrac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi (-6x-2) \cos(nx)\,\text{d}x
$$

We use the integration by parts:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u =-6x - 2 & ⇒u^{′} =-6 \\ v^{′} = \cos (nx) & ⇒v = \frac{1}{n}\sin (nx)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= \left.\frac{2}{π}(uv\right|_{0}^{π} - \int_{0}^{π}vu^{′}dx) \\
&= \frac{2}{π}(\frac{1}{n}[(-6x - 2)\sin (nx)]_{0}^{π} + \frac{6}{n}\int_{0}^{π}\sin (nx)dx) \\
&= \frac{2}{nπ}(((-6π - 2)\sin (nπ) + 2\sin (0)) - \frac{6}{n}[\cos (nx)]_{0}^{π}) \\
&= \frac{2}{nπ}(0 - \frac{6}{n}(\cos (nπ) - \cos (0))) \\
&= (12(1 - \cos (nπ)))/(n^{2}π)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, since

$$
\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n
$$

we have

$$
a_n = \dfrac{12(1-(-1)^n)}{\pi n^2}
$$

Finally, the Fourier cosine series is

$$
f(x) \sim (-2-3\pi) + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt gainsboro]{\dfrac{12(1-(-1)^n)}{\pi n^2}} \cdot \bbox[4pt, border: solid 1pt gainsboro]{\cos(nx)}
$$
