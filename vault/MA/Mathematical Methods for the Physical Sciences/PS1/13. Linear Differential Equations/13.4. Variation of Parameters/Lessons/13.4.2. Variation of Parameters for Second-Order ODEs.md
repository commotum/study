## Introduction

In this topic, we'll learn how to apply the method of *variation of parameters* (VOP) to second-order linear differential equations. This is a powerful method because it applies to any second-order linear equation with continuous coefficients and is especially helpful when finding a particular solution is difficult using other methods.

To motivate the discussion, let's start by reviewing the variation of parameters method for first-order equations:

$$
y'(x) + p(x)y(x) = q(x)
$$

In general, its solution has the form

$$
y(x) = y_c(x) + y_p(x)
$$

where the complementary solution $y_c(x)$ is the general solution of the homogeneous equation

$$
y'_c(x) + p(x)y_c(x) = 0
$$

which can be written as

$$
y_c(x) = A e^{-\int p(x) \, \textrm{d}x}
$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant.

As we've seen, to seek a particular solution $y_p(x)$ using variation of parameters, we allow the constant $A$ to vary. Thus, in our particular solution, we replace the constant $A$ with a function $u(x){:}$

$$
y_p(x) = u(x) e^{-\int p(x)\,\textrm{d}x}
$$

Substituting this ansatz into the original equation and solving the resulting differential equation for $u(x)$ yields the required particular solution.

We can apply VOP to second-order (and even higher-order) equations; the general idea is the same, but the situation is more complex because the complementary solution contains *two* arbitrary constants, not one! Thus, the variation of parameters ansatz will contain *two* unknown functions, and we will need additional conditions to determine them.

Before we dive into the second-order case, suppose we denote the function $y_1$ as

$$
y_1 = e^{-\int p(x)\,\textrm{d}x}
$$

Then, we can write our variation of parameters ansatz as

$$
y_p = u_1(x) y_1(x)
$$

The function $y_1$ is a fundamental solution of the homogeneous equation. Since it is nonzero for all $x$, it spans the solution space of the first-order homogeneous equation. In other words, the set
$S = \{y_1\}$
is (trivially) linearly independent.

As we'll see, thinking of the variation of parameters ansatz in terms of products of unknown functions and *linearly independent* fundamental solutions will help us when we apply variation of parameters to higher-order equations.

Let's now consider a concrete example.

---

<a id="variation-of-parameters-for-second-order-equations"></a>
## Variation of Parameters for Second-Order Equations

Now, consider the second-order linear inhomogeneous equation

$$
y'' - 3y' + 2y = f(x)
$$

where we assume that the forcing function $f$ is not identically zero.

The corresponding homogeneous equation is

$$
y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0
$$

Substituting

$$
y = e^{\lambda x}
$$

into the equation and solving the resulting quadratic in $\lambda$, we find that the complementary solution is

$$
y_c(x) = Ae^x + Be^{2x}
$$

where $A$ and $B$ are constants, and the fundamental solutions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are

$$
y_1(x) = e^{x}, \qquad y_2(x) = e^{2x}
$$

Note that the Wronskian

$$
\begin{bmatrix}e^{x} & e^{2x} \\ (e^{x})^{′} & (e^{2x})^{′}\end{bmatrix}
$$

is nonzero for all $x$, and so the set

$$
S = \{y_1, y_2\}
$$

is a linearly independent set of fundamental solutions.

Now, suppose we wish to seek a particular solution using variation of parameters. By allowing the constants $A$ and $B$ to vary, we can introduce new functions $u_1(x)$ and $u_2(x)$, and seek a particular solution of the form

$$
y_p(x) = u_1y_1+ u_2y_2
$$

But we have a problem! Differentiating

$$
y_p=u_1y_1+u_2y_2
$$

we get terms involving $u'_1$ and $u'_2$. Substituting into the ODE produces a single equation involving both $u'_1$ and $u'_2$, so we need a second equation.

To help with this issue, we will seek a solution that satisfies the following constraint:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}^{′} &= u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}
\end{aligned}
$$

This constraint for $y_p'$ does **not** come from differentiating $y_p$. Rather, it is a condition that we force so that we obtain a solvable system of equations. We'll go over its justification in more detail at the end of the lesson.

Let's go ahead and summarize everything we talked about, as well as a strategy for finding the unknown functions.

---

<a id="a-summary"></a>
## A Summary

We wish to find a particular solution $y_p$ of the equation

$$
y'' - 3y' + 2y = f(x)
$$

with the fundamental solutions

$$
y_1(x) = e^{x}, \qquad y_2(x) = e^{2x}
$$

Using variation of parameters, we seek functions $u_1(x)$ and $u_2(x)$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p} &= u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2} \\
y_{p}^{′} &= u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let's outline our strategy for calculating $u_1$ and $u_2$.

- **Step 1**: To ensure the expression for $y_p'$ above is valid, we must first impose the condition $u_1' y_1 + u_2' y_2 = 0$.
- **Step 2**: Next, we differentiate $y_p'$ to find $y_p''$, substitute it into the differential equation, and simplify. This yields the second condition $u_1' y_1' + u_2' y_2' = f(x)$.
- **Step 3**: We solve the resulting system of two equations for the derivatives $u_1'$ and $u_2'$, then integrate to find $u_1$ and $u_2$.

In this lesson, we will practice each step of the process.

Let's begin by seeing an example where we derive the system of equations for $u_1'$ and $u_2'$.

$$
\begin{aligned}

\end{aligned}
$$

$$

$$

Note that the expressions in parentheses are both zero. Therefore, the equation reduces to

$$
u_1'e^{x} - 6u_2'e^{-6x} = p(x)

## Finding a Second Condition

Let's remind ourselves of our strategy for calculating $u_1$ and $u_2$.

- **Step 1**: First, we substitute the above expressions for $y_p$ and $y_p'$ into the differential equation. This will give one relationship between $u_1$ and $u_2.\:{\color{green}{\checkmark}}$
- **Step 2**: Next, we differentiate $y_p$ and equate the resulting expression to our constraint for $y_p'$. This will give a second relationship between $u_1$ and $u_2$.
- **Step 3**: Now that we have two equations for the two unknown functions, we solve the system to yield expressions for $u_1$ and $u_2$.

Now that we've completed step 1 in some cases, let's go ahead and get some practice with step 2.

For example, consider the differential equation

$$
y'' + y' - 6y = xe^{5x}
$$

for which the corresponding homogeneous equation has the fundamental solutions

$$
y_1(x) = e^{2x}
$$

and

$$
y_2(x) = e^{-3x}
$$

Given a second-order linear inhomogeneous differential equation, the variation of parameters method requires that we seek a particular solution of the form

$$
y_p(x) = u_1(x)y_1(x)+u_2(x)y_2(x)
$$

that satisfies the constraint

$$
y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2 y_2'
$$

Here, $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equation, and $u_1$ and $u_2$ are functions to be determined.

In this case, we're told that the fundamental solutions are

$$
y_1 = e^{2x}, \qquad y_2 = e^{-3x}
$$

Substituting the fundamental solutions into our expression for $y_p$, we get

$$
y_p = u_1e^{2x}+u_2e^{-3x}
$$

Substituting the fundamental solutions into our constraint equation for $y_p'$, we get

$$
y_p' = u_1(e^{2x})'+u_2 (e^{-3x})'
$$

We can form a constraint that relates $u_1'$ and $u_2'$ by differentiating $y_p$ and equating this expression to our constraint on $y_p'$.

Differentiating $y_p$ with respect to $x$ using the product rule, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}^{′} &= (u_{1}e^{2x} + u_{2}e^{-3x})^{′} \\
&= (u_{1}e^{2x})^{′} + (u_{2}e^{-3x})^{′} \\
&= u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + u_{1}(e^{2x})^{′} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-3x} + u_{2}(e^{-3x})^{′} \\
&= u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + 2u_{1}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-3x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we equate our two expressions for $y_p'$, and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + 2u_{1}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-3x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x} &= u_{1}(e^{2x})^{′} + u_{2}(e^{-3x})^{′} \\
u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + 2u_{1}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-3x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x} &= 2u_{1}e^{2x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x} \\
u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + 2u_{1}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-3x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x} &= 2u_{1}e^{2x} - 3u_{2}e^{-3x}
\end{aligned}
$$

which finally gives

$$
u_1' e^{2x} + u_2' e^{-3x} = 0
$$

$$
y_p(x) = u_1(x)y_1(x)+u_2(x)y_2(x)
$$

that satisfies the constraint

$$
y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2 y_2'
$$

Here, $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equation, and $u_1$ and $u_2$ are functions to be determined.

$$
y_1 = \sin(4x), \qquad y_2 = \cos(4x)

$$
y_p =u_1\sin(4x)+u_2\cos(4x)
$$

$$
y_p' = u_1(\sin(4x))'+u_2 (\cos(4x))'

$$
\begin{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}

<a id="the-variation-of-parameters-method"></a>
## The Variation of Parameters Method

Now, we’ll apply the method of variation of parameters from start to finish to see the full picture.

Consider the differential equation

$$
y'' - 4y = 4e^{2x}
$$

for which the corresponding homogeneous equation has the fundamental solutions

$$
y_1 = e^{2x}
$$

and

$$
y_2 = e^{-2x}
$$

Let’s work through this example using the method of variation of parameters.

We seek a particular solution of the form

$$
y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2
$$

subject to the standard constraints:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} = 4e^{2x}; u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} = 0
$$

First, let's verify that the fundamental solutions $y_1$ and $y_2$ are independent by computing the Wronskian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{1}^{′}y_{2} \\
&= (e^{2x})(e^{-2x})^{′} - (e^{2x})^{′}(e^{-2x}) \\
&= (e^{2x})(-2e^{-2x}) - (2e^{2x})(e^{-2x}) \\
&=-2 - 2 \\
&=-4 \\
&\ne 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
W \neq 0
$$

the functions are linearly independent.

Substituting $y_1, y_2$ and their derivatives into our constraints gives the system:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}(e^{2x})^{′} + u_{2}^{′}(e^{-2x})^{′} = 4e^{2x}; u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-2x} = 0⟹{2u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} - 2u_{2}^{′}e^{-2x} = 4e^{2x}; u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + u_{2}^{′}e^{-2x} = 0
$$

We start by solving for $u_2'$ from the second equation to get

$$
u_2' = -\dfrac{u_1'e^{2x}}{e^{-2x}}=-u_1'e^{4x}
$$

Substituting this into the first equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
2u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} - 2u_{2}^{′}e^{-2x} &= 4e^{2x} \\
2u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} - 2(-u_{1}^{′}e^{4x})e^{-2x} &= 4e^{2x} \\
2u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} + 2u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} &= 4e^{2x} \\
4u_{1}^{′}e^{2x} &= 4e^{2x} \\
u_{1}^{′} &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, we have

$$
u_2' = -(1)e^{4x} = -e^{4x}
$$

Next, we integrate $u_1'$ and $u_2'$ with respect to $x$ (taking constants as $0$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1} &= ∫u_{1}^{′}dx = ∫1dx = x, \\
u_{2} &= ∫u_{2}^{′}dx = ∫ - e^{4x}dx =-\frac{1}{4}e^{4x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, our particular solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p} &= u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2} \\
&= (x)e^{2x} + (-\frac{1}{4}e^{4x})e^{-2x} \\
&= xe^{2x} - \frac{1}{4}e^{2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

The general solution is

$$
y = y_c + y_p
$$

where

$$
y_c = C_1 e^{2x} + C_2 e^{-2x}
$$

So,

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= (C_{1}e^{2x} + C_{2}e^{-2x}) + (xe^{2x} - \frac{1}{4}e^{2x}) \\
&= (C_{1} - \frac{1}{4})e^{2x} + C_{2}e^{-2x} + xe^{2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, replacing constants

$$
C_1 - \dfrac{1}{4}
$$

with $A$ and $C_2$ with $B$, we get

$$
\bbox[4pt,border: 1px solid lightgray]{y(x) = A e^{2x} + B e^{-2x} + xe^{2x}}
$$

where $A$ and $B$ are arbitrary constants.

## Applying Variation of Parameters to a Second-Order Inhomogeneous ODE

**Example:** Consider the differential equation
$y'' + 9y = 6\cos x$.
The corresponding homogeneous equation has the fundamental solutions $y_1(x) = \cos(3x)$ and $y_2 = \sin(3x)$.

According to the method of variation of parameters, we should seek a particular solution
$y_p(x) = u_1(x) y_1 + u_2(x) y_2$
that satisfies the following constraints:
${u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} = 6\cos x; u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} = 0$

Given that $\displaystyle u_2(x) = \int g(x)\,\textrm d x$, find the function $g$.

**Explanation**

We notice that the forcing term is

$$
f(x) = 6\cos x
$$

Additionally, we're given the fundamental solutions

$$
y_1(x) = \cos(3x), \qquad y_2(x) = \sin(3x)
$$

First, let's verify that the fundamental solutions $y_1$ and $y_2$ are independent by computing the Wronskian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{1}^{′}y_{2} \\
&= (\cos (3x))(\sin (3x))^{′} - (\cos (3x))^{′}(\sin (3x)) \\
&= (\cos (3x))(3\cos (3x)) - (-3\sin (3x))(\sin (3x)) \\
&= 3\cos^{2} (3x) + 3\sin^{2} (3x) \\
&= 3 \ne 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the Wronskian is not equal to zero, this confirms that the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are linearly independent.

Now, we have the following constraints:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}(\cos (3x))^{′} + u_{2}^{′}(\sin (3x))^{′} = 6\cos x; u_{1}^{′}\cos (3x) + u_{2}^{′}\sin (3x) = 0⟹{-3u_{1}^{′}\sin (3x) + 3u_{2}^{′}\cos (3x) = 6\cos x; u_{1}^{′}\cos (3x) + u_{2}^{′}\sin (3x) = 0
$$

We wish to find an integral expression for $u_2$. We start by eliminating $u_1'$ from the second equation:

$$
u_1' = -\dfrac{u_2'\sin(3x)}{\cos(3x)}
$$

Substituting this into the first equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
-3u_{1}^{′}\sin (3x) + 3u_{2}^{′}\cos (3x) &= 6\cos x \\
- 3(- (u_{2}^{′}\sin (3x))/(\cos (3x)))\sin (3x) + 3u_{2}^{′}\cos (3x) &= 6\cos x \\
3u_{2}^{′}(\sin^{2} (3x))/(\cos (3x)) + 3u_{2}^{′}\cos (3x) &= 6\cos x \\
3u_{2}^{′}(\sin^{2} (3x) + \cos^{2} (3x))/(\cos (3x)) &= 6\cos x \\
3u_{2}^{′}(1)/(\cos (3x)) &= 6\cos x \\
u_{2}^{′} &= 2\cos x\cos (3x)
\end{aligned}
$$

Integrating both sides with respect to $x$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫u_{2}^{′}dx &= ∫2\cos x\cos (3x)dx \\
u_{2} &= ∫2\cos x\cos (3x)dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we conclude that

$$
g(x) = 2\cos x \cos(3x)
$$

<a id="justifying-the-derivative-condition"></a>
## Justifying the Derivative Condition

In variation of parameters for a second-order linear equation, we seek a particular solution of the form

$$
y_p = u_1y_1 + u_2y_2
$$

where $y_1$ and $y_2$ are linearly independent solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equation, and $u_1$ and $u_2$ are unknown functions.

At first, it may seem natural to differentiate $y_p$ directly:

$$
y_p' = (u_1y_1+u_2y_2)' = u_1'y_1 + u_1y_1' + u_2'y_2 + u_2y_2'
$$

This expression contains the *extra terms* $u_1'y_1$ and $u_2'y_2$, which will also appear (after differentiating again) in the expression for $y_p''$.

If we substitute $y=y_p$ into the differential equation, we will obtain *one* equation involving *two* unknown derivatives $u_1'$ and $u_2'$, so we need a second equation to determine them.

To create a second equation, we *choose* to impose the condition

$$
u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0
$$

This is not a property of $u_1$ and $u_2$ that we already know, but it is an additional constraint that we enforce to make the system solvable.

Now, observe what happens to the formula for $y_p'$. Starting from the product rule expansion,

$$
y_p' = u_1'y_1 + u_1y_1' + u_2'y_2 + u_2y_2'
$$

and using the constraint

$$
u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0
$$

we obtain

$$
y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2y_2'
$$

So, the commonly stated "derivative condition"

$$
y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2y_2'
$$

is simply the product-rule derivative of $y_p$ *after* we impose

$$
u_1'y_1+u_2'y_2=0
$$

This constraint has two key benefits:

1. *It produces a solvable system.* Substituting $y_p$ into the differential equation gives one equation in $u_1'$ and $u_2'$. The constraint $u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2=0$ gives a second equation.
2. *It simplifies the computation of $y_p''$.* Since the $u_1'y_1$ and $u_2'y_2$ terms vanish, the expression for $y_p''$ becomes simpler, and the final system for $u_1'$ and $u_2'$ is easier to solve.

This is why variation of parameters uses the constraint

$$
u_1'y_1 + u_2'y_2 = 0
$$

and why it leads to the simplified expression

$$
y_p' = u_1y_1' + u_2y_2'
$$
