## Introduction

In this topic, we'll derive a formula for variation of parameters for second-order linear ODEs.

Suppose we're given the differential equation

$$
y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = f(x)
$$

and let $y_1$ and $y_2$ be fundamental solutions of the corresponding homogeneous equation.

Recall that the method of variation of parameters begins by assuming that a particular solution satisfies the following constraints:

$$
{y_{p} = u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2}; y_{p}^{′} = u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}
$$

Using these constraints, we will set up a system of two equations that we can solve for $u_1$ and $u_2$.

- To obtain the first equation, we differentiate the constraint for $y_p$ given above,
$y_{p}^{′}|= (u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2})^{′}; = u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}$,
and since we assumed that $y_p' = u_1 y_1' + u_2 y_2'$, we reach
$u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}|= u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}; 0|= u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}$.
- To obtain another equation, we substitute the constraints into the differential equation and simplify:
$y^{″} + p(x)y^{′} + q(x)y|= f(x); (u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′})^{′} + p(x)(u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}) + q(x)(u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2})|= f(x); (u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{1}y_{1}^{″} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{″}) + p(x)(u_{1}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}y_{2}^{′}) + q(x)(u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2})|= f(x); u_{1}(y_{1}^{″} + p(x)y_{1}^{′} + q(x)y_{1}) + u_{2}(y_{2}^{″} + p(x)y_{2}^{′} + q(x)y_{2}) + u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′}|= f(x)$
Here, $y_1'' + p(x) y_1' + q(x)y_1=0$ and $y_2'' + p(x) y_2' + q(x) y_2=0$ since $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions of the corresponding homogeneous ODE. Thus, we get
$u_1' y_1' + u_2' y_2' = f(x)$.

So, we have the following system:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} = 0; u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} = f
$$

According to Cramer's rule, the solution of the system is the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1}^{′} &= \frac{\begin{vmatrix}0 & y_{2} \\ f & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}} =-\frac{y_{2}f}{y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{2}y_{1}^{′}} = - (f(x)y_{2}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2})) \\
u_{2}^{′} &= \frac{\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & 0 \\ y_{1}^{′} & f\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix}} = \frac{y_{1}f}{y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{2}y_{1}^{′}} = (f(x)y_{1}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2}))
\end{aligned}
$$

where $W(y_1,y_2)$ is the Wronskian.

Integrating, we reach the final result:

$$
u_{1} = - ∫(f(x)y_{2}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2}))dx, u_{2} = ∫(f(x)y_{1}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2}))dx
$$

## Solving a Differential Equation Whose Characteristic Equation Has Real Roots

**Example:** Consider the following differential equation.
$y'' - 2y' + y = 18x e^{x}$

The general solution to this equation can be written as
$y(x) = A e^{x} + B x e^{x} + g(x)$,
where $A$ and $B$ are constants. What could be the function $g(x)$?

**Explanation**

Suppose we have a second-order linear differential equation with linearly independent fundamental solutions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$. Then, according to the method of variation of parameters, we can seek a particular solution $y_p$ may be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}(x) &= u_{1}(x)y_{1}(x) + u_{2}(x)y_{2}(x)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $u_1$ and $u_2$ satisfy the following system:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} = f(x); u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} = 0
$$

The solution to this system is

$$
u_1(x) = -\int \dfrac{f(x)y_2(x)}{W(y_1,y_2)}\,dx, \qquad u_2(x) = \int \dfrac{f(x)y_1(x)}{W(y_1,y_2)}\,dx
$$

where $W(y_1,y_2)$ is the Wronskian.

From the given information, we see that the complementary solution is

$$
y_c(x) = A e^{x} + B x e^{x}
$$

Therefore, the fundamental solutions to the corresponding homogeneous equation are

$$
y_1(x) = e^{x}, \qquad y_2(x) = x e^{x}
$$

We can use the method of variation of parameters if these solutions are independent. So, let's first compute the Wronskian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= y_{1}y_{2}^{′} - y_{1}^{′}y_{2} \\
&= (e^{x})(xe^{x})^{′} - (e^{x})^{′}(xe^{x}) \\
&= (e^{x})(e^{x} + xe^{x}) - (e^{x})(xe^{x}) \\
&= e^{2x} + xe^{2x} - xe^{2x} \\
&= e^{2x} \ne 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the Wronskian is not equal to zero, this confirms that the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are independent.

Now compute $u_1(x)$ and $u_2(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1}(x) &= - ∫\frac{18xe^{x} \cdot xe^{x}}{e^{2x}}dx \\
&=-18∫x^{2}dx \\
&=-6x^{3} \\
u_{2}(x) &= ∫\frac{18xe^{x} \cdot e^{x}}{e^{2x}}dx \\
&= 18∫xdx \\
&= 9x^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the particular solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}(x) &= u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2} \\
&= (-6x^{3})e^{x} + (9x^{2})(xe^{x}) \\
&=-6x^{3}e^{x} + 9x^{3}e^{x} \\
&= 3x^{3}e^{x}
\end{aligned}
$$

The general solution is the sum of the complementary and particular solutions, and can be written as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(x) &= y_{c}(x) + y_{p}(x) \\
&= Ae^{x} + Bxe^{x} + 3x^{3}e^{x}_(⏟)_(g(x))
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
g(x) = 3x^3 e^{x}
$$

## Solving a Differential Equation Whose Characteristic Equation Has Complex Roots

**Example:** Consider the following differential equation
$y''+y=\tan{x}$.

The general solution to this equation can be written as
$y(x)=A\cos x + B\sin x + g(x)$,
where $A$ and $B$ are constants. What could be the function $g(x)$?

*Hint: Use the variation of parameters method. You may also wish to make use of the following result (integration constant omitted):*
$\int \tan x \sin x\,\textrm{d}x = -\sin x + \ln\!\left|\tan x+\sec x\right|$

**Explanation**

Suppose we have a second-order linear differential equation with linearly independent fundamental solutions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$. Then, according to the method of variation of parameters, we can seek a particular solution $y_p$ using the ansatz

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}(x) &= u_{1}(x)y_{1}(x) + u_{2}(x)y_{2}(x)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $u_1$ and $u_2$ satisfy the following system:

$$
{u_{1}^{′}y_{1}^{′} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2}^{′} = f(x); u_{1}^{′}y_{1} + u_{2}^{′}y_{2} = 0
$$

The solution to this system is

$$
u_1(x)=-\displaystyle\int\dfrac{f(x)y_2(x)}{W(y_1,y_2)}\,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad u_2(x)=\displaystyle\int\dfrac{f(x)y_1(x)}{W(y_1,y_2)}\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

where $W(y_1,y_2)$ is the Wronskian.

First, we note that the forcing term is

$$
f(x)=\tan x
$$

From the given information, we see that the complementary solution is

$$
y_c(x)=A\cos x + B\sin x
$$

Therefore, the fundamental solutions to the corresponding homogeneous equation are

$$
y_1(x)=\cos x, \qquad y_2(x)=\sin x
$$

We can use the method of variation of parameters if these solutions are linearly independent. So, let's first compute the Wronskian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W(y_{1}, y_{2}) &= \begin{vmatrix}y_{1} & y_{2} \\ y_{1}^{′} & y_{2}^{′}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= y_{1}(x)y_{2}^{′}(x) - y_{1}^{′}(x)y_{2}(x) \\
&= (\cos x)(\sin x)^{′} - (\cos x)^{′}(\sin x) \\
&= (\cos x)(\cos x) - (-\sin x)(\sin x) \\
&= \cos^{2} x + \sin^{2} x \\
&= 1 \ne 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the Wronskian is not equal to zero, this confirms that the functions $y_1(x)$ and $y_2(x)$ are independent.

Let’s compute $u_1(x)$ and $u_2(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{1}(x) &= - ∫(f(x)y_{2}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2}))dx \\
&= - ∫\tan x\sin xdx \\
&= \sin x - \ln \mid \tan x + \sec x \mid \\
u_{2}(x) &= ∫(f(x)y_{1}(x))/(W(y_{1}, y_{2}))dx \\
&= ∫\tan x\cos xdx \\
&= ∫\sin xdx \\
&=-\cos x
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the particular solution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{p}(x) &= u_{1}y_{1} + u_{2}y_{2} \\
&= (\cos x)(\sin x - \ln\begin{vmatrix}\tan x + \sec x &) + (-\cos x)(\sin x) \\ =-\cos x\ln & \tan x + \sec x\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The general solution is the sum of the complementary and particular solutions, and can be written as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(x) &= y_{c}(x) + y_{p}(x) \\
&= A\cos x + B\sin x- \cos x\ln \mid \tan x + \sec x \mid _(⏟)_(g(x))
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
g(x)=-\cos x\ln\!\left|\tan x+\sec x\right|
$$
