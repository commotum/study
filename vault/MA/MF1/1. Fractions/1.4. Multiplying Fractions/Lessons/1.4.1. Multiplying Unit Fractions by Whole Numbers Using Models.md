## Introduction

We can use fraction models to multiply unit fractions by whole numbers.

For example, let's consider the following product:

$$
\dfrac 13 \times 2
$$

First, we represent

$$
\dfrac{1}{3}
$$

using a model:

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/cff0265ca8e97395f8b6cf70a6b8f346.png>)

Multiplication by a whole number is simply a repeated addition. And, since we need to multiply

$$
\dfrac{\color{red}1}{3}
$$

by ${\color{blue}2}$, we repeat the fraction model $\color{blue}2$ times, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/762ccc6debd80c00afb886b8bda8505b.png>)

There are

$$
{\color{blue}2} \times {\color{red}1} = 2
$$

shaded pieces in total. Therefore, we conclude that

$$
2 \times \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{2}{3}
$$

## Multiplying a Unit Fraction by a Whole Number Using Repeated Addition

**Example:** Use the model above to calculate $3 \times \dfrac{1}{5}$.

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/c83fe5390d2e44fffb3e2eca95095e32.png>)

**Explanation**

Since we need to multiply

$$
\dfrac{\color{red}1}{5}
$$

by $\color{blue}3$, we repeat the fraction model $\color{blue}3$ times, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/ed0a7607c952da8bbb703d2f308f548a.png>)

There are

$$
{\color{blue}3} \times {\color{red}1} = 3
$$

shaded pieces in total. Therefore,

$$
3 \times \dfrac{1}{5} = \dfrac{3}{5}
$$

<a id="a-faster-way-of-computing-a-product-using-models"></a>
## A Faster Way of Computing a Product Using Models

There's another way we can use fraction models to multiply unit fractions by whole numbers.

To demonstrate, let's consider the multiplication problem below:

$$
\dfrac{1}{10} \times 7
$$

We start by drawing a fraction model to represent

$$
\dfrac{1}{10}
$$

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/aa072d0e01b4c54c4965e36d9bec2d74.png>)

Now, rather than repeating our model $7$ times (which would take a long time), we can instead multiply the number of shaded pieces by $7$.

Our model has $1$ shaded piece. So, the resulting model must have

$$
1 \times 7 = 7
$$

shaded pieces:

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/616c9ebe36cb792390535567284fa99d.png>)

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\dfrac{1}{10}\times 7 = \dfrac{7}{10}
$$

<a id="identifying-a-multiplication-problem-given-a-model"></a>
## Identifying a Multiplication Problem Given a Model

**Example:** What multiplication problem is represented by the model above?

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/7e79bafb1cf2ece2ec662c17758919f2.png>)

**Explanation**

We have

$$
\dfrac{1}{9}
$$

on the left and

$$
\dfrac{4}{9}
$$

on the right.

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/679c798eeca9aafbda28bcc6244b38d1.png>)

Therefore, we have

$$
\dfrac{1}{9} \times 4 = \dfrac{4}{9}
$$

**Note:** We can swap the factors on the left-hand side. So, the following would be a correct answer too:

$$
4 \times \dfrac{1}{9} = \dfrac{4}{9}
$$

<a id="identifying-a-missing-factor-given-a-model"></a>
## Identifying a Missing Factor Given a Model

**Example:** What number is missing from the multiplication problem above?

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/3caf310ffde8e83f0df4f6a36a83ebf9.png>)

**Explanation**

We have

$$
\dfrac{1}{5}
$$

on the left and

$$
\dfrac{3}{5}
$$

on the right.

![](<../Source/1.4.1. Multiplying Unit Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/cdf798e857f45e0a1d62044e2c60a803.png>)

The shape on the left has $1$ shaded piece, and the shape on the right has $3$ shaded pieces. So the number of shaded pieces has increased by a factor of ${\color{blue}3}$.

Therefore, we have

$$
\dfrac{1}{5} \times {\color{blue}3} = \dfrac{3}{5}
$$

So, the missing number is ${\color{blue}3}$.
