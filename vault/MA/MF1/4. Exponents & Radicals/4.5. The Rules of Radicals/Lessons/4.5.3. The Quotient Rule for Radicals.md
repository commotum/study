## Introduction

To divide square roots, we simply divide the numbers under the root. This is called the **quotient rule for radicals**.

To demonstrate, let's simplify the expression

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

by applying the quotient rule:

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{10}{2}} = \sqrt{5}
$$

The quotient rule also works for cube roots. For example,

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{10}}{\sqrt[3]{2}} = \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{10}{2}} = \sqrt[3]{5}
$$

In general, the quotient rule works for any quotient of $n$th roots. So for any $n$, we have

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt[n]{10}}{\sqrt[n]{2}} = \sqrt[n]{\dfrac{10}{2}} = \sqrt[n]{5}
$$

**Watch out!** We cannot use the quotient rule when we have the quotient of different radicals, such as square roots and cube roots. For example, the expression

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt[3]{5}}
$$

cannot be simplified because the radicals $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$ are different.

## Separating a Square Root Into a Quotient of Two Square Roots

**Example:** Express $\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{7}}$ as the quotient of two radicals.

**Explanation**

Applying the quotient rule, we have

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{7}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{7}}
$$

## Writing a Quotient of Two Square Roots as a Single Square Root

**Example:** Express $\dfrac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}}$ as a single radical.

**Explanation**

Using the quotient rule, we have

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{12}{3}}= \sqrt{4} = 2
$$

## The Quotient Rule With Cube Roots

**Example:** Express $\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{3}}{\sqrt[3]{4}}$ as a single radical.

**Explanation**

Applying the quotient rule, we have

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{3}}{\sqrt[3]{4}} = \sqrt[3] {\dfrac{3}{4}}
$$
