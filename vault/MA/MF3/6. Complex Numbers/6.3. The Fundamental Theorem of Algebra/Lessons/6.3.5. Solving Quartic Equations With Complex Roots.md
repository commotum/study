## Introduction

The fundamental theorem of algebra tells us that any quartic polynomial with real coefficients has precisely four roots.

Moreover, since complex roots always come in complex conjugate pairs, if $p(x)$ is a quartic polynomial with real coefficients, we must have one of the following cases:

- $p(x)$ has $4$ real roots (some of which might be repeated).
- $p(x)$ has $2$ (possibly repeated) real roots and $2$ complex conjugate roots. For example,
$p(x)|= x^{4} - 1; = (x^{2} - 1)(x^{2} + 1); = (x - 1)(x + 1)(x - \textrm{i})(x + \textrm{i})$.
In this case, the real roots are $x=\pm 1$, and the complex conjugate roots are $x=\pm\textrm i$.

- $p(x)$ has two pairs of complex conjugate roots. For example,
$p(x)|= (x^{2} + 1)(x^{2} + 4); = (x - \textrm{i})(x + \textrm{i})(x - 2\textrm{i})(x + 2\textrm{i})$.
In this case, the complex conjugate pairs of roots are $x=\pm\textrm i$ and $x=\pm 2\textrm i$. Also, notice that it's possible for the complex conjugate pairs of roots to be repeated. For example,
$p(x)|= x^{4} + 2x^{2} + 1; = (x^{2} + 1)^{2}; = ((x - \textrm{i})(x + \textrm{i}))^{2}; = (x - \textrm{i})(x - \textrm{i})(x + \textrm{i})(x + \textrm{i})$.
In this case, the roots are $x=\pm\textrm i$. Each of these roots is a double root.

## Finding All the Roots of a Quartic Equation Given Two Real Roots

**Example:** Given that $x=-1$ and $x=1$ are solutions to the equation $x^4 - 6 x^3 + 12 x^2 + 6 x - 13 = 0$, find the remaining solutions.

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(x)=x^4 - 6 x^3 + 12 x^2 + 6 x - 13
$$

Since $x_1=-1$ and $x_2=1$ are roots of $f(x)$, then according to the factor theorem,

$$
(x+1)(x-1) = x^2-1
$$

is a factor of $f(x)$.

Next, we find the quotient when $f(x)$ is divided by $x^2-1$ using polynomial division.

$$
x^{2} - 6x + 13; x^{2} - 1x^{4} - 6x^{3} + 12x^{2} + 6x - 13; x^{4} - x^{2}_(―); - 6x^{3} + 13x^{2} + 6x; - 6x^{3} + 6x_(―); x^{4} + 313x^{2} - 13; x^{4} + 313x^{2} - 13_(―); x^{4} + 3x^{3} - 5x^{2} + 150
$$

Thus,

$$
f(x) = (x^2-1)(x^2-6x+13)
$$

To find the remaining two roots, we solve

$$
x^2-6x+13=0
$$

by completing the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} - 6x + 13 &= 0 \\
(x^{2} - 6x + 3^{2}) - 3^{2} + 13 &= 0 \\
(x - 3)^{2} + 4 &= 0 \\
(x - 3)^{2} &= -4 \\
x - 3 &= ± 2\textrm{i} \\
x &= 3 ± 2\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions to our original equation are $x=\pm 1$ and

$$
x=3\pm 2\textrm{i}
$$

## Finding All the Roots of a Quartic Equation Given One Complex Root

**Example:** Given that $x=2\textrm{i}$ is a solution to the quartic equation $x^4 + 13 x^2 + 36 = 0$, find all of the solutions.

**Explanation**

First, we let

$$
f(x) = x^4 + 13 x^2 + 36
$$

Since $f(x)$ has real coefficients and

$$
x_1=2\text{i}
$$

is a root of $f(x)$, the complex conjugate

$$
\overline{x_1}=\overline{2\textrm{i}}=-2\text{i}
$$

must also be a root of $f(x)$.

Now, according to the factor theorem,
$\big(x-2\text{i}\big)\big(x-(-2\text{i})\big)$
is a factor of $f(x)$. Expanding the parentheses, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x - 2\textrm{i})(x - (-2\textrm{i})) &= (x - 2\textrm{i})(x + 2\textrm{i}) \\
&= (x^{2} - (2\textrm{i})^{2}) \\
&= x^{2} + 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we find the quotient when $f(x)$ is divided by $x^2+4$ using long division.

$$
x^{2} + 9; x^{2} + 4x^{4} + 13x^{2} + 36; x^{4} + 4x^{2}_(―); 2x^{4} +9x^{2} + 36; 2x^{4} +9x^{2} + 36_(―); 2x^{4} + 2x^{2} +00
$$

Thus,

$$
f(x) = (x^2+4)(x^2+9)
$$

To find the other two roots, we solve

$$
x^2+9 = 0{:}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + 9 &= 0 \\
x^{2} &= -9 \\
x &= ± 3\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the roots of our quartic equation are

$$
x=\pm 2\textrm{i}, \, \pm 3 \textrm{i}
$$
