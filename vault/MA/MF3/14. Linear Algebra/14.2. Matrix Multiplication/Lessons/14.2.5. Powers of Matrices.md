## Introduction

Powers of matrices are similar to powers of numbers. If $A$ is a matrix, then $A^n$ is $A$ multiplied by itself $n$ times:

$$
A^n = \underbrace{A\:\cdot\: A\:\cdot\: \ldots \:\cdot\: A}_{\Large{n\text{times}}}
$$

For $A$ to be conformable with itself, $A$ needs to be a square matrix. So we can only take powers of square matrices.

To demonstrate, suppose that
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Then we can calculate $A^2$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{2} &= A \cdot A \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 3 + (-1) \cdot 2 & 3 \cdot (-1) + (-1) \cdot 1 \\ 2 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 2 & 2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}7 & -4 \\ 8 & -1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

We say that $A^0 = I$, the identity matrix of the same dimension as $A$.

## Finding a Power of a Matrix

**Example:** Find $A^4$ given that $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 5 & -1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, to find $A^2$, we multiply $A$ by itself:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{2} &= A \cdot A \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 5 & -1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 5 & -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}9 & 0 \\ 10 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since

$$
A^4 = A^2 \cdot A^2
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{4} &= A^{2} \cdot A^{2} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}9 & 0 \\ 10 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}9 & 0 \\ 10 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}81 & 0 \\ 100 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Matrix Polynomials

Now that we know how to compute powers of matrices, we can evaluate polynomials where the input is a square matrix.

For example, consider the polynomial

$$
f(X) = X^2 + 4X + 3
$$

Note that, in order to evaluate this polynomial for an input matrix $X$, we need to interpret any constants as multiples of the identity matrix. So, we interpret the polynomial as

$$
f(X) = X^2 + 4X + 3I
$$

For example, if $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ then

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

and we can evaluate $f(A)$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(A) &= A^{2} + 4A + 3I \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix} + 4\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}4 & 4 \\ 0 & 8\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 + 4 + 3 & 3 + 4 + 0 \\ 0 + 0 + 0 & 4 + 8 + 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}8 & 7 \\ 0 & 15\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating a Matrix Polynomial

**Example:** Consider the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}$
Find $f(D)$ if $f(X)=-X^2+3X+2$.

**Explanation**

First, we rewrite the polynomial, interpreting any constants as multiples of the identity matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(X) &= -X^{2} + 3X + 2 \\
&=-X^{2} + 3X + 2I
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we want to find

$$
f(D)=-D^2+3D+2I
$$

Let's calculate $D^2$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D^{2} &= D \cdot D \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & -10 \\ 0 & 9\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we can evaluate $f(D)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(D) &= -D^{2} + 3D + 2I \\
&=-\begin{bmatrix}1 & -10 \\ 0 & 9\end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & -3\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 10 \\ 0 & -9\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3 & 15 \\ 0 & -9\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-1 + 3 + 2 & 10 + 15 + 0 \\ 0 & -9 - 9 + 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 25 \\ 0 & -16\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
