## Introduction

Given a $2 \times 2$ matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$

the **inverse** of $A$ can be found using the following formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1} &= (1)/(det(A))\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-inverse-of-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## Calculating the Inverse of a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Calculate $A^{-1}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Given a $2 \times 2$ matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$

the inverse of $A$ can be found using the following formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1} &= (1)/(det(A))\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

First, we compute the determinant:

$$
\det(A) = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot (-2) = 1
$$

Using the formula, then, the inverse is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1} &= (1)/(det(A))\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}. \\
&= \frac{1}{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -0 \\ - (-2) & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can verify the result as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
AA^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}✓ \\
A^{-1}A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

## Invertible and Singular Matrices

Remember that the inverse of a $2\times 2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$ is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}$

If $\det(A)=0$, then $A^{-1}$ doesn't exist since we can't divide by zero. In fact, we have the following statement:

> $A^{-1}$ exists if and only if $\det(A) \neq 0$.

A matrix that has an inverse is called **invertible**. Otherwise, the matrix doesn't have an inverse and is called **non-invertible**. A square matrix that is non-invertible is called **singular**.

---

<a id="identifying-singular-matrices"></a>
## Identifying Singular Matrices

**Example:** Which of the matrices below are singular?
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

- For the matrix $A$, we have
$\det({A})=1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1 = 0$.
Because $\det(A)=0$, we conclude that $A$ is not invertible. Since $A$ is a square matrix that is not invertible, we also say that $A$ is singular.
- The matrix $B$ is a $3 \times 2$ matrix. So, it is not invertible. Since the matrix is not square, we can't say that it's singular.
- For the matrix $C$, we have
$\det({C})=3 \cdot 3 - (-3) \cdot 3 = 18$.
Because $\det(C) \neq 0$, we conclude that $C$ is invertible.

Therefore, only $A$ is singular.

<a id="solving-for-a-variable-in-a-singular-matrix"></a>
## Solving for a Variable in a Singular Matrix

**Example:** If
$\begin{bmatrix}1 - k & 2 \\ 2 & 1 - k\end{bmatrix}$
is a singular matrix, calculate the value(s) of $k$.

**Explanation**

The matrix $A$ is singular if and only if $\det(A)=0$. Calculating the determinant, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A) &= (1 - k)(1 - k) - 4 \\
&= k^{2} - 2k - 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we set the determinant equal to $0$ and solve for $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k^{2} - 2k - 3 &= 0 \\
(k - 3)(k + 1) &= 0 \\
k &= 3,-1
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the values of $k$ are $k=3$ and $k=-1$.

<a id="verifying-the-formula"></a>
## Verifying the Formula

Given a matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$
we have been using the following formula to compute the inverse:
$\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix}$

To check that this formula is correct for a general matrix $A$, we need to verify that

$$
AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_2
$$

First, we compute $A^{-1}A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1} \cdot A &= \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix}ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & -bc + ad\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we compute $A A^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A \cdot A^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}d & -b \\ -c & a\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix}ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & -bc + ad\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have verified that

$$
AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_2
$$

and we can be sure that the given formula is the correct formula for $A^{-1}$.
