## Introduction

Consider the standard basis $\big\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \big\}$ of the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the unit square spanned by the vectors.

Let $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ be a matrix of a linear transformation $\mathbf T$. We can find the images of $\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \}$ under $\mathbf{T}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
i^{′} &= T(i) = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = 2i \\
j^{′} &= T(j) = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = j
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying this transformation, we obtain the following image:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/913f713af57050bff01b5e79203d8e02.png>)

We see that the transformation represented by the matrix $T$ multiplies the vector $\mathbf{i}$ by a factor of $2$ and leaves the vector $\mathbf j$ unchanged. This type of transformation is called a **stretch in the $\mathbf x$-direction.** We can also call it a **horizontal stretch.** In this case, we have a **horizontal stretch of scale factor** $\mathbf 2$.

In general, a stretch in the $x$-direction of scale factor $k$ with its center at the origin is represented by the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}k & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

---

<a id="vertical-stretch"></a>
## Vertical Stretch

Now, let $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ be a matrix of a linear transformation $\mathbf T$. We can find the images of $\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \}$ under $\mathbf T$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
i^{′} &= T(i) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = i \\
j^{′} &= T(j) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = 2j
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying this transformation, we obtain the following image:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/d4281f0df958135cfc892c1765a3df06.png>)

The transformation represented by the matrix $T$ multiplies the vector $\mathbf{j}$ by $2$ and leaves the vector $\mathbf i$ unchanged. This type of transformation is called a **stretch in the $\mathbf y$-direction.** We can also call this a **vertical stretch**. In this case, we have a **vertical stretch of scale factor** $\mathbf 2$.

In general, a stretch in the $y$-direction of scale factor $k$ with its center at the origin is represented by the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & k\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="finding-the-standard-matrix-of-a-stretch-transformation-given-a-vector-and-its-image"></a>
## Finding the Standard Matrix of a Stretch Transformation Given a Vector and its Image

**Example:** A linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ represents a stretch along the $x$-axis or $y$-axis. Given that $\begin{bmatrix}-4 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-16 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ what is the standard matrix of $\mathbf{T}$?

**Explanation**

First, we are told that $\mathbf{T}$ represents a stretch along the $x$-axis or $y$-axis.

Comparing the $x$- and $y$-components of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})$, we note the following:

- the $x$-component of $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})$ is $4$ times the $x$-component of $\mathbf{v}$, while
- the $y$-components of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})$ are the same.

So, we conclude that $\mathbf{T}$ represents a stretch of scale factor $4$ in the $x$-direction.

Therefore, the standard matrix of $\mathbf{T}$ is
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="horizontal-shear"></a>
## Horizontal Shear

Consider the standard basis $\big\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \big\}$ of the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the unit square spanned by the vectors.

Let $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ be a matrix of a linear transformation $\mathbf T$. We can find the images of $\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \}$ under $\mathbf{T}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
i^{′} &= T(i) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = i \\
j^{′} &= T(j) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.5 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 0.5i + j
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying this transformation, we obtain the following image:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/12b52dfba1b01fdd1f6a270330eef8d8.png>)

The transformation represented by the matrix $T$ adds $0.5\mathbf{i}$ to $\mathbf j$, while the vector $\mathbf i$ remains unchanged. This type of transformation is called a **horizontal shear.** In this case, we have a **horizontal shear with a shear factor of** $\mathbf{0.5}$.

In general, a horizontal shear of scale factor $k$ with its center at the origin is represented by the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & k \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

---

<a id="vertical-shear"></a>
## Vertical Shear

Now, let $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ be a matrix of a linear transformation $\mathbf T$. We can find the images of $\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j} \}$ under $\mathbf{T}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
i^{′} &= T(i) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0.5\end{bmatrix} = i + 0.5j \\
j^{′} &= T(j) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = j
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives the following image:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/ca5278fc7c512c32157435f321b3e215.png>)

The transformation represented by the matrix $T$ adds $0.5\mathbf{j}$ to $\mathbf i$, while the vector $\mathbf j$ remains unchanged. This type of transformation is called a **vertical shear.** In this case, we have a **vertical shear with a shear factor of** $\mathbf{0.5}$.

In general, a vertical shear of scale factor $k$ with its center at the origin is represented by the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ k & 1\end{bmatrix}$

## Identifying a Shear or Stretch Transformation Given the Images of the Standard Basis Vectors

**Example:** Consider the standard basis $\{\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j} \}$ of $\,\mathbb{R}^2$. If $\mathbf{T}$ is a linear transformation such that $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{i})=\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{j})=\mathbf{j}$ then, what is the geometric interpretation of the transformation $\mathbf{T}$?

**Explanation**

First, let's consider the unit square spanned by the vectors $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$.

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/c28904112781381da0083548d8c2dbbf.png>)

Now, using the fact that

$$
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{i})=\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{j})=\mathbf{j}
$$

we can draw the image of the square under the transformation $\mathbf{T}$:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/e46ae3a679baae89ff308c327360ee8e.png>)

Therefore, the linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ represents a vertical shear with a shear factor of $2$.

<a id="identifying-a-shear-or-stretch-transformation-represented-by-a-matrix"></a>
## Identifying a Shear or Stretch Transformation Represented by a Matrix

**Example:** What linear transformation is represented by the following matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We can find the images of the standard basis vectors $\mathbf i$ and $\mathbf j$ under the action of $\mathbf T$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(i) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = i \\
T(j) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 2i + j
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the matrix represents a *horizontal* shear with a shear factor of $2$.

## Finding the Image of a Square Under a Shear or Stretch Transformation

**Example:** Let $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ be the standard matrix of the linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$. What is the image of the square $R$ (shown above) under the action of $\mathbf{T}$?

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/8e226d694c513c348b7136c96c29a52b.png>)

**Explanation**

Looking at the matrix $T$, we can see that $\mathbf{T}$ represents a vertical shear of shear factor $-1$. Therefore, the image of the square $R$ under $\mathbf{T}$ is as follows:

![](<../Source/14.5.6. Shear and Stretch as Linear Transformations/Images/6c7707ce3c667c5692b4312d72bc9578.png>)

**Alternative method:**

We can check that the vertices of $R$ are mapped to the vertices of the parallelogram shown above as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
T(\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
