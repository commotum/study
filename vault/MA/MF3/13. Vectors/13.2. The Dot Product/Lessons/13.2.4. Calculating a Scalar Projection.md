## Introduction

The **scalar projection** of a vector $\mathbf{a}$ onto another vector $\mathbf{b}$ is the distance that the vector $\mathbf{a}$ extends in the direction of $\mathbf{b}$.

For example, consider two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ shown below. Suppose we know that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \,\mathbf{a}\, &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

and that the angle between the vectors is

$$
\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Calculating a Scalar Projection/Images/64af6629c431159c6711f4513a638ed2.png>)

To illustrate the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ along $\mathbf{b}$, consider the point $P$ on the line that contains $\mathbf{b}$ with $AP \perp \mathbf{b}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Calculating a Scalar Projection/Images/afba77446d85db03d103433487bec49b.png>)

The length $OP$ is called the **scalar projection** of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ (or the **component** of $\mathbf{a}$ along $\mathbf{b}$). It is denoted as $\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a}$, and we can compute it using cosine in $\triangle OPA$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= OP \\
&= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 2\cos (\frac{π}{4}) \\
&= 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \\
&= \sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

This formula is the general definition of the scalar projection:

$$
\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a}= \mid \,\mathbf{a}\, \mid \cos\theta
$$

<a id="negative-scalar-projections"></a>
## Negative Scalar Projections

The scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ can be negative. This happens when the vector $\mathbf{a}$ extends in the direction opposite to that of $\mathbf{b}$.

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Calculating a Scalar Projection/Images/a246cb0b361184d4b0f402de21f2919c.png>)

For example, consider two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, where it is known that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \,\mathbf{a}\, &= 2, \qquad \theta = 135^\circ
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the angle $\theta$ between the vectors is obtuse.

Computing the scalar projection of $\mathbf a$ onto $\mathbf b$ using the formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 2\cos (135^{∘}) \\
&= 2 \cdot (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \\
&=-\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

In general, if

$$
90^\circ < \theta \leq 180^\circ
$$

then $\cos\theta <0$ and the scalar projection will be negative.

## Calculating a Scalar Projection Using Angles and Magnitudes

**Example:** Suppose $\mid \,\mathbf{a}\,|=13$ and the angle between vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is $\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$. Find the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the scalar projection, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \mid a \mid \cos θ \\
&= 13\cos (\frac{2π}{3}) \\
&= 13 \cdot (-\frac{1}{2}) \\
&=-\frac{13}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="deriving-a-general-formula"></a>
## Deriving a General Formula

We have been using the following formula for the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$:

$$
\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a}= \mid \,\mathbf{a}\, \mid \cos\theta
$$

However, sometimes we are not given the angle between two vectors. In such cases, we can solve for $\cos \theta$ in terms of the dot product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a \cdot b &= \mid a \mid \mid b\begin{vmatrix}\cos θ \\ \cos θ|= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid a \mid \mid b\end{vmatrix}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting this into our original formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \begin{vmatrix}a & \cos θ \\ = & a\end{vmatrix}\cdot \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid a \mid \mid b\begin{vmatrix}} \\ = \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b\end{vmatrix}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, another general formula for the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b \mid}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-a-scalar-projection-using-components"></a>
## Calculating a Scalar Projection Using Components

**Example:** Let $\mathbf{a}=\langle 3,3,-1 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{b}=\langle 2,-7,0 \rangle$. Find the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the scalar projection, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b \mid} \\
&= \frac{a_{x}b_{x} + a_{y}b_{y} + a_{z}b_{z}}{\sqrt{b_{x}^{2} + b_{y}^{2} + b_{z}^{2}}} \\
&= (3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot (-7) + (-1) \cdot 0)/(\sqrt{2^{2} + (-7)^{2} + 0^{2}}) \\
&=-\frac{15}{\sqrt{53}}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="projecting-perpendicular-vectors"></a>
## Projecting Perpendicular Vectors

Notice that if two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are perpendicular, then the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ (or $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$) is equal to $0$.

For example, consider the following illustration. Notice that, since $\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b}$, the vector $\mathbf{a}$ doesn't extend in the direction of $\mathbf{b}$ at all. So, the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is $0$.

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Calculating a Scalar Projection/Images/343c7ce96cd3598d0a81cd8f5e38ae9f.png>)

Furthermore, we have the following fact:

> *If $\mathbf{a}$ is a nonzero vector, its scalar projection onto another nonzero vector $\mathbf{b}$ equals $0$ if and only if the vectors are perpendicular.*

---

<a id="projecting-a-vector-onto-a-perpendicular-vector"></a>
## Projecting a Vector Onto a Perpendicular Vector

**Example:** Let $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ Find the scalar projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$.

**Explanation**

Using the formula for the scalar projection, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{b}a &= \frac{a \cdot b}{\mid b \mid} \\
&= \frac{a_{x}b_{x} + a_{y}b_{y} + c_{z}b_{z}}{\sqrt{b_{x}^{2} + b_{y}^{2} + b_{z}^{2}}} \\
&= (0 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot (-4) + 1 \cdot 0)/(\sqrt{2^{2} + (-4)^{2} + 0^{2}}) \\
&= (0)/(\sqrt{2^{2} + (-4)^{2} + 0^{2}}) \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$
