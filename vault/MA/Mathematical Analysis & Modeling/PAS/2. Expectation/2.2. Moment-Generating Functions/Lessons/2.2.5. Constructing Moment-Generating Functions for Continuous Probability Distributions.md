## Introduction

Let $X$ be a continuous random variable with probability density function $f(x)$. Recall that the moment-generating function of $X$ is defined as

$$
M(t) =\textrm E \left[ e^{tX} \right] =\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f (x) e^{tx} \textrm dx
$$

In this lesson, we'll construct moment-generating functions for some important continuous probability distributions, namely:

- the uniform distribution
- the exponential distribution
- the normal distribution
- the chi-square distribution

As we'll soon see, we can use the moment-generating functions to prove certain properties about these distributions.

Let's begin by considering the MGF of a uniformly distributed random variable.

## MGFs of Continuous Uniform Random Variables

**Example:** Find the moment-generating function $M(t)$ of $X\sim U(0, 3)$ for $t\neq 0$.

**Explanation**

The moment-generating function of a continuous random variable $X$ is given by

$$
M(t) =\textrm E \left[ e^{tX} \right] =\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f (x) e^{tx} \,\textrm dx
$$

where $f(x)$ is its probability density function.

Given that $X\sim U(0, 3)$, we have the following probability density function:

$$
{\frac{1}{3},\begin{vmatrix}0 < x < 3 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= ∫_{- ∞}^{∞}f(x)e^{tx}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{3}\frac{1}{3}e^{tx}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{e^{tx}}{t} \mid _{0}^{3} \\
&= \frac{1}{3} \cdot (\frac{e^{3t}}{t} - \frac{1}{t}) \\
&= e 3 t - 1 3 t
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that when finding the antiderivative, we used the fact that

$$
t\neq0
$$

which ensures we can divide by $t$.

## MGFs of Exponential Random Variables

**Example:** Find the moment-generating function $M(t)$ of $X\sim \mathrm{Exp}(5)$ for $t < 5$.

**Explanation**

The moment-generating function of a continuous random variable $X$ is given by

$$
M(t) =\textrm E \left[ e^{tX} \right] =\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f (x) e^{tx} \,\textrm dx
$$

where $f(x)$ is its probability density function.

Given that $X\sim \mathrm{Exp}(5)$, we have the following probability density function:

$$
{5e^{-5x},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= ∫_{- ∞}^{∞}f(x)e^{tx}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{∞}5e^{-5x}e^{tx}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{∞}5e^{(t - 5)x}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, note the following:

- For $t=5$, our integral becomes
$M(5) = \int_0^{\infty} 5e^{(5-5)x} \, \textrm dx = \int_0^{\infty} 5 \, \textrm dx$,
which doesn't converge.
- For $t \neq 5$, our integral becomes
$M(t)|= ∫_{0}^{∞}5e^{(t - 5)x}dx; = \frac{5}{t - 5}e^{(t - 5)x} \mid _{0}^{∞}; = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{5}{t - 5}e^{(t - 5)x} - \frac{5}{t - 5}; = \frac{5}{t - 5}lim_(x → ∞)e^{(t - 5)x} - \frac{5}{t - 5}$.
To evaluate the limit at infinity, we consider two cases:If $t < 5$, then $t-5< 0$, and $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} e^{(t - 5)x} = 0$.If $t > 5$, then $t-5 > 0$, and $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} e^{(t - 5)x} = \infty$.

Therefore,

$$
M(t) =-\dfrac{5}{t-5} = \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\dfrac{5}{5-t}}, \qquad t < 5
$$

and $M(t)$ is undefined for

$$
t \geq 5
$$

## MGFs of Normal Random Variables

**Example:** Find the moment-generating function $M(t)$ of $X\sim N(0, 1)$.

*Hint. The probability density function of $X$ is*
$f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$. *Moreover, we have the following Gaussian integral formula:*
$\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} e^{-b(x+a)^2} \textrm dx = \sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{b}}$
where $a\in \mathbb R$ and $b \in \mathbb R^+$.

**Explanation**

The moment-generating function of a continuous random variable $X$ is given by

$$
M(t) =\textrm E \left[ e^{tX} \right] =\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f (x) e^{tx} \,\textrm dx
$$

where $f(x)$ is its probability density function.

We are given that

$$
f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= ∫_{- ∞}^{∞}f(x)e^{tx}dx \\
&= ∫_{- ∞}^{∞}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2π}}e^{-x^{2}/2} \cdot e^{tx}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2π}}∫_{- ∞}^{∞}e^{-x^{2}/2 + tx}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's express the exponent with a common denominator and complete the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{x^{2}}{2} + tx &= -\frac{x^{2} - 2tx}{2} \\
&=-\frac{x^{2} - 2tx + t^{2} - t^{2}}{2} \\
&= - ((x - t)^{2} - t^{2})/(2) \\
&= - ((x - t)^{2})/(2) + \frac{t^{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above result in our integral, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2π}}∫_{- ∞}^{∞}e^{-x^{2}/2 + tx}dx \\
&= \frac{e^{t^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2π}}∫_{- ∞}^{∞}e^{- (x - t)^{2}/2}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, by applying the given formula

$$
\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} e^{-b(x+a)^2} \,\textrm dx = \sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{b}}
$$

with $a=-t$ and

$$
b=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

we get

$$
\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} e^{-(x-t)^2/2} \,\textrm dx = \sqrt{2 \pi}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= \frac{e^{t^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2π}}∫_{- ∞}^{∞}e^{- (x - t)^{2}/2}dx \\
&= \frac{e^{t^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2π}} \cdot \sqrt{2π} \\
&= e t 2 / 2
\end{aligned}
$$

## MGFs of Chi-Square Random Variables

**Example:** Find the moment-generating function $M(t)$ of $X\sim \chi^2(4)$ for $t < \dfrac12$.

*Hint. The probability density function of $X$ is*
${(x \cdot e^{-x/2})/(4 \cdot Γ(2))\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0, \\ 0\end{vmatrix}otherwise$ *where*
$\Gamma(k) = \int_{0}^\infty t^{k-1} e^{-t}\,\mathrm d t$
is the gamma function.

**Explanation**

The moment-generating function of a continuous random variable $X$ is given by

$$
M(t) =\textrm E \left[ e^{tX} \right] =\int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f (x) e^{tx}\, \textrm dx
$$

where $f(x)$ is its probability density function.

We are given that

$$
f(x) = \dfrac{{x} \cdot e^{-x/2}}{4 \cdot \Gamma\left(2 \right)}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= ∫_{- ∞}^{∞}f(x)e^{tx}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{∞}(x \cdot e^{-x/2})/(4 \cdot Γ(2)) \cdot e^{tx}dx \\
&= (1)/(4 \cdot Γ(2))∫_{0}^{∞}x \cdot e^{tx - x/2}dx \\
&= (1)/(4 \cdot Γ(2))∫_{0}^{∞}x \cdot e^{x(t - 1/2)}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, note the following:

- For $t=\dfrac 12$ our integral becomes
$M\left(\dfrac 12 \right) = \int^{\infty}_{0} {x} \cdot e^{x(1/2-1/2)} \, \textrm dx = \int^{\infty}_{0} {x} \,\textrm dx$,
which doesn't converge.
- For $t > \dfrac 12$, we have $t - \dfrac 12 > 0$, which means
$x e^{x\left(t- 1/2\right)} \to \infty\quad \textrm{as} \quad x \to \infty$.
So, our integral doesn't converge.
- For $t < \dfrac 12$, we use the substitution
$u = x\left(\dfrac 12 - t\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathrm d x = \dfrac{1}{1/2 - t} \mathrm d u$.
Rewriting our integral, we get
$∫_{0}^{∞}x \cdot e^{x(t - 1/2)}dx|= ∫_{0}^{∞}(1)/((1/2 - t))(\frac{u}{1/2 - t})e^{-u}du; = (1)/((1/2 - t)^{2})∫_{0}^{∞}ue^{-u}du; = (1)/((1/2 - t)^{2}) \cdot Γ(2)$.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(t) &= (1)/(4 \cdot Γ(2))∫_{0}^{∞}x \cdot e^{x(t - 1/2)}dx \\
&= (1)/(4 \cdot Γ(2)) \cdot (1)/((1/2 - t)^{2}) \cdot Γ(2) \\
&= (1)/(4 \cdot Γ(2)) \cdot (1)/((1/2 - t)^{2}) \cdot Γ(2) \\
&= \frac{1}{2^{2}} \cdot (1)/((1/2 - t)^{2}) \\
&= 1 (1 - 2 t) 2fort < \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

and $M(t)$ is undefined for

$$
t\geq \dfrac 12
$$

## A Summary of the MGFs for Some Important Continuous Distributions

The table below gives the moment-generating functions $M(t)$ of some common continuous probability distributions.

| Distribution | $M(t)$ | $t$-domain |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $U[a,b]$ | ${(e^{tb} - e^{ta})/(t(b - a)),\begin{vmatrix}t \ne 0 \\ 1,\end{vmatrix}t = 0$ | $t\in\mathbb R$ |
| $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ | $\exp\left({\mu t+\dfrac12\sigma^2t^2}\right)$ | $\,\,\,t\in\mathbb R\,\,\,$ |
| $\chi^2(k)$ | $(1-2t)^{-k/2}$ | $t<\dfrac12$ |
| $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ | $\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}$ | $t<\lambda$ |
| $\Gamma(\alpha,\lambda)$ | $\left(\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^\alpha$ | $t<\lambda$ |

From here, we can make some observations:

- At the beginning of this lesson, we proved the formula for a continuous uniform random variable $X\sim U[a,b]$ for some specific values of $a$ and $b$, where we assumed $t\neq 0$. However, when $t=0$, we have
$M(0)|= ∫_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{b - a} \cdot e^{0 \cdot x}dx; = ∫_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{b - a}dx; = \frac{b - a}{b - a}; = 1$.

- Notice the similarity between the MGFs of the exponential and gamma random variables. This is reflective of the relationship between the distributions: $\textrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ gives the total time until the next event occurs in a Poisson process, while $\Gamma(\alpha, \lambda)$ gives the total time for $\alpha$ events to occur. In a future lesson, we will discuss the precise relationship between these two MGFs.
