## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to conduct chi-square goodness-of-fit tests to determine whether some sample data fit a continuous uniform distribution.

First, let's remind ourselves of some basic facts.

- Given a random variable $X \sim U[\alpha, \beta]$, the probability density function (PDF) of $X$ is given by
${\frac{1}{β - α},\begin{vmatrix}a \le x \le b, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$.
- For any interval $[x_1, x_2] \subseteq [\alpha, \beta]$, the probability that $X$ lies within that interval is
$P(x_1 \leq X \leq x_2) = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x) \, dx = \dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{\beta-\alpha}$.
- If we divide the range $[\alpha, \beta]$ into $k$ intervals, then the expected frequency of observations falling in the $i$th interval $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ is given by
$E_i = N \cdot P(x_{i-1} \leq X \leq x_i) = N \cdot \dfrac{x_i - x_{i-1}}{\beta-\alpha}$.

Let's get some practice at computing expected frequencies using the continuous uniform distribution.

## Calculating Expected Frequencies

**Example:** The salaries of a company's employees range from $\[math]30\,000$ to $\[/math]90\,000$ dollars. A company director wishes to carry out a hypothesis test with the following null and alternative hypotheses:

- $H_0:\:$ The salaries are distributed uniformly.
- $H_1:\:$ The salaries are not distributed uniformly.

A random sample of employees is taken, and the frequency distribution of their salaries is shown below. Calculate the expected frequency $E_i$ for each salary category.

| Salary (thousands of $\[math]$) | $30\leq s_i < 42$ | $42\leq s_i < 60$ | $60\leq s_i < 80$ | $80\leq s_i \leq 90$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Observed Frequency $(O_i)$ | $12$ | $10$ | $6$ | $2$ |
| Expected Frequency $(E_i)$ |  |  |  |  |

**Explanation**

Let $X$ be the salary of a randomly selected employee (in thousands of dollars). Under the null hypothesis, we have that $X\sim U[30,90]$. Therefore,

$$
P(a\leq X\lt b)= \dfrac{b-a}{90-30} = \dfrac{b-a}{60}
$$

where $a \lt b$ and $a,b\in [30,90]$.

The sample size is

$$
N = 12+10+6+2 = 30
$$

Therefore, the expected frequencies for each class of observations are as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1} &= N \cdot P(30 \le X < 42) \\
&= 30 \cdot (\frac{42 - 30}{60}) \\
&= 6 \\
E_{2} &= N \cdot P(42 \le X < 60) \\
&= 30 \cdot (\frac{60 - 42}{60}) \\
&= 9 \\
E_{3} &= N \cdot P(60 \le X < 80) \\
&= 30 \cdot (\frac{80 - 60}{60}) \\
&= 10 \\
E_{4} &= N \cdot P(80 \le X \le 90) \\
&= 30 \cdot (\frac{90 - 80}{60}) \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

A table summarizing the observed and corresponding expected frequencies is given below:

| Salary (thousands of $\[math]$) | $30\leq s_i < 42$ | $42\leq s_i < 60$ | $60\leq s_i < 80$ | $80\leq s_i \leq 90$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Observed Frequency $(O_i)$ | $12$ | $10$ | $6$ | $2$ |
| Expected Frequency $(E_i)$ | $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}6}$ | $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}9}$ | $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}10}$ | $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}5}$ |

<a id="the-chi-square-test"></a>
## The Chi-Square Test

Let's remind ourselves of the key facts regarding chi-square goodness-of-fit tests.

Suppose we have a set of observed frequencies $O_i$ and corresponding expected frequencies $E_i$, as defined by a theoretical model under the null hypothesis. Then, the random variable

$$
X = \sum_{i} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}
$$

approximately follows a chi-square distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, where

- $O_i$ is the observed frequency for category $i$,
- $E_i$ is the expected frequency for category $i$, as predicted under the null hypothesis,
- The number of degrees of freedom $\nu$ is given by

$$
\nu = \textrm{number of categories} - \textrm{number of constraints}
$$

This approximation holds when all expected frequencies $E_i$ are sufficiently large (typically

$$
E_i \geq 5
$$

for each category), and the observations are independent.

In this lesson, we'll only consider the case with one constraint: the sum of the expected frequencies should equal $N$, the total number of observations. We'll deal with cases with two or more constraints in other lessons.

## Calculating a Test Statistic

**Example:** Suppose $Y$ is a continuous random variable with support $S=[0,120]$. A random sample is carried out, and the frequency distribution of the sample data is shown below.

| Class | $0\leq y_i < 30$ | $30\leq y_i< 90$ | $90\leq y_i \leq 120$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Observed Frequency $(O_i)$ | $31$ | $42$ | $27$ |

A chi-square goodness of fit test is conducted at the $1\%$ significance level with the following null and alternative hypotheses:

- $H_0:\: Y\sim U[0,120]$
- $H_1:\:Y\not\sim U[0,120]$

Calculate the value of the test statistic for this hypothesis test.

**Explanation**

Suppose we have a set of observed frequencies $O_i$ and corresponding expected frequencies $E_i$, as defined by a theoretical model under the null hypothesis. Then, the random variable

$$
X = \sum_{i} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}
$$

approximately follows a chi-square distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, where

- $O_i$ is the observed frequency for category $i$, and
- $E_i$ is the expected frequency for category $i$, as predicted under the null hypothesis.

This approximation holds when all expected frequencies $E_i$ are sufficiently large (typically

$$
E_i \geq 5
$$

for each category), and the observations are independent.

Under the null hypothesis, we have that $Y\sim U[0,120]$. Therefore,

$$
P(a\leq Y\lt b)= \dfrac{b-a}{120-0} = \dfrac{b-a}{120}
$$

where $a \lt b$ and $a,b\in [0,120]$.

The sample size is

$$
N = 31 + 42 + 27 = 100
$$

Therefore, the expected frequencies for each class of observation are as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1} &= N \cdot P(0 \le Y < 30) \\
&= 100 \cdot (\frac{30 - 0}{120}) \\
&= 25 \\
E_{2} &= N \cdot P(30 \le Y < 90) \\
&= 100 \cdot (\frac{90 - 30}{120}) \\
&= 50 \\
E_{3} &= N \cdot P(90 \le Y < 120) \\
&= 100 \cdot (\frac{120 - 90}{120}) \\
&= 25
\end{aligned}
$$

A table summarizing the observed and corresponding expected frequencies is given below:

| Class | $0\leq y_i < 30$ | $30\leq y_i< 90$ | $90\leq y_i \leq 120$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $O_i$ | $31$ | $42$ | $27$ |
| $E_i$ | $25$ | $50$ | $25$ |
| $\dfrac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}$ | $1.44$ | $1.28$ | $0.16$ |

We compute our test statistic by summing the last row:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∑_(i)((O_{i} - E_{i})^{2})/(E_{i}) &= 1.44 + 1.28 + 0.16 \\
&= 2.88
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the value of the test statistic is $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}2.88}$.

<a id="carrying-out-a-chi-square-test"></a>
## Carrying Out a Chi-Square Test

**Example:** The salaries of a company's employees range from $\[math]30\,000$ to $\[/math]90\,000$ dollars. A random sample of employees is taken, and the frequency distribution of their salaries is shown below.

| Salary (thousands of $\[math]$) | $30\leq y_i \lt 40$ | $40\leq y_i \lt 60$ | $60\leq y_i \lt 80$ | $80\leq y_i \leq 90$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Observed Frequency $(O_i)$ | $\,27\,$ | $\,49\,$ | $\,27\,$ | $\,17\,$ |

Conduct a chi-squre goodness -of-fit test at the $5\%$ significance level with the following null and alternative hypotheses:

- $H_0:\:$ The salaries are distributed uniformly.
- $H_1:\:$ The salaries are not distributed uniformly.

The table below gives the values of $w$ that satisfy $P(W\geq w) = p$, where $W\sim \chi^2(\nu)$.

| $\nu \setminus p$ | $0.010$ | $0.050$ | $0.100$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $2$ | $\,9.210\,$ | $\,5.991\,$ | $\,4.605\,$ |
| $3$ | $\,11.345\,$ | $\,7.815\,$ | $\,6.251\,$ |
| $4$ | $\,13.277\,$ | $\,9.488\,$ | $\,7.779\,$ |
| $5$ | $\,15.086\,$ | $\,11.070\,$ | $\,9.236\,$ |

**Explanation**

Suppose we have a set of observed frequencies $O_i$ and corresponding expected frequencies $E_i$, as defined by a theoretical model under the null hypothesis. Then, the random variable

$$
X = \sum_{i} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}
$$

approximately follows a chi-square distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, where

- $O_i$ is the observed frequency for category $i$, and
- $E_i$ is the expected frequency for category $i$, as predicted under the null hypothesis.

This approximation holds when all expected frequencies $E_i$ are sufficiently large (typically

$$
E_i \geq 5
$$

for each category), and the observations are independent.

Let $Y$ be the salary of a randomly selected employee (in thousands of dollars). Under the null hypothesis, we have that $Y\sim U[30,90]$. Therefore,

$$
P(a\leq Y\lt b)= \dfrac{b-a}{90-30} = \dfrac{b-a}{60}
$$

where $a \lt b$ and $a,b\in [30,90]$.

The sample size is

$$
N = 27+49+27+17 = 120
$$

Therefore, the expected frequencies for each class of observation are as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{1} &= N \cdot P(30 \le Y < 40) \\
&= 120 \cdot (\frac{40 - 30}{60}) \\
&= 20 \\
E_{2} &= N \cdot P(40 \le Y < 60) \\
&= 120 \cdot (\frac{60 - 40}{60}) \\
&= 40 \\
E_{3} &= N \cdot P(60 \le Y < 80) \\
&= 120 \cdot (\frac{80 - 60}{60}) \\
&= 40 \\
E_{4} &= N \cdot P(80 \le Y \le 90) \\
&= 120 \cdot (\frac{90 - 80}{60}) \\
&= 20
\end{aligned}
$$

A table summarizing the observed and corresponding expected frequencies is given below:

| Salary (thousands of $\[math]$) | $30\leq y_i \lt 40$ | $40\leq y_i \lt 60$ | $60\leq y_i \lt 80$ | $80\leq y_i \leq 90$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $O_i$ | $\,27\,$ | $\,49\,$ | $\,27\,$ | $\,17\,$ |
| $E_i$ | $\,20\,$ | $\,40\,$ | $\,40\,$ | $\,20\,$ |
| $\dfrac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}$ | $\,2.45\,$ | $\,2.025\,$ | $\,4.225\,$ | $\,0.45\,$ |

We compute our test statistic by summing the last row:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∑_(i)((O_{i} - E_{i})^{2})/(E_{i}) &= 2.45 + 2.025 + 4.225 + 0.45 \\
&= 9.15
\end{aligned}
$$

There is only one constraint on our data: the total number of observations must equal $N$. Therefore, the number of degrees of freedom $\nu$ is calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
ν &= number of categories - number of constraints \\
&= 4 - 1 \\
&= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

So, there are

$$
\nu = \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}3}
$$

degrees of freedom.

From the given chi-square table, the critical value for $\nu=3$ at a $5\%$ significance level is

$$
\chi^2_{\textrm{critical}}=7.815
$$

and the critical region is

$$
X\geq 7.815
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Testing Continuous Uniform Models Using Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit/Images/fd7610bedec3a398961d18bc2cabb564.png>)

Our test statistic $(9.15)$ lies in the critical region. Therefore, there is $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\textrm{sufficient}}$ evidence to reject the null hypothesis.
