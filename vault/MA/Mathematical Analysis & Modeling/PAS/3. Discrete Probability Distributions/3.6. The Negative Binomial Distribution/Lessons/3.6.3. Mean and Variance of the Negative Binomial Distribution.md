## Introduction

Suppose the random variable $X$ follows a negative binomial distribution with $r$ successes and success probability $p{:}$

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)
$$

The expected value of $X$ is given by

$$
\textrm E[X] = \dfrac{r}{p}
$$

To understand this result, we can compare it to the expected value of a geometric random variable:
$X_{\textrm{G}}\sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$

In a previous lesson, we showed that

$$
\textrm E[X_{\textrm{G}}] = \dfrac{1}{p}
$$

Remember that a geometric random variable is a negative binomial random variable with $r=1$ successes. In other words:

- $X_{\textrm G}$ measures the number of trials needed for the *first* success, while
- $X$ measures the number of trials for $r$ successes.

Thus, $X$ can be thought of as the *sum* of $r$ independent geometric random variables:

$$
X = \underbrace{X_{\textrm{G}_1} + X_{\textrm{G}_2} +\cdots + X_{\textrm{G}_r}}_{r \textrm{times}}
$$

where $X_{\textrm{G}_1}, X_{\textrm{G}_2}, \ldots, X_{\textrm{G}_r} \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$ (note that this can be proved formally).

We can interpret our result for the expected value of $\textrm{NB}(r,p)$ as follows:

- If the expected value of $X_{\textrm{G}}$ is $\dfrac1p$, then there should be (on average) $\dfrac1p$ trials to reach the first success.For example, if $X_{\textrm{G}}$ represents the number of times a fair coin is spun before the first head, we have $p = \dfrac12$, and therefore, we'd expect (on average) two trials to reach the first success:
$\textrm E [X_{\textrm G}] = \dfrac1p = \dfrac{1}{\left(\dfrac12\right)} = 2$
- Now, since $X\sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)$, there should be $r\cdot \dfrac{1}{p} = \dfrac{r}{p}$ trials (on average) before the first $r$ successes.For example, if $X$ represents the number of times a fair coin is spun before the *five* heads, we have $r=5$ and $p = \dfrac12$. Therefore, we'd expect ten trials to achieve the first five successes:
$\textrm E[X] = 5\cdot \dfrac{1}{\left(\dfrac12\right)} = 5\cdot 2 = 10$

## Finding the Expected Value of a Negative Binomial Distribution

**Example:** Given that $X\sim \textrm{NB}\left(4, 0.25\right)$, what is $\textrm E[X]$?

**Explanation**

In general, if $X \sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)$ is a negative binomial random variable, then

$$
\textrm E[X] = \dfrac{r}{p}
$$

So, for our random variable

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}\left(4, 0.25\right)
$$

we have the following expected value:

$$
\textrm E[X] = \dfrac{4}{0.25} = 16
$$

## Finding the Expected Value of a Negative Binomial Distribution in Context

**Example:** A fair die is rolled until five $6$s are obtained. What is the expected number of rolls?

**Explanation**

Let $X$ represent the number of rolls until the fifth $6$ is obtained. Rolling a die is a Bernoulli trial, and we wish to find the number of repeated trials until we reach $5$ successes. So, we can model $X$ using the negative binomial distribution:

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}\left(5, \dfrac16\right)
$$

We wish to compute $\textrm E[X]$. In general, if $X \sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)$, then

$$
\textrm E[X] = \dfrac{r}{p}
$$

So, for our random variable

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}\left(5, \dfrac16\right)
$$

we have the following expected value:

$$
\textrm E[X] = \dfrac{5}{\left(\dfrac16\right)} = 30
$$

Therefore, the expected number of rolls is $30$.

## The Variance of a Negative Binomial Random Variable

The variance of $X$ is determined by the following formula:

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \dfrac{r(1-p)}{p^2}
$$

Again, it's worth comparing this to the variance of $X_{\textrm G}\sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$. Recall that

$$
\textrm{Var}[X_{\textrm G}] = \dfrac{(1-p)}{p^2}
$$

It's easy to see how the two rules are related. First, we can think of $X$ as a sum of $r$ independent geometric random variables:

$$
X = \underbrace{X_{\textrm{G}_1} + X_{\textrm{G}_2} +\cdots + X_{\textrm{G}_r}}_{r \textrm{times}}
$$

where $X_{\textrm{G}_1}, X_{\textrm{G}_2}, \ldots, X_{\textrm{G}_r} \sim \textrm{Geom}(p)$. By the properties of variance for sums of independent random variables, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[X] &= Var[X_{G_{1}}] + Var[X_{G_{2}}] + ⋯ + Var[X_{G_{r}}] \\
&= ((1 - p))/(p^{2}) + ((1 - p))/(p^{2}) + ⋯ + ((1 - p))/(p^{2})_(⏟)_(rtimes) \\
&= (r(1 - p))/(p^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-variance-of-a-negative-binomial-distribution"></a>
## Finding the Variance of a Negative Binomial Distribution

**Example:** Given that $X\sim \textrm{NB}\left(4, 0.25\right)$, what is $\textrm{Var}[X]$?

**Explanation**

In general, if $X \sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)$ is a negative binomial random variable, then

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \dfrac{r(1-p)}{p^2}
$$

So, for our random variable

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}\left(4, 0.25\right)
$$

we have the following variance:

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \dfrac{4\left(0.75\right)}{\left(0.25\right)^2} = 48
$$

## Finding the Variance of a Negative Binomial Distribution in Context

**Example:** A fair die is rolled until an even number is obtained $14$ times. What is the variance in the number of rolls?

**Explanation**

Let $X$ represent the number of rolls until the $14$th even number is obtained. Rolling a die is a Bernoulli trial, and we wish to find the number of repeated trials until we reach $14$ successes. So, we can model $X$ using the negative binomial distribution:

$$
X \sim NB\left(14, 0.5\right)
$$

We wish to compute $\textrm{Var}[X]$. In general, if $X \sim \textrm{NB}(r, p)$, then

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \dfrac{r(1-p)}{p^2}
$$

So, for our random variable

$$
X \sim \textrm{NB}\left(14, 0.5\right)
$$

we have the following variance:

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \dfrac{14(0.5)}{(0.5)^2} = 28
$$

Therefore, the variance is $28$.
