## Introduction

To calculate the line integral of a function $f(x,y)$ along a curve $C$, we use the formula

$$
\int\limits_C f(x, y) \, \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_a^b f(\mathbf r(t)) \, \| \mathbf r'(t) \| \, \textrm{d}t
$$

The curve $C$ must be parametrized by the vector function $\mathbf r(t)$ to apply this formula.

Now suppose we want to evaluate the line integral

$$
\displaystyle \int \limits_{C} \dfrac{y}{x} \,\textrm ds
$$

where $C$ is the section of the curve $y = x^2$ between the points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

![](<../Source/4.1.4. Line Integrals of Scalar Functions Over Paths Expressed as Functions of X/Images/64c817ee9579fb95f93ba7a9e86bcb86.png>)

To reduce the calculation of the line integral to a parametric case, we must first parametrize the curve.

Notice that the curve $C$ is of the form $y = y(x)$. Therefore, by setting $x=t$, we have

$$
y = y(t) = t^2
$$

This gives the following parametrization for $C$:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t, t^2 \right\rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 1
$$

Computing $\| \mathbf{r}'(t) \|$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
‖r^{′}(t)‖ &= \sqrt{(x^{′}(t))^{2} + (y^{′}(t))^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1^{2} + (2t)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1 + 4t^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Also, using $x=t$ and $y=t^2$, we have

$$
f(r(t)) = \frac{y}{x} = \frac{t^{2}}{t} = t
$$

Therefore, we can express the line integral in terms of $t$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)\frac{y}{x}ds &= ∫_{a}^{b}f(r(t))‖r^{′}(t)‖dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}t\sqrt{1 + 4t^{2}}dt
\end{aligned}
$$

## Reducing the Line Integral of a Scalar Function Along a Curve to a Definite Integral

**Example:** Consider the line integral $\displaystyle \int \limits_{C} \dfrac{y^2}{x} \,\textrm ds$, where $C$ is the section of the curve $y = \dfrac{x^3}3$ between the points $(0,0)$ and $(3,9)$.
Write the line integral in the form $\displaystyle\int\limits_a^b f(\mathbf r(t)) \, \| \mathbf r'(t) \| \, \textrm{d}t$.

**Explanation**

Notice that the given curve is of the form $y=y(x)$. Therefore, by setting $x=t$, we have

$$
y = y(t) = \dfrac{t^3}{3}
$$

This gives the following parametrization of the curve:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t, \dfrac{t^3}3 \right\rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 3
$$

Computing $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ and $\| \mathbf{r}'(t) \|$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r^{′}(t) &= \frac{dx}{dt}i + \frac{dy}{dt}j \\
&= \frac{d}{dt}(t)i + \frac{d}{dt}(\frac{t^{3}}{3})j \\
&= i + t^{2}j \\
‖r^{′}(t)‖ &= \sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1^{2} + (t^{2})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1 + t^{4}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since

$$
f(x,y) = \dfrac{y^2}{x}
$$

we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r(t)) &= ((\frac{t^{3}}{3})^{2})/(t) = \frac{1}{9}t^{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we can write the integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)f(x, y)ds &= ∫_{0}^{3}f(r(t))‖r^{′}(t)‖dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{3}\frac{1}{9}t^{5} \cdot \sqrt{1 + t^{4}} \cdot dt \\
&= \frac{1}{9}∫_{0}^{3}t^{5}\sqrt{1 + t^{4}}dt
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing a Line Integral of a Scalar Function Along a Curve Given in Explicit Cartesian Form

**Example:** Evaluate the integral $\displaystyle \int\limits_{C} \dfrac{y^3}{x^2} \, \text{d}s$, where the curve $C$ is given by $y = x$ for $\, 0 \le x \le 4$.

**Explanation**

Notice that the given curve is of the form $y=y(x)$. Therefore, by setting $x=t$, we have

$$
y = y(t) = t
$$

This gives the following parametrization of the curve:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t, \: t \right\rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 4
$$

Computing $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ and $\| \mathbf{r}'(t) \|$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r^{′}(t) &= \frac{dx}{dt}i + \frac{dy}{dt}j \\
&= \frac{d}{dt}(t)i + \frac{d}{dt}(t)j \\
&= i + j \\
‖r^{′}(t)‖ &= \sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1^{2} + 1^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since

$$
f(x,y) = \dfrac{y^3}{x^2}
$$

, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r(t)) &= \frac{t^{3}}{t^{2}} = t
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we can write the integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)\frac{y^{3}}{x^{2}}ds &= ∫_{0}^{4}f(r(t))‖r^{′}(t)‖dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{4}t \cdot \sqrt{2} \cdot dt \\
&= \sqrt{2}∫_{0}^{4}tdt \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}t^{2} \mid _{0}^{4} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(4^{2} - 0^{2}) \\
&= 8\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
