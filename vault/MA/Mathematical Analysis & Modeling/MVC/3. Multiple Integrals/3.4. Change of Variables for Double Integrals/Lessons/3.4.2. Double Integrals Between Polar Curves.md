## Introduction

Suppose we want to compute the double integral

$$
\displaystyle \iint\limits_{D}{{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,\mathrm d A}}
$$

where $D$ is a washer enclosed between two circles centered at $(0,0)$ with radii $1$ and $2$, respectively, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/f4b7b29cf6d1a235ada61cf5eeaf4eab.png>)

The easiest way to solve this problem is to convert it to polar coordinates. Let's define a transformation $\mathbf T$ as

$$
x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta
$$

The preimage of $D$ under the action of $\mathbf T$ is the rectangular region $\Delta$, given by

$$
\Delta = \big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 1 \leq r \leq 2, \: 0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi \big\}
$$

The region $D$ and its preimage $\Delta$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/2343b09b6603ea6f47f7e772c9cc9f27.png>)

The change of variables formula for polar coordinates states

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Therefore, using the change of variables formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}dA &= ∬_(Δ)\sqrt{(r\cos θ)^{2} + (r\sin θ)^{2}}rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{1}^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ)}rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{1}^{2}\sqrt{r^{2}} \cdot rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{1}^{2}r^{2}drdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluating this integral, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{1}^{2}r^{2}drdθ &= ∫_{0}^{2π}dθ∫_{0}^{2}r^{2}dr \\
&= 2π∫_{1}^{2}r^{2}dr \\
&= 2π \cdot [\frac{1}{3}r^{3}]_{1}^{2} \\
&= \frac{2}{3}π \cdot [2^{3} - 1^{3}] \\
&= \frac{2}{3}π \cdot 7 \\
&= \frac{14π}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\iint\limits_{D}{{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,\mathrm d A}} = \dfrac{14\pi}{3}
$$

## Converting an Integral Over an Annular Region to Polar Coordinates

**Example:** The region $D$ in the first quadrant is enclosed between the quarter-circles of radii $3$ and $4$, as shown above. What is
$\displaystyle \iint\limits_D \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2} \: \textrm{d}A$
expressed in polar coordinates?

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/dab6cca85da8e22d877207792771a513.png>)

**Explanation**

We will use the change of variables formula in the form

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

The region $D$ represents a portion of the washer enclosed between the circles of radii $3$ and $4$ centered at the origin. In polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the region can be expressed as

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 3 \leq r \leq 4, \: 0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2} \Big\}
$$

Therefore, using the change of variables formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)\frac{1}{x^{2} + y^{2}}dA &= ∬_(Δ)(1)/((r\sin θ)^{2} + (r\cos θ)^{2})rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}∫_{3}^{4}(r)/(r^{2}(\sin^{2} θ + \cos^{2} θ))drdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}∫_{3}^{4}\frac{1}{r}drdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Writing More Complex Regions in Polar Coordinates

Let's get some more practice at expressing regions in polar coordinates.

Consider the region enclosed by the curve

$$
x^2+(y-2)^2=4
$$

and the line $y=2$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/7c52a0abf285a299cb12764ca4151981.png>)

This region represents a portion of the disk of radius $2$ centered at $(0,2)$.

First, we recall the usual transformations from polar to Cartesian coordinates:

$$
{x = r\cos θ; y = r\sin θ
$$

Let's now use the above formulas to write the functions that bound our region in polar coordinates:

- For $x^2+(y-2)^2 = 4$, we obtain
$x^{2} + (y - 2)^{2}|= 4; (r\cos θ)^{2} + (r\sin θ - 2)^{2}|= 4; (r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ - 4r\sin θ + 4|= 4; r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ) - 4r\sin θ|= 0; r^{2} - 4r\sin θ|= 0; r(r - 4\sin θ)|= 0$.
Since $r$ cannot be identical to zero on the entire semicircle, we must have $r = 4\sin\theta$.
- For $y=2$, we get
$y|= 2; r\sin θ|= 2; r|= \frac{2}{\sin θ}; r|= 2\csc θ$.

Now, let's determine the limits for $r$ and $\theta$.

First of all, note that if we draw an arrow through the region from the origin, it enters the region via the curve

$$
r=2\csc\theta
$$

and exits via

$$
r=4\sin\theta
$$

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/4b50b933ef3fc8ce150d51976b99a80d.png>)

Secondly, our curves intersect at the points $(-2,2)$ and $(2,2)$. So, the entire region $D$ lies between the polar lines

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

and

$$
\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}
$$

as depicted below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/28ad7f237e1ddd631db33a5cdbe5c703.png>)

Therefore, in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the region can be expressed as follows:

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 2\csc\theta \leq r \leq 4\sin\theta, \: \dfrac{\pi}{4} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{3\pi}{4} \Big\}
$$

## Converting an Integral Over a Region Enclosed Between Two Curves to Polar Coordinates

**Example:** The region $D$ is enclosed between the curve $(x+1)^2+y^2=1$ and the line $x=-1$, as shown above. Express the following double integral in polar coordinates:

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/45342b77b0830e7179c7f7275385a285.png>)

$\displaystyle \iint\limits_D xy \: \textrm{d}A$

**Explanation**

We need to change the coordinate system from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates. To do this, we will use the change of variables formula in the form

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Notice that the region $D$ represents a portion of the disk of radius $1$ centered at $(-1,0)$.

Cartesian and polar coordinates are connected by the following equations:

$$
{x = r\cos θ; y = r\sin θ
$$

With that in mind, let's write our curves in polar coordinates.

- For $(x+1)^2+y^2=1$, we obtain
$(x + 1)^{2} + y^{2}|= 1; (r\cos θ + 1)^{2} + (r\sin θ)^{2}|= 1; r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + 2r\cos θ + 1 + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ|= 1; r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ) + 2r\cos θ|= 0; r^{2} + 2r\cos θ|= 0; r(r + 2\cos θ)|= 0$.
Since $r$ cannot be identical to zero on the entire semicircle, we must have $r = -2\cos\theta$.
- For $x=-1$, we get
$x|=-1; r\cos θ|=-1; r|=-\frac{1}{\cos θ}; r|=-\sec θ$.

Now, let's find the limits for the parameter $\theta$. Our curves intersect at the point $(-1,1)$. So, the entire region $D$ lies between the polar lines

$$
\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}
$$

and $\theta=\pi$, as depicted below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/2aebb0d409159e559ba17ec286da940f.png>)

As a result, the region can be expressed in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ as

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: -\sec\theta \leq r \leq -2\cos\theta, \: \dfrac{3\pi}{4} \leq \theta \leq {\pi} \Big\}
$$

Therefore, using the change of variables formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)xydA &= ∬_(Δ)(r\cos θ \cdot r\sin θ)rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{3π/4}^{π}∫_{-\sec θ}^{-2\cos θ}r^{3}\cos θ\sin θdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{3π/4}^{π}\cos θ\sin θ∫_{-\sec θ}^{-2\cos θ}r^{3}drdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating a Double Integral Over a Region Enclosed Between Two Curves

**Example:** Evaluate the double integral $\displaystyle \iint\limits_D \dfrac{2y}{x^2+y^2} \: \textrm{d}A$, where the region $D$ is shown above.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/a14cb4ba64dd6df3dde2fc1568d8d921.png>)

**Explanation**

We need to change the coordinate system from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates. To do this, we will use the change of variables formula in the form

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Notice that the region $D$ represents a portion of the disk of radius

$$
\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

centered at

$$
\left(0,\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\!
$$

Cartesian and polar coordinates are connected by the following equations:

$$
{x = r\cos θ; y = r\sin θ
$$

With that in mind, let's write our curves in polar coordinates.

- For $x^2+\left(y-\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^2=\dfrac14$, we obtain
$x^{2} + (y - \frac{1}{2})^{2}|= \frac{1}{4}; (r\cos θ)^{2} + (r\sin θ - \frac{1}{2})^{2}|= \frac{1}{4}; r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ - r\sin θ + \frac{1}{4}|= \frac{1}{4}; r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ) - r\sin θ|= 0; r^{2} - r\sin θ|= 0; r(r - \sin θ)|= 0$.
Since $r$ cannot be identical to zero on the entire semicircle, we must have $r = \sin\theta$.
- For $y=\dfrac{1}{2}$, we get
$y|= \frac{1}{2}; r\sin θ|= \frac{1}{2}; r|= \frac{1}{2\sin θ}; r|= \frac{1}{2}\csc θ$.

Now, let's find the limits for the parameter $\theta$. Our curves intersect at the point

$$
\left(\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{1}{2}\right)
$$

So, the entire region lies between the radial lines

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

and

$$
\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

as depicted below.

![](<../Source/3.4.2. Double Integrals Between Polar Curves/Images/d3184675aa31da9558e2a6f51cc60b70.png>)

As a result, the region can be expressed in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ as

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: \dfrac{1}{2}\csc\theta \leq r \leq \sin\theta, \: \dfrac{\pi}{4} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2} \Big\}
$$

Therefore, using the change of variables formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)\frac{2y}{x^{2} + y^{2}}dA &= ∬_(Δ)\frac{2r\sin θ}{r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ}rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2}∫_{1/2\csc θ}^{\sin θ}2\sin θdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2}2\sin θ∫_{1/2\csc θ}^{\sin θ}drdθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2}2\sin θ[r]_{1/2\csc θ}^{\sin θ}dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2}2\sin θ(\sin θ - \frac{1}{2}\csc θ)dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2}(2\sin^{2} θ - 1)dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/4}^{π/2} - \cos (2θ)dθ \\
&=-\frac{1}{2}[\sin (2θ)]_{π/4}^{π/2} \\
&=-\frac{1}{2}(0 - \sin (\frac{π}{2})) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$
