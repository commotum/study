## Introduction

Suppose we have two point masses $A$ and $B$ of mass $20\,\textrm{kg}$ and $40\,\textrm{kg}$, respectively, connected by a rigid, massless rod of length $1\,\textrm{m}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/fc89bbb4b2e7e8053a4413f5a81b873b.png>)

Our intuition tells us that if we place a **fulcrum** (i.e., a balancing point) at the *midpoint* between the two masses, the rod will tip down at the right end, as shown below.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/3942ec65acf89851b3934dedde08467d.png>)

To balance the rod perfectly, we must move the fulcrum closer to the larger mass.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/8ca82f4e75f06d9a7db1d3718cd02562.png>)

At what distance from the first mass ($d_1$ in the picture) should the fulcrum be placed for the masses to balance perfectly?

To answer this, we must introduce the concept of a **moment**:

> *The moment of a mass about a point $P$ equals the product of the mass and its displacement from $P$.*

Note the following:

- Loosely speaking, moment describes the amount of *turning effect* produced by a mass about a point (usually the tip of the fulcrum).
- For masses measured in kilograms and distances in meters, the units of a moment are $\textrm{kg}\cdot\textrm m$.
- Moment is a *vector* quantity. A moment is either *positive* (representing *clockwise* induced motion) or *negative* (for *counterclockwise* motion). A moment's sign (positive/clockwise or negative/counterclockwise) is determined by the direction the rod would rotate if the corresponding mass were the only mass in the system.

Let's now see how to use moments to calculate the required distance $d_1$.

---

<a id="the-principle-of-moments"></a>
## The Principle of Moments

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/271f430df6cbf1e7937f02e2552b88ea.png>)

Let's write down the moment of each mass *about the fulcrum*:

- The moment of mass $A$ equals $-20\cdot d_1 = -20d_1$ (negative since the motion induced by this mass would be counterclockwise).
- The moment of mass $B$ equals $40\cdot d_2 = 40d_2$ (positive since the motion induced by this mass would be clockwise).

The **principle of moments** tells us that, for the two masses to balance, *the sum of the moments must equal zero*.

So in our case, we have

$$
40d_2 - 20d_1 = 0
$$

Using the fact that the rod has length $1\,\textrm{m}$, we have

$$
d_1 + d_2 = 1\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad d_2 = 1 - d_1
$$

Substituting this into the first equation, we get

$$
40(1-d_1) - 20d_1 = 0
$$

which solves to give

$$
d_1 = \dfrac23\,\textrm m
$$

Therefore, if the fulcrum is placed at a distance of

$$
\dfrac23\,\textrm m
$$

from $A$, the rod would balance perfectly.

Before we move on, note the following:

- The fulcrum also has mass. However, since the distance between the fulcrum and itself is zero, its moment about the fulcrum is zero, and it doesn't feature when we apply the principle of moments. Thus, taking moments about the fulcrum was convenient because we don't need to know its mass! Generally, taking moments about a convenient point can save us a lot of work.
- Selecting a positive sign for a clockwise moment and negative for a counterclockwise moment *may* seem counterintuitive. After all, positive angles are usually measured counterclockwise. However, we'll soon see that selecting the signs using this convention makes it easy to model moment problems using coordinate axes.

<a id="applying-the-principle-of-moments"></a>
## Applying the Principle of Moments

**Example:** Two point masses $A$ and $B$ are connected by a rigid, massless rod of length $9\,\rm{ft}$ that is perfectly balanced on a fulcrum, as shown above. If $B$ has mass $10\,\rm{lb}$ and is $4\,\rm{ft}$ from the fulcrum, find the mass of $A$.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/ce815ce5a9571e39d171d6fccd5e66bf.png>)

**Explanation**

Let $m$ be the mass of $A$.
The length of the rod is $9\,\rm{ft}$ and the distance between the fulcrum and $B$ is $4\,\rm{ft}$. So, the distance between the fulcrum and $A$ is

$$
9 - 4 = 5\,\rm{ft}
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/5fbffe1d0b5c5d2ef8b9c2618163c14b.png>)

Let's write down the moment of each mass about the fulcrum:

- The moment of mass $A$ equals $-m \cdot 5 = -5m\,\textrm{lb}\cdot\textrm{ft}$ (negative since the motion induced by this mass would be counterclockwise).
- The moment of mass $B$ equals $10 \cdot 4 = 40\,\textrm{lb}\cdot\textrm{ft}$ (positive since the motion induced by this mass would be clockwise).

The *principle of moments* tells us that for the two masses to balance perfectly, the sum of the moments must equal zero.

Therefore, in this case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
40 - 5m &= 0 \\
5m &= 40 \\
m &= 8lb
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="moments-about-the-origin"></a>
## Moments About the Origin

As discussed, we denote the direction of a moment as either clockwise or counterclockwise, depending on the direction in which it causes the rod to rotate about the fulcrum.

In general, the **total moment** about the origin $O$ of a collection of masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ along the $x$-axis is defined as

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{O} &= m_{1} \cdot x_{1} + m_{2} \cdot x_{2} + ⋯ + m_{n}x_{n} \\
&= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

The total moment is a single number that indicates the combined turning effect of the system. A total moment of zero (as in the last example, taking the fulcrum as the origin) means no combined turning effect, so the system balances perfectly.

The moment of a single mass $m_i$ about the origin is equals $m_i x_i$, and its sign is determined by its position along the $x$-axis:

- If $x_i > 0$, the induced motion is *clockwise*, and the associated moment is *positive*.
- If $x_i < 0$, the induced motion is *counterclockwise*, and the associated moment is *negative*.

Consider the two point masses below, where the distances along the $x$-axis are measured in meters. Let's find the total moment of this system about the origin.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/bea90df14c74965d78ecad5e0834b940.png>)

In this case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}m_{1} &= 5, & m_{2}|= 4, \\ x_{1}|=-2, & x_{2}|= 3\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

We calculate the total moment of this system about the origin as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{O} &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i} \\
&= m_{1}x_{1} + m_{2}x_{2} \\
&= 5 \cdot (-2) + 4 \cdot 3 \\
&=-10 + 12 \\
&= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total moment of the system is $2\,\textrm{kg} \cdot \textrm{m}$.

<a id="sums-of-moments-for-masses-distributed-along-the-x-axis"></a>
## Sums of Moments for Masses Distributed Along the X-Axis

**Example:** Consider the collection of point masses $m_1=1\,\textrm{kg}$, $m_2=2\,\textrm{kg}$, $m_3=3\,\textrm{kg}$, and $m_4=3\,\textrm{kg}$ located at $x_1=-1\,\textrm{m}$, $x_2 = 1\,\textrm{m}$, $x_3 = 3\,\textrm{m}$, and $x_4 = 5 \, \textrm{m}$, respectively, along the $x$-axis. Find the total moment of this system about the origin.

**Explanation**

The moment about the origin $O$ of a collection of masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ along the $x$-axis is given by

$$
M_O = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i x_i
$$

In our case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}m_{1} &= 1, & m_{2}|= 2, & m_{3}|= 3, & m_{4}|= 3, \\ x_{1}|=-1, & x_{2}|= 1, & x_{3}|= 3, & x_{4}|= 5\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

We calculate the total moment of this system about the origin as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{O} &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i} \\
&= m_{1}x_{1} + m_{2}x_{2} + m_{3}x_{3} + m_{4}x_{4} \\
&= 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 5 \\
&=-1 + 2 + 9 + 15 \\
&= 25
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total moment of the system is $25\,\textrm{kg} \cdot \textrm{m}$.

<a id="center-of-mass"></a>
## Center of Mass

Suppose we have a collection of masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ along the $x$-axis.

The system's **center of mass** is the point $\bar{x}$ on the $x$-axis where the system's total mass could be concentrated to give the same total moment as the original system.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/32e8140b26cb651550b4b55eed79eaf5.png>)

Let's derive the equation for the center of mass $\bar{x}$ for this system.

The total moment of the distributed masses about the origin is given by

$$
M_{O} = ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i}
$$

If the total mass of the system is

$$
m = m_1+m_2+\cdots m_n = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i
$$

then the total moment about the origin of the single mass $m$ located at $\bar{x}$ equals

$$
m \bar{x}
$$

Equating our two moments, we get

$$
M_O = m \bar{x}
$$

Solving for $\bar{x}$, we have

$$
\bar{x} = \dfrac{M_O}{m}
$$

The center of mass is an important physical quantity, as it gives the location where a fulcrum would need to be placed for the system to balance perfectly.

<a id="calculating-the-center-of-mass-for-a-collection-of-masses-along-the-x-axis"></a>
## Calculating the Center of Mass for a Collection of Masses Along the X-Axis

**Example:** Consider the collection of point masses above, where the $x$-coordinates are measured in meters. What is the $x$-coordinate of the center of mass for this system?

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/7d1080a4e47e1448ba5bd6fef71f207e.png>)

**Explanation**

The center of mass, denoted $\overline{x}$, of a collection of point masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ along the $x$-axis is given by

$$
\overline{x} = \dfrac{M_O}{m}
$$

where

- $\displaystyle m = \sum\limits_{i=1}^n m_i$ is the total mass of the system,
- $\displaystyle M_O = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i x_i$ is the total moment of the system about the origin $O$.

In our case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}m_{1} &= 2, & m_{2}|= 4, & m_{3}|= 3, \\ x_{1}|=-1, & x_{2}|= 2, & x_{3}|= 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the total mass of the system is

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i} \\
&= m_{1} + m_{2} + m_{3} \\
&= 2 + 4 + 3 \\
&= 9
\end{aligned}
$$

and the total moment of the system about the origin is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{O} &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i} \\
&= m_{1}x_{1} + m_{2}x_{2} + m_{3}x_{3} \\
&= 2 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 4 \\
&=-2 + 8 + 12 \\
&= 18
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\overline{x} = \dfrac{M_O}{m} = \dfrac{18}{9}=2
$$

<a id="center-of-mass-for-objects-in-the-plane"></a>
## Center of Mass for Objects in the Plane

When a collection of masses is distributed in two or more dimensions of space, we must take moments about a fixed **axis.**

In the following work, we will only consider moments about the coordinate axes.

Consider the collection of point masses below, where the distances along the $x$- and $y$-axes are measured in meters.

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/6492cd4515a9bf09990d7b37bfefc73e.png>)

The center of mass of this two-dimensional system, denoted $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$, is the point where the entire mass of the system could be concentrated to give the same total moment about the $x$- and $y$-axes as the original system.

More precisely, the center of mass of a collection of point masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)$ in the plane is given by

$$
(\overline{x},\overline{y}) = \left(\dfrac{M_y}{m},\dfrac{M_x}{m}\right)
$$

where

- $\displaystyle m = \sum\limits_{i=1}^n m_i$ is the total mass of the system,
- $\displaystyle M_x = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i y_i$ is the total moment of the system about the $x$-axis, and
- $\displaystyle M_y = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i x_i$ is the total moment of the system about the $y$-axis.

Let's compute $\bar{x}$, the $x$-coordinate of the center of mass for this system.

First, we denote the masses as

$$
m_1 = 3, \qquad m_2 = 5, \qquad m_3 = 4
$$

Then, the corresponding $x$-coordinates of these masses are

$$
x_1 = -2, \qquad x_2 = -1, \qquad x_3 = 4
$$

So, the total mass of the system is

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i} \\
&= m_{1} + m_{2} + m_{3} \\
&= 3 + 5 + 4 \\
&= 12kg
\end{aligned}
$$

and the moment of the system about the $\boldsymbol y$**-axis** is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{y} &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}x_{i} \\
&= m_{1}x_{1} + m_{2}x_{2} + m_{3}x_{3} \\
&= 3 \cdot (-2) + 5 \cdot (-1) + 4 \cdot 4 \\
&=-6 - 5 + 16 \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\overline{x} = \dfrac{M_y}{m} = \dfrac{5}{12}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-center-of-mass-for-a-collection-of-masses-in-the-plane"></a>
## Calculating the Center of Mass for a Collection of Masses in the Plane

**Example:** Consider the collection of point masses shown above. Given that the center of mass for the collection is located at $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$, what is the value of $\overline{y}$?

![](<../Source/6.2.3. Moments and Center of Mass/Images/a98849cc62e6b254955495d1c98f4f85.png>)

**Explanation**

The center of mass of a collection of point masses $m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n$ located at $(x_1y_1),(x_2,y_2),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)$ on the plane is given by

$$
(\overline{x},\overline{y}) = \left(\dfrac{M_y}{m},\dfrac{M_x}{m}\right)
$$

where

- $\displaystyle m = \sum\limits_{i=1}^n m_i$ is the total mass of the system,
- $\displaystyle M_x = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i y_i$ is the total moment of the system about the $x$-axis, and
- $\displaystyle M_y = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i x_i$ is the total moment of the system about the $y$-axis.

Since we require the $y$-coordinate of the center of mass, we need to compute the moment about the $x$-axis.

Let's denote the masses as

$$
m_1 = 3, \qquad m_2 = 5, \qquad m_3 = 1
$$

Then, the corresponding $y$-coordinates of the masses are

$$
y_1 = 3, \qquad y_2 = 0, \qquad y_3 = 3
$$

So, the total mass of the system is

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i} \\
&= m_{1} + m_{2} + m_{3} \\
&= 3 + 5 + 1 \\
&= 9
\end{aligned}
$$

and the moment of the system about the $x$-axis is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{x} &= ∑_(i = 1)^(n)m_{i}y_{i} \\
&= m_{1}y_{1} + m_{2}y_{2} + m_{3}y_{3} \\
&= 3 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 3 \\
&= 9 + 0 + 3 \\
&= 12
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\overline{y} = \dfrac{M_x}{m} = \dfrac{12}{9} = \dfrac{4}{3}
$$
