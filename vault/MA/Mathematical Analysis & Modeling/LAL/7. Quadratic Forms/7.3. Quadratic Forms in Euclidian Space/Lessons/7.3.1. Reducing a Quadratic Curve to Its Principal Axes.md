## Introduction

Let $A$ be a $2\times 2$ symmetric matrix with real entries. As we know, there exists an orthonormal change of variables

$$
\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{y}
$$

that transforms the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^TA\mathbf{x}$ into the quadratic form $\mathbf{y}^TD\mathbf{y}$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix.

The columns of the matrix $P$ are the (normalized) unit eigenvectors of $A$, which are mutually orthogonal. These are called the **principal axes** of the quadratic form.

Let's use this knowledge to find out which curve is defined by the equation

$$
6x_1^2+4x_1x_2+3x_2^2= 14
$$

and see the geometric interpretation of the principal axes.

If we plot our curve using a graphical calculator, we obtain the following:

![](<../Source/7.3.1. Reducing a Quadratic Curve to Its Principal Axes/Images/34cef09ac88b98d6a2ae6351d0835a47.png>)

It looks like an ellipse. Let's now find our coordinate system, in which the equation of the curve will be the simplest.

First, we consider the corresponding quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})={\color{blue}6}x_1^2+{\color{red}4}x_1x_2+{\color{blue}3}x_2^2
$$

which we reduce to its canonical (diagonal) form.

So, we write down the matrix of $Q(\mathbf{x})$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, we find the eigenvalues of $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}6 - λ & 2 \\ 2 & 3 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 9λ + 14 &= 0 \\
λ &= 7, 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let's find the principal axes of the curve.

- Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-7I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_1$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_1 \| = \sqrt{5}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_2$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_2 \| = \sqrt{5}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the principal axes of our quadratic form (and the corresponding curve) are

$\qquad$ $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

Notice that in the diagram below, the principal axes correspond to the axes $y_1$ and $y_2$.

![](<../Source/7.3.1. Reducing a Quadratic Curve to Its Principal Axes/Images/985b65a4dfd49288dfa8229ce19ed99b.png>)

In the new coordinate system, our quadratic form can be reduced to

$$
7y_1^2 + 2y_2^2
$$

Therefore, the curve has the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
7y_{1}^{2} + 2y_{2}^{2} &= 14 \\
(y_{1}^{2})/((\sqrt{2})^{2}) + (y_{2}^{2})/((\sqrt{7})^{2}) &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

This is an ellipse with semiaxes $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt{7}$.

## Identifying a Quadratic Plane Curve

**Example:** Which curve is defined by the equation $6x_1^2+8x_1x_2-9x_2^2= 70$?

**Explanation**

Consider the quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})=6x_1^2+8x_1x_2-9x_2^2
$$

To determine the curve defined by the given equation, we reduce $Q(\mathbf{x})$ to its canonical (diagonal) form.

First, we write down the matrix of $Q(\mathbf{x})$, which is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}6 & 4 \\ 4 & -9\end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, we find the eigenvalues of $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}6 - λ & 4 \\ 4 & -9 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
λ^{2} + 3λ - 70 &= 0 \\
λ &= 7,-10
\end{aligned}
$$

So, our quadratic form can be reduced to $7y_1^2 -10 y_2^2$.

In the new variables, the curve has the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
7y_{1}^{2} - 10y_{2}^{2} &= 70 \\
\frac{y_{1}^{2}}{10} - \frac{y_{2}^{2}}{7} &= 1 \\
(y_{1}^{2})/((\sqrt{10})^{2}) - (y_{2}^{2})/((\sqrt{7})^{2}) &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a hyperbola with semiaxes $\sqrt{10}$ and $\sqrt{7}$.

## Some Special Cases

In some special cases, a quadratic equation might generate a degenerate (real) curve or even an empty set.

- For example, consider the quadratic equation $-3x_1^2+8x_1x_2+3x_2^2 = 0$.
Reducing the left-hand side to the canonical form, we obtain
$5y_1^2 - 5y_2^2 = 0$
which represents two intersecting lines $y_1 = y_2$ and $y_1 = -y_2$.

- Now consider the quadratic equation $-2x_1^2+8x_1x_2-8x_2^2 = 10$.
Reducing the left-hand side to the canonical form, we obtain
$-10y_2^2 =10$
which represents an empty set as the equation $y_2^2 = -1$ does not have any real solution.
- Finally, reducing the left-hand side of $x_1^2-4x_1x_2+4x_2^2 = 5$
to the canonical form, we obtain
$5y_2^2 =5$
which represents a pair of parallel lines $y_2 = 1$ and $y_2=-1$.

## Finding the Principal Axes of a Quadratic Plane Curve Given the Corresponding Eigenvalues

**Example:** Consider the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ and the quadratic curve $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x} = 1$. Given that $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=3$ and $\lambda_2=1$, find the principal axes of the curve.

**Explanation**

Notice that $A$ is symmetric. The principal axes of the curve

$$
\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x} = 1
$$

are the vectors that give an orthonormal eigenvector basis for $A$. Let's now find these eigenvectors.

- For $\lambda_1=3$, we have
$A - 3I|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-3I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_1$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_1 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_2=1$, we have
$A - 1I|= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix} - 1\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_2$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_2 \| = \sqrt{2}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the principal axes of our curve are

$\qquad$ $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

## Finding the Principal Axes of a Quadratic Plane Curve

**Example:** Find the principal axes of the curve $19x_1^2-6x_1x_2+11x_2^2 = 20$.

**Explanation**

First, consider the quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})=19x_1^2-6x_1x_2+11x_2^2
$$

The matrix of the form is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}19 & -3 \\ -3 & 11\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice that $A$ is symmetric. The principal axes of the curve

$$
\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x} = 20
$$

are the vectors that form an orthonormal eigenvector basis of $A$.

By solving the characteristic equation

$$
\mid A-\lambda I \mid = 0
$$

we obtain

$\qquad$

$$
\lambda_1=20 \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \lambda_2=10
$$

Let's now find the eigenvectors.

- For $\lambda_2=20$, we have
$A - 20I|= \begin{bmatrix}19 & -3 \\ -3 & 11\end{bmatrix} - 20\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1 & -3 \\ -3 & -9\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-20I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_2$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_2 \| = \sqrt{10}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

- For $\lambda_1=10$, we have
$A - 10I|= \begin{bmatrix}19 & -3 \\ -3 & 11\end{bmatrix} - 10\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}9 & -3 \\ -3 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-10I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ Dividing $\mathbf{v}_1$ by its norm $\| \mathbf{v}_1 \| = \sqrt{10}$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the principal axes of our curve are

$\qquad$ $\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$
