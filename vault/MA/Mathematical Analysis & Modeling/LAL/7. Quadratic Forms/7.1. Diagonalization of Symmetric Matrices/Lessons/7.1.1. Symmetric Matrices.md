## Introduction

A square matrix $A$ is **symmetric** if it is equal to its transpose:

$$
A^T = A
$$

Equivalently, an $n\times n$ matrix is symmetric if

$$
a_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

for all

$$
1 \leq i,j\leq n
$$

If $A$ is symmetric, this really means that it is symmetric across its main diagonal.

For example, the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 4 \\ 4 & -5\end{bmatrix}
$$

is symmetric since $A^T = A$.

On the other hand, the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & 4 \\ 2 & -5\end{bmatrix}
$$

is not symmetric. Indeed, since

$$
b_{12} \neq b_{21}
$$

we have

$$
B^T \neq B
$$

## Identifying Symmetric Matrices

**Example:** Which of the following matrices are symmetric?

$\begin{bmatrix}1 & -6 \\ -6 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

A square matrix $A$ is symmetric if it is equal to its transpose:
$A = A^T$

Equivalently,

$$
a_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

for all $i,j$. In other words, $A$ is symmetric across its main diagonal.

With that in mind, let's examine each matrix in turn.

- $P$ is a symmetric matrix. Indeed, $P=P^T$.
- $Q$ is a symmetric matrix. Indeed, $Q=Q^T$.
- $R$ is *not* a symmetric matrix. Since $r_{12} \neq r_{21}$, we obtain $R \neq R^T$.
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the correct answer is "$P$ and $Q$ only."

## Properties of Symmetric Matrices

Suppose that $A$ is a symmetric matrix. Then, we have the following properties:

- $-A$ is symmetric
- $A^m$ is symmetric for any positive integer $m$
- $A^{-1}$ is symmetric for nonsingular $A$

If $A$ and $B$ are $n\times n$ symmetric matrices, we have the following properties:

- $A+B$ is symmetric
- The product $A\cdot B$ is *not* always symmetric. For example, if
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
then the product $A\cdot B$ is
$A \cdot B|= \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\cdot$
which is *not* symmetric.

Finally, the following two properties can be used to construct symmetric matrices:

- $A + A^T$ is symmetric for any *square* matrix $A$
- $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ are symmetric for *any* matrix $A$

The last property is true for *any* $m\times n$ matrix. This property is particularly useful, as we'll discover in future lessons.

## Identifying True Statements Regarding Properties of Symmetric Matrices

**Example:** Which of the following statements are true?

1. If $A$ is *any* $n\times n$ matrix, then $A+A^T$ is always symmetric.
2. The difference between two $n\times n$ symmetric matrices is symmetric.
3. The product of two $n\times n$ symmetric matrices is symmetric.

**Explanation**

A square matrix $A$ is symmetric if it is equal to its transpose:
$A = A^T$

Equivalently,

$$
a_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

for all $i,j$. In other words, $A$ is symmetric across its main diagonal.

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is true. Indeed, for any matrix $A$, we have
$(A + A^{T})^{T}|= A^{T} + (A^{T})^{T}; = A^{T} + A; = A + A^{T}$.
- Statement II is true. Indeed, the sum or difference between two symmetric matrices is symmetric.
- Statement III is false. As a counterexample, consider the symmetric matrices
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
Their product is not symmetric:
$AB|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."
