## Introduction

The **Gram-Schmidt process** is an algorithm that we can use to construct an *orthogonal* basis if we are given a non-orthogonal basis to start with.

For example, consider the basis $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$ for the subspace

$$
V=\textrm{Span} \{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}
$$

where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$

We can use the above basis to construct an *orthogonal* basis for the given $2$-dimensional subspace. To do this, we use the Gram-Schmidt process, which involves $2$ steps:

- **Step 1:** Set ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1}=\mathbf{a}_1$.
- **Step 2:** Find a vector $\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2$ in $V$ that's orthogonal to $\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1$. This can be achieved by computing
$v_{2}|= a_{2}- proj_{v_{1}}a_{2}; = a_{2} - \frac{a_{2} \cdot v_{1}}{v_{1} \cdot v_{1}}v_{1}$.![](<../Source/6.4.1. The Gram-Schmidt Process for Two Vectors/Images/417c5afa5e0daa667d60c63ed4a75b5d.png>)
Notice that $\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2$ is written as a linear combination of $\mathbf{a}_1(={\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1})$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$. So this means that ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \in V$, and also ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \perp {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1}$.
Moreover,
$\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_2\} = V = \textrm{Span}\{{\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \}$.

After applying the Gram-Schmidt process, we get the set

$$
\mathcal{B}=\{{\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2}\}
$$

which is an orthogonal basis for $V$.

So, in our example, we start by setting $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ Then, we find the vector $\mathbf{v}_2$ that's orthogonal to $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{v}_1\}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{2} &= a_{2} - \frac{a_{2} \cdot v_{1}}{v_{1} \cdot v_{1}}v_{1} \\
&= a_{2} - (4 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot (-1))/(1^{2} + (-1)^{2})v_{1} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} - (-1) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ is an orthogonal basis for $V$.

## Finding an Orthogonal Basis For the Span of Two Vectors

**Example:** Consider the subspace $V$ and an orthogonal basis $\mathcal B$ of $V$, given below. Given that $\mathcal B$ is derived from $V$ using the Gram-Schmidt process, what is the value of $\dfrac{a}{c}$?

$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, let's denote

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

We proceed to find an orthogonal basis using the Gram-Schmidt process, as follows.

- **Step 1**: We set $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 2**: Next, we find $\mathbf{v}_2$, using the formula
$v_{2}|= a_{2} - \frac{a_{2} \cdot v_{1}}{v_{1} \cdot v_{1}}v_{1}$.
Applying the formula for $\mathbf v_2$ in our case gives
$v_{2}|= a_{2} - ((-3) \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 2)/(1^{2} + (-1)^{2} + 2^{2})v_{1}; = \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 5 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} + \frac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}-\frac{7}{3} \\ \frac{13}{3} \\ \frac{10}{3}\end{bmatrix}$.

Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ is an orthogonal basis for $V$.

Finally,

$$
a=-\dfrac 7 3
$$

$$
c=\dfrac{10}{3}
$$

and

$$
\dfrac{a}{c} = \dfrac{\left(-\dfrac{7}{3}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{10}{3}\right)} = -\dfrac{7}{10}
$$

## Scaling the Vectors After the Gram-Schmidt Process

Sometimes it's helpful to "clean up" the result of a Gram-Schmidt process by scaling the resulting vectors.

For example, suppose that the set $\mathcal B$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

is an orthogonal basis for $\textrm{Span} \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \}$.

Since the vector $\mathbf{v}_2$ lies in $\textrm{Span} \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \}$ and $\mathbf{v}_1 \perp \mathbf{v}_2$, the vector $3\mathbf{v}_2$ also lies in $\textrm{Span} \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \}$ and $\mathbf{v}_1 \perp 3\mathbf{v}_2$. Therefore, taking

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-7 \\ 13 \\ 10\end{bmatrix}
$$

instead of $\mathbf{v}_2$, we again obtain an orthogonal basis for $\textrm{Span} \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

But this time, the second vector in the basis looks slightly nicer and is probably easier to work with.

## Finding an Orthogonal Basis For the Column Space of a Matrix

**Example:** Consider the subspace $\textrm{Col}(A)$ and an orthogonal basis $\mathcal B$ of $\textrm{Col}(A)$, where $A$ and $\mathcal B$ are given below. Given that $\mathcal B$ is derived from $\textrm{Col}(A)$ using the Gram-Schmidt process, what is the value of $\dfrac{c}{a}$?

$\begin{bmatrix}1 & -5 \\ -2 & -1 \\ 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Recall that $\textrm{Col}(A)$ is the span of the columns of $A$.

First, let's denote

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

We proceed to find an orthogonal basis using the Gram-Schmidt process, as follows.

- **Step 1**: We set $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 2**: Next, we find $\mathbf{v}_2$, by using the formula
$v_{2}|= a_{2} - \frac{a_{2} \cdot v_{1}}{v_{1} \cdot v_{1}}v_{1}$.
Applying the formula for $\mathbf v_2$ in our case gives
$v_{2}|= a_{2} - ((-5) \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot (-2) + (-3) \cdot 2)/(1^{2} + (-2)^{2} + 2^{2})v_{1}; = \begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ -1 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}-4 \\ -3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$.

Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ is an orthogonal basis for $\textrm{Col}(A)$.

Finally, $a=-4$, $c=-1$, and

$$
\dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{-1}{-4} = \dfrac{1}{4}
$$

## Using the Gram-Schmidt Process to Find an Orthonormal Basis

Given a basis $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2\}$ for a subspace $V$, we can use the Gram-Schmidt process to find an *orthonormal* basis of the subspace.

To do this, we simply execute the Gram-Schmidt process as before to obtain an orthogonal basis $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}$, and then we normalize each vector in this basis to get the orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{u}_2\}$, where

$$
\mathbf{u}_1 = \dfrac{\mathbf{v}_1}{\Vert \mathbf{v}_1 \Vert}, \quad \mathbf{u}_2 = \dfrac{\mathbf{v}_2}{\Vert \mathbf{v}_2 \Vert}
$$

---

<a id="finding-an-orthonormal-basis-of-a-vector-space"></a>
## Finding an Orthonormal Basis of a Vector Space

**Example:** Consider the subspace $V$ and an **orthonormal** basis $\mathcal B$ of $V$, given below. Given that $\mathcal B$ is derived from $V$ using the Gram-Schmidt process, what is the value of $\begin{vmatrix}a & + & b & + & c\end{vmatrix}$?
'
$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, let's denote

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

We proceed to find an orthogonal basis using the Gram-Schmidt process, as follows.

- **Step 1**: We set $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$
- **Step 2**: Next, we find $\mathbf{v}_2$, by using the formula
$v_{2}|= a_{2} - \frac{a_{2} \cdot v_{1}}{v_{1} \cdot v_{1}}v_{1}$.
Applying the formula for $\mathbf v_2$ in our case gives
$= a_{2} - (6 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot (-1))/(2^{2} + 2^{2} + (-1)^{2})v_{1}; = \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 0 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$.

Now, we normalize each vector to get an orthonormal basis:

- $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$
- $\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{3} \\ \frac{2}{3} \\ -\frac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

is an orthonormal basis for $V$.

Finally,

$$
a=\dfrac{2}{3}
$$

$$
b=-\dfrac1 3
$$

$$
c=\dfrac{2}{3}
$$

and

$$
\begin{vmatrix}a & + & b & + & c & = \left & \dfrac{2}{3} \right & + \left & -\dfrac{1}{3}\right & + \left & \dfrac{2}{3} \right\end{vmatrix} = \dfrac 5 3
$$
