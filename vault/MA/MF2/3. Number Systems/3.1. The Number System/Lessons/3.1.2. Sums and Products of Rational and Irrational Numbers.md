## Introduction

When adding numbers, it's usually possible to say whether the sum is rational or irrational.

We have three possible cases:

- The sum of two rational numbers is *always* rational. Rational numbers can be represented as fractions of the form $\dfrac ab$ (where $a$ and $b$ are integers), and adding two of these fractions always produces another fraction.
- The sum of a rational and an irrational number is *always* irrational. To demonstrate, let's consider the following example:
$\sqrt 2 + \dfrac12$
It's easy to see that this is irrational. Writing down the sum in decimal form, we get
$1.414213562… + 0.5 = 1.914213562…$
which is irrational since the numbers after the decimal point extend forever with no regular pattern.
- The sum of two irrational numbers is *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational! For example, the following sum is irrational:
$\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3 = 3.146\,264\,37\ldots$
However, the following sum is rational:
$\sqrt 2 + (-\sqrt 2) = 0$

---

<a id="a-summary"></a>
## A Summary

To summarize:

- the sum (or difference) of two rational numbers is *always* rational:
$\textrm{rational} + \textrm{rational} = \textrm{rational}$
- the sum (or difference) of a rational number and an irrational number is *always* irrational:
$\textrm{rational} + \textrm{irrational} = \textrm{irrational}$
- the sum (or difference) of two irrational numbers is *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational.

<a id="classifying-a-sum-or-difference-of-numbers"></a>
## Classifying a Sum or Difference of Numbers

**Example:** Which of the following expressions is irrational?

1. $\dfrac{3}{5} + 5^{9}$
2. $7 + \sqrt{5}$
3. $\sqrt{64} + 7^3$

**Explanation**

Recall that:

- the sum of two rational numbers is *always* rational:
$\textrm{rational} + \textrm{rational} = \textrm{rational}$
- the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is *always* irrational:
$\textrm{rational} + \textrm{irrational} = \textrm{irrational}$
- the sum of two irrational numbers *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational.

With that in mind, let's examine the statements.

- Expression I is not irrational. Since both $\dfrac{3}{5}$ and $5^9$ are rational, their sum must be rational.
- Expression II is irrational. Since $7$ is rational and $\sqrt{5}$ is irrational, their sum must be irrational.
- Expression III is not irrational. Notice that $\sqrt{64} = 8$ is a rational number. The number $7^3$ is rational too. So, their sum must be rational.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

## Products of Rational and Irrational Numbers

When multiplying real numbers, it's usually possible to say whether the product is rational or irrational.

We have three possible cases:

- The product of two rational numbers is *always* rational since the result must be a fraction of the form $\dfrac ab$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers.
- The product of a *nonzero* rational number and an irrational number is *always* irrational. To demonstrate, let's consider the following example:
$2\cdot \sqrt 2$
It's easy to see that this is irrational. Writing down the product in decimal form, we get
$2 \cdot 1.414213562… = 2.828427125…$
which is irrational since the numbers after the decimal point extend forever with no regular pattern.
- The product of two irrational numbers is *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational! For example, the following product is irrational:
$\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{3}|= \sqrt{2 \cdot 3}; = \sqrt{6}$
However, the following product is rational:
$\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2}|= \sqrt{2 \cdot 2}; = \sqrt{2^{2}}; = 2$

---

<a id="a-summary"></a>
## A Summary

To summarize:

- the product (or quotient) of two rational numbers is *always* rational:
$\textrm{rational} \times \textrm{rational} = \textrm{rational}$
- the product (or quotient) of a nonzero rational number and an irrational number is *always* irrational:
$\textrm{rational} \times \textrm{irrational} = \textrm{irrational}$
- the product (or quotient) of two irrational numbers is *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational.

Finally, it's worth pointing out that if $a$ is irrational, then the second rule implies that

$$
\dfrac1a
$$

is also irrational.

<a id="classifying-a-product-or-quotient-of-numbers"></a>
## Classifying a Product or Quotient of Numbers

**Example:** Which of the following expressions are irrational?

1. $6^3 \cdot \sqrt{7}$
2. $\dfrac{1}{10} \cdot \sqrt{11}$
3. $\dfrac{1}{2^9} \cdot \sqrt{25}$

**Explanation**

Recall that

- the product of two rational numbers is *always* rational:
$\textrm{rational} \times \textrm{rational} = \textrm{rational}$
- the product of a nonzero rational number and an irrational number is *always* irrational:
$\textrm{rational} \times \textrm{irrational} = \textrm{irrational}$
- the product of two irrational numbers is *sometimes* rational and *sometimes* irrational.

With that in mind, let's examine the statements.

- Expression I is irrational. Since $6^3$ is rational and $\sqrt{7}$ is irrational, their product must be irrational.
- Expression II is irrational. Since $\dfrac{1}{10}$ is rational and $\sqrt{11}$ is irrational, their product must be irrational.
- Expression III is rational. Notice that $\sqrt{25} = 5$ is a rational number. The number $\dfrac{1}{2^9}$ is rational too. So, their product must be rational.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."
