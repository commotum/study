## Introduction

The **experimental probability** of an event is the fraction of the time that the event occurred in an experiment.

For example, suppose we conduct an experiment in which we toss a coin $100$ times, and we find that the coin lands heads up $44$ times.

To compute the *experimental probability*, we need to take the number of times the coin landed heads up, and divide it by the number of times we flipped the coin:

$$
P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{44}{100} = 0.44
$$

So, the experimental probability of getting heads is $0.44$.

The process that we repeat (here, coin tosses) is called the **trial**, while the outcome that we are looking to obtain (here, a head) is an **event**.

In general, the formula for the experimental probability is

$$
\text{Experimental probability} = \dfrac{\text{number of event occurrences}}{\text{total number of trials}}
$$

<a id="using-experimental-probability-for-predictions"></a>
## Using Experimental Probability for Predictions

Once we've calculated the experimental probability of an event, we can use the experimental probability to predict how often the event will occur in a different experiment.

For example, suppose we flip a coin $100$ times and it lands heads up $44$ of those times. Then the experimental probability is

$$
P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{44}{100} = 0.44
$$

and we can use this probability to predict how many times the coin will land heads up in a different experiment.

For example, suppose we are going to toss the coin $250$ times. How many times do we expect it to land heads up?

To estimate the number of occurrences of this event, we multiply the new number of tosses $(250)$ by the experimental probability $(0.44)$ as follows:

$$
n = 250\cdot P(\text{head}) = 250\cdot 0.44 \approx 110
$$

So, if we toss the coin $250$ times, then based on the experimental probability, we estimate that the coin will land heads up about $110$ of those times.

<a id="calculating-an-experimental-probability-from-experimental-data"></a>
## Calculating an Experimental Probability From Experimental Data

**Example:** Elizabeth rolled a dice $45$ times, obtaining an odd number $36$ times. Based on this data, what is the experimental probability of getting an *even* number?

**Explanation**

Elizabeth rolled an even number a total of

$$
45-36 = 9 \ \text{times}
$$

To compute the experimental probability, we need to take the number of times that Elizabeth rolled an even number and divide it by the number of times she rolled the die:

$$
P(\text{even number}) = \dfrac{9}{45} = \dfrac{1}{5} = 0.2
$$

Therefore, the experimental probability of getting an even number is $0.2$.

## Using Experimental Probability to Estimate the Number of Occurrences of an Event Given a Table

**Example:** Martin surveyed $200$ students in New Hampton School to observe the distribution of students' eye colors. The table below shows the results of his survey.

| Eye color | Frequency |
| --- | ---: |
| Brown | $60$ |
| Blue | $36$ |
| Green | $28$ |
| Gray | $60$ |
| Hazel | $16$ |

In Brewster Academy, there are $350$ students. Assuming that the eye color distribution is the same for both schools, estimate the number of students with blue eyes in Brewster Academy.

**Explanation**

Out of the $200$ students that Martin surveyed, $36$ of those students had blue eyes. So, the experimental probability that a student has blue eyes is

$$
P(\text{student has blue eyes}) = \dfrac{36}{200} = 0.18
$$

To find an estimate for the number of students with blue eyes in Brewster Academy, we multiply the number of students in Brewster Academy $(350)$ by the experimental probability $(0.18)$. When we do this, we get

$$
n = 350\cdot P(\text{student has blue eyes}) = 350\cdot 0.18 = 63
$$

Therefore, we estimate that $63$ students in Brewster Academy will have blue eyes.

## Calculating Experimental Probability From a Dot Plot

**Example:** Gordon surveyed classes at Lincoln Elementary School to determine the number of students in each class. The diagram below shows the results of his survey. Each dot represents a single class.

![](<../Source/14.1.2. Experimental Probability/Images/e14efee5eaa65b9d50e8f286cf35c73d.png>)

There are $20$ classes at Washington Elementary School. Assuming that the distribution of class sizes is the same in both schools, find the number of classes with $28$ students at Washington Elementary School.

**Explanation**

The total number of classes Gordon surveyed is

$$
5+3+1+3 = 12
$$

Out of the $12$ classes that Gordon surveyed, $3$ of those classes have $28$ students. So, the experimental probability that a class has $28$ students is

$$
28
$$

Therefore, the estimated number of classes with $28$ students at Washington Elementary School is

$$
28
$$
