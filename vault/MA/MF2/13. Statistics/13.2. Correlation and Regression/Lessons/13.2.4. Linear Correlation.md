## Introduction

There is a **linear correlation** between two sets of paired observations if the data points on the corresponding scatter plot are located along a straight line.

We say that the correlation is **positive** if the corresponding trend line has a *positive slope*. Similarly, we say that the correlation is **negative** if the corresponding trend line has a *negative slope*.

We have a **strong** correlation if the data points lie *close* to the corresponding trend line. Similarly, we have a **weak** correlation if the data points are scattered *far* from the corresponding trend line.

<a id="the-correlation-coefficient"></a>
## The Correlation Coefficient

The linear **correlation coefficient** $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho, pronounced "row") is a real number between $-1$ and $1$ that shows the strength of a linear correlation. It has the following properties:

- $\rho = 1$ indicates a perfect positive correlation
- $\rho = -1$ indicates a perfect negative correlation
- $\rho = 0$ indicates no (linear) correlation

The approximate value of $\rho$ can be determined from the scatter plot.

| ![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/ac7d7556d81b6c18e7ff50ae16dcbab6.png>) | For a strong positive correlation, we have $0.7 \leq \rho \leq 1$. |
| --- | ---: |
| ![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/5d3b612df702296455d2b0a7976ea6a5.png>) | For a weak positive correlation, we have $0.3 \leq \rho \lt 0.7$. |
| ![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/4def0b97ab4316b2f4b91435c319b97c.png>) | For a strong negative correlation, we have $-1 \leq \rho \leq -0.7$. |
| ![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/74e83969f5a269c23d33e6aa554aef46.png>) | For a weak negative correlation, we have $-0.7 \lt \rho \leq -0.3$. |
| ![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/4bdc04420cff544e6922978b358aedd9.png>) | For little or no correlation, we have $-0.3 < \rho < 0.3$. |

## Estimating the Correlation Coefficient

**Example:** Which of the following could be the correlation coefficient for the data in the scatter plot above?

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/891aa60a153b96d951ffad7d42a8c748.png>)

1. $\rho =-0.8$
2. $\rho= 0.3$
3. $\rho = 0.9$

**Explanation**

Recall that the linear correlation coefficient $\rho$ satisfies the inequality

$$
-1 \leq \rho \leq 1
$$

where $\rho = 1$ indicates a perfect positive correlation, and $\rho = -1$ indicates a perfect negative correlation.

With that in mind, let's examine our scatter plot.

![](<../Source/13.2.4. Linear Correlation/Images/cc5927e28423d7a4cec19a53a24eb524.png>)

We note the following:

- The points in the scatter plot seem to follow a straight line with a *positive* slope. So, the correlation coefficient must be *positive*. Thus, we disregard the following value:
$\rho = -0.8$
- The points lie pretty close to the corresponding trend line. So, the correlation coefficient must be closer to $1$. Thus, we disregard the following value:
$\rho = 0.3$

Therefore, the best approximation of the correlation coefficient from the given options is $\rho= 0.9$.

## Identifying a Scatter Plot Given a Correlation Coefficient

**Example:** It is known that there is a linear correlation between the variables $x$ and $y$. Given that the correlation coefficient is $\rho\approx -0.85$, sketch a possible scatter plot that corresponds to this situation.

**Explanation**

Recall that the linear correlation coefficient $\rho$ satisfies the inequality

$$
-1 \leq \rho \leq 1
$$

where $\rho = 1$ indicates a perfect positive correlation, and $\rho = -1$ indicates a perfect negative correlation.

Therefore, since

$$
\rho \approx -0.85
$$

there is a *strong negative* linear correlation in the data.

- The *negative* linear correlation means that the data points should be scattered along a line with a *negative* slope.
- Since the correlation is *strong*, most of the points should lie *close* to the trend line.
