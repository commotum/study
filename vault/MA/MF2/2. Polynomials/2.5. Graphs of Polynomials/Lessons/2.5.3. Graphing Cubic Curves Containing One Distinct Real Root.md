## Introduction

The graph of any cubic function

$$
y = f(x)= ax^3 + bx^2 + cx +d
$$

where $a >0$, has a characteristic inverted ${\textsf{S}}$-shape, with $y$-values ranging from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. This means that every such function has at least one real root and at most three distinct roots.

For instance, suppose that we want to plot the graph of

$$
y = x^3-3x^2 +3x-1
$$

First, we need to find the roots, so we set $y=0$ and solve. Noting that the polynomial is a perfect cube, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{3} - 3x^{2} + 3x - 1 &= 0 \\
(x - 1)^{3} &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So we get one real root $x=1$, which has multiplicity $3$.

At a root of multiplicity $3$, the graph becomes **tangent** (i.e., lies parallel) to the $x$-axis and crosses the $x$-axis.

Therefore, the graph looks as follows:

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/9c0a169392c0e232fdea24674564fcbb.png>)

<a id="graphing-a-factored-cubic-curve-with-a-positive-leading-coefficient"></a>
## Graphing a Factored Cubic Curve with a Positive Leading Coefficient

**Example:** Sketch the curve $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{5}(x-4)(x^2 + 5)$.

**Explanation**

The equation is already fully factored. Note that the discriminant of

$$
x^2 + 5 = 0
$$

is

$$
\mathcal{D}= 0^2 - 4(1)(5) = -20 < 0
$$

which implies that the quadratic factor has no real roots. So by the zero-product rule, we deduce that the function $f(x)$ has a single root at $x=4$.

We also find the $y$-intercept of the curve by plugging the value $x=0$ into the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= \frac{1}{5}(0 - 4)(0^{2} + 5) \\
&= \frac{1}{5}(-4)(5) \\
&=-4
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the curve:

- has a $y$-intercept at the point $(0,-4)$,
- passes through the $x$-axis at the single root $x=4$, and
- has no more $x$-intercepts.

Because the leading coefficient is positive, the graph takes an inverted ${\textsf{S}}$-shape. Therefore, the graph looks as follows:

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/3567a9f10d8334c4ac37cd1451c1de94.png>)

<a id="graphing-an-expanded-cubic-curve-with-a-positive-leading-coefficient"></a>
## Graphing an Expanded Cubic Curve with a Positive Leading Coefficient

**Example:** Given that $(x-3)$ is a factor of $f(x) = x^3 + x^2 - 4x - 24$, sketch the curve $y = f(x)$.

**Explanation**

First, we need to factor the polynomial. We are told that $(x-3)$ is a factor of $f(x)$, so $(x-3)$ divides $f(x)$ with zero remainder. To begin factoring $f(x)$, we can divide it by $(x-3)$ using synthetic division:
$x^{3}\begin{vmatrix}x^{2} & x^{1} & x^{0} \\ 3 & 1 & 1\end{vmatrix}- 4\begin{vmatrix}-24 \\ 3\end{vmatrix}12\begin{vmatrix}24 \\ 1\end{vmatrix}4 \mid 8 \mid 0$

Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{f(x)}{x-3} = x^2 + 4x + 8
$$

which means that

$$
f(x) = (x-3)(x^2 + 4x + 8)
$$

The equation is now fully factored. Note that the discriminant of

$$
x^2 + 4x + 8 = 0
$$

is

$$
\mathcal{D}= 4^2 - 4(1)(8) = -16 < 0
$$

which implies that the quadratic factor has no real roots. So by the zero-product rule, we deduce that $f(x)$ has a single root at $x=3$.

We also find the $y$-intercept of the curve by substituting the value $x=0$ into the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= 0^{3} + 0^{2} - 4(0) - 24 =-24
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the curve:

- has a $y$-intercept at $(0,-24)$,
- passes through the $x$-axis at the single root $x=3$, and
- has no more $x$-intercepts.

Because the leading coefficient is positive, the graph takes an inverted ${\textsf{S}}$-shape. Therefore, the graph looks as follows (figure not to scale):

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/c2c780ca8947108a4000dda33521f78c.png>)

## Negative Cubic Curves With Only One Distinct Real Root

The graph of any cubic function

$$
y = f(x)= ax^3 + bx^2 + cx +d
$$

where $a <0$, has a characteristic ${\textsf{S}}$-shape, with $y$-values ranging from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$.

For instance, suppose that we want to plot the graph of

$$
y = -x^3+3x^2 -3x+1
$$

First, we need to find the roots, so we set $y=0$ and solve. Noting that the polynomial is a perfect cube, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
-x^{3} + 3x^{2} - 3x + 1 &= 0 \\
(-x + 1)^{3} &= 0 \\
- (x - 1)^{3} &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So we get one real root $x=1$, which has multiplicity $3$.

At a root of multiplicity $3$, the graph becomes tangent to the $x$-axis and crosses the $x$-axis. Therefore, the graph looks as follows:

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/6e426dff21f788ab0d2dfb09a4cc4d0f.png>)

## Graphing a Factored Cubic Curve with a Negative Leading Coefficient

**Example:** Sketch the curve $f(x) = -(x-2)(x^2 + 2x + 2)$.

**Explanation**

The equation is already fully factored. Note that the discriminant of

$$
x^2 + 2x + 2 = 0
$$

is

$$
\mathcal{D}= 2^2 - 4(1)(2) = -4 < 0
$$

which implies that the quadratic factor has no real roots. So by the zero-product rule, we deduce that the function $f(x)$ has a single root at $x=2$.

We also find the $y$-intercept of the curve by plugging the value $x=0$ into the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= - (0 - 2)(0^{2} + 2(0) + 2) \\
&= - (-2)(2) \\
&= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the curve:

- has a $y$-intercept at the point $(0,4)$,
- passes through the $x$-axis at the single root $x=2$, and
- has no more $x$-intercepts.

Because the leading coefficient is negative, the graph has an ${\textsf{S}}$-shape. Therefore, the graph looks as follows (figure not to scale):

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/37d14cbdbabdfa7e9f3e16efa60d5593.png>)

## Graphing an Expanded Cubic Curve with a Negative Leading Coefficient

**Example:** Given that $(x-4)$ is a factor of $f(x) = -x^3 + 4x^2 - 4x + 16$, sketch the curve $y=f(x)$.

**Explanation**

First, we need to factor the polynomial. We are told that $(x-4)$ is a factor of $f(x)$, so $(x-4)$ divides $f(x)$ with zero remainder. To begin factoring $f(x)$, we can divide it by $(x-4)$ using synthetic division:

$$
x^{3}\begin{vmatrix}x^{2} & x^{1} & x^{0} \\ 4 & -1 & 4\end{vmatrix}- 4\begin{vmatrix}16 \\ -4\end{vmatrix}0\begin{vmatrix}-16 \\ -1\end{vmatrix}0 \mid - 4 \mid 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(f(x))/(x - 4) &= -x^{2} - 4 \\
&= - (x^{2} + 4)
\end{aligned}
$$

which means that

$$
f(x) = -(x-4)(x^2 + 4)
$$

The equation is now fully factored. Note that the discriminant of

$$
x^2 + 4 = 0
$$

is

$$
\mathcal{D} = 0^2 - 4(1)(4) =-16 < 0
$$

which implies that the quadratic factor has no real roots. So by the zero-product rule, we deduce that $f(x)$ has a single root at $x=4$.

We also find the $y$-intercept of the curve by substituting the value $x=0$ into the equation:

$$
y = -(0)^3 + 4(0)^2 - 4(0) + 16 = 16
$$

So, the curve:

- has a $y$-intercept at $(0,16)$,
- passes through the $x$-axis at the single root $x=4$, and
- has no more $x$-intercepts.

Because the leading coefficient is negative, the graph has an ${\textsf{S}}$-shape. Therefore, the graph looks as follows (figure not to scale):

![](<../Source/2.5.3. Graphing Cubic Curves Containing One Distinct Real Root/Images/00b69052f7ee6e6ed3ebfec97e63f155.png>)
