## Introduction

A polygon has **reflective symmetry** if the resulting figure lies perfectly on top of the original figure when reflected across some line.

For example, let's consider the rectangle below.

![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/0b43a893707cd810fc5b8539942dccde.png>)

This rectangle has reflective symmetry because it appears unchanged after the reflection over the line $l$, as shown below.

## Identifying Lines That Reflect a Polygon to Itself

**Example:** Reflections across which lines carry the polygon below onto itself?

![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/87e73ebee9ad90e2c502bf496850c74d.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's consider each of the lines in turn.

- By reflecting the polygon across line I, we do *not* map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/52c293ea3ae4f43fb84fb9f44c0b0f69.png>)

- By reflecting the polygon across line II, we map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/003f8e4dcca0f948fc2e8684f51e5943.png>)

- By reflecting the polygon across line III, we do *not* map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/4598bfcce15749dfda44a6334d9589ef.png>)

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

## Determining Reflections That Map a Polygon to Itself in a Coordinate Plane

**Example:** Reflections across which of the following lines carry the polygon below onto itself?

1. the line $l$
2. the line $y=-x$
3. the $y$-axis

![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/7fd0095f480825396493ab430485e83c.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's consider each of the lines in turn.

- By reflecting the polygon across $l$, we do *not* map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/3a34edcee597e33abf98e6521ab4f4c5.png>)

- By reflecting the polygon across $y=-x$, we map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/48e2d7319c1216b2fbf853e748cf08ab.png>)

- By reflecting the polygon across $y$-axis, we map the polygon onto itself.![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/92b5ba9c7b6d90f91c10f62a7a446787.png>)

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."

## Axes of Symmetry

If a polygon remains the same when reflected across a line, then we call that line an **axis of symmetry**.

For example, the equilateral triangle below has the line $l$ as an axis of symmetry.

![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/0af8ece4e14b4c0e242cd51299685e53.png>)

Some polygons have multiple axes of symmetry. For example, the equilateral triangle has other axes of symmetry, $r$ and $s$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/8.2.14. Reflective Symmetry/Images/fbe4106991a4654c346eea15a549933e.png>)

These are all the axes of symmetries for the equilateral triangle. So, this triangle has $3$ axes of symmetry.
