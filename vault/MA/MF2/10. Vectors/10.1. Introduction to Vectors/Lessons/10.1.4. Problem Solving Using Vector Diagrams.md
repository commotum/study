## Introduction

It's often helpful to express a given vector in terms of sums and scalar multiples of a handful of known vectors.

As an example, let's consider the following diagram:

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/f11ae31b3ffa2959c07a3dc34173835a.png>)

How can we express the vector $\overrightarrow{PR}$ in terms of the vectors $\textbf{a}$ and $\textbf{b}$ only?

According to the triangle law of addition,

$$
\begin{aligned}
PR^(→) &= PQ^(→) + QR^(→)
\end{aligned}
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/4487bf6b6483aa17832f50d4ddbbc114.png>)

From the original diagram, we have that

$$
{\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{PQ}}} = \mathbf{a} \qquad\textrm{and}\qquad {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{QR}}} = -\mathbf{b}
$$

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/955fff9b361f7cb97663b52983855d75.png>)

Therefore, by substituting these values into our equation for ${\color{red}\overrightarrow{PR}}$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
PR^(→) &= PQ^(→) + QR^(→) \\
&= a + (-b) \\
&= a - b
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's see another example.

## Expressing a Vector as a Sum or Difference of Two Known Vectors

**Example:** Consider the diagram above, where $\overrightarrow{PS}=\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{QS}=\mathbf{b}$, $\overrightarrow{PT}=\mathbf{c}$, and $\overrightarrow{TS}=\mathbf{d}$. Find $\overrightarrow{TQ}$ in terms of $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$, and $\mathbf{d}$.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/e1428d48e9b657cb65b4abaf82ef8e51.png>)

**Explanation**

We want to find a "path" from $T$ to $Q$ consisting only of the vectors $\mathbf a, \mathbf b, \mathbf c$, and $\mathbf d$.

According to the triangle law of addition,

$$
{\color{red}{\overrightarrow{TQ}}} = {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{TS}}} + {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{SQ}}}
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/d79b0f30be6e0809efe831e4862b1043.png>)

Now, from the original diagram, we have that

$$
{\color{blue}\overrightarrow{TS}} = \mathbf{d}, \qquad {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{SQ}}} = -\mathbf{b}
$$

Let's add these to our diagram.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/20b51829636015f084208e464d30bea9.png>)

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
TQ^(→) &= TS^(→) + SQ^(→) \\
&= d + (-b) \\
&= d - b
\end{aligned}
$$

## Expressing a Vector as a Sum or Difference of More Than Two Known Vectors

**Example:** Consider the diagram shown below, where $\overrightarrow{PQ}=\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{PT}=\mathbf{b}$, and $\overrightarrow{QS}=\mathbf{c}$. Find $\overrightarrow{TS}$ in terms of $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/ee9aee4946e062b8a7ad1fae2e350d37.png>)

**Explanation**

We want to find a "path" from $T$ to $S$ consisting only of the vectors $\mathbf a, \mathbf b$, or $\mathbf c$.

According to the triangle law of addition,

$$
{\color{red}{\overrightarrow{TS}}} = {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{TP}}} + {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{PQ}}} + {\color{blue}{\overrightarrow{QS}}}
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/70335ddb9f1d79ca14b9138a4291465d.png>)

Now, from the original diagram, we have that

$$
{\color{blue}\overrightarrow{TP}} = -\mathbf{b}, \qquad {\color{blue}\overrightarrow{PQ}} = \mathbf{a}, \qquad {\color{blue}\overrightarrow{QS}} = \mathbf{c}
$$

Let's add these to our diagram.

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/f5c06f75325a6ca44c7da008f6706fb2.png>)

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
TS^(→) &= TP^(→) + PQ^(→) + QS^(→) \\
&=-b + a + c \\
&= a - b + c
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying Vector Equalities

**Example:** In parallelogram $ABCD$ shown above, which of the following equalities are true?

![](<../Source/10.1.4. Problem Solving Using Vector Diagrams/Images/38d6af513afb9edf4208166303e35ed3.png>)

1. $\overrightarrow{CB}+\overrightarrow{BA}=\overrightarrow{CA}$
2. $\overrightarrow{CB}-\overrightarrow{AB}=\overrightarrow{AC}$
3. $\overrightarrow{DC}=\overrightarrow{BA}$

**Explanation**

- Equality I is true. Indeed, $\overrightarrow{CB}+\overrightarrow{BA}=\overrightarrow{CA}$.
- Equality II is false. Indeed,
$CB^(→) - AB^(→)|= CB^(→) + BA^(→); = CA^(→); \ne AC^(→)$
since $\overrightarrow{CA}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ have opposite directions.
- Equality III is false. Indeed, $\overrightarrow{DC}\neq\overrightarrow{BA}$ since they have opposite directions.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only."
