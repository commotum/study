## Introduction

Consider the line

$$
y = x + 2
$$

and the parabola

$$
y = x^2 + x + 1
$$

shown in the graph below.

![](<../Source/1.2.12. Finding Intersections of Lines and Quadratic Functions/Images/9b8a73e7bad00d015ed142e48f2a78da.png>)

We can see from the diagram that there are two points of intersection. So let's calculate the coordinates of these intersection points.

An intersection point is a common solution of the equations

$$
y = x + 2
$$

and

$$
y = x^2 + x + 1
$$

In other words, to get the coordinates of the intersection points, we have to solve the following system of equations:

$$
{y = x^{2} + x + 1; y = x + 2
$$

Setting the equations equal to one another and then solving for $x$ using our knowledge of quadratic equations, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + x + 1 &= x + 2 \\
x^{2} + x + 1 &= x + 2 \\
x^{2} + 1 &= 2 \\
x^{2} - 1 &= 0 \\
(x + 1)(x - 1) &= 0 \\
x &= -1, 1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points are $x=-1$ and $x=1$.

To find the $y$-coordinates of the intersection points, we plug these values into either curve. Let's choose the line since it's simpler.

- Substituting $x=-1$, we get
$y|= x + 2; = (-1) + 2; = 1$,
so one point of intersection is $(-1,1)$.
- Substituting $x=1$, we get
$y|= x + 2; = (1) + 2; = 3$,
so the other point of intersection is $(1,3)$.

Therefore, we conclude that the points of intersection are

$$
\left(-1,\,1 \right)
$$

and

$$
\left(1,\,3 \right)
$$

## Finding the X-Coordinates of the Intersection Points of a Parabola and a Line

**Example:** The parabola $y = x^2 + 2$ and the line $y = 3x$ intersect at the points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, where $x_1 < x_2$. What is the value of $x_1 + x_2$?

**Explanation**

We need to solve the following system consisting of the equations of the curves:

$$
{y = x^{2} + 2; y = 3x
$$

Setting the equations equal to one another and solving, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + 2 &= 3x \\
x^{2} - 3x + 2 &= 0 \\
(x - 1)(x - 2) &= 0 \\
x &= 1, 2
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points are $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 2$. Therefore,

$$
x_1 + x_2 = 1 + 2 = 3
$$

## Finding the Y-Coordinates of the Intersection Points of a Parabola and a Line

**Example:** The parabola $y = -x^2 + x + 4$ and the line $y = 2x + 2$ intersect at the points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, where $x_1 < x_2$. What is the value of $y_1 + y_2$?

**Explanation**

We need to solve the following system consisting of the equations of the curves:

$$
{y =-x^{2} + x + 4; y = 2x + 2
$$

Setting the equations equal to one another and solving, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
-x^{2} + x + 4 &= 2x + 2 \\
- x^{2} - x + 2 &= 0 \\
- (x^{2} + x - 2) &= 0 \\
- (x + 2)(x - 1) &= 0 \\
x &= -2, 1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points are $x_1 = -2$ and $x_2 = 1$.

To find the $y$-coordinates of the intersection points, we plug these values into either the curve or the line. Let's choose the line since it's simpler.

- Substituting $x=-2$, we get
$y|= 2x + 2; = 2(-2) + 2; =-2$,
so one point of intersection is $(-2,-2)$.
- Substituting $x=1$, we get
$y|= 2x + 2; = 2(1) + 2; = 4$,
so the other point of intersection is $(1,4)$.

The points of intersection are $(-2,-2)$ and $(1,4)$. Therefore,

$$
y_1 = -2,\; y_2 = 4
$$

and

$$
y_1 + y_2 = -2 + 4 = 2
$$

## The Number of Intersection Points

![](<../Source/1.2.12. Finding Intersections of Lines and Quadratic Functions/Images/62995b84b93fb7f3985af35efe5b2630.png>)

A line and a parabola may have **two**,
**one**, or **no** points of intersection.

This is similar to how a quadratic equation may have **two**,
**one**, or **no** solutions.

## Finding the Intersection Point of a Parabola and a Line

**Example:** The parabola $y=-x^2+6x$ and the line $y=9$ intersect at a single point with coordinates $(p,q)$. What is $p+q$?

**Explanation**

We need to solve the following system consisting of the equations of the curves:

$$
{y =-x^{2} + 6x; y = 9
$$

Setting the equations equal to one another and solving, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
-x^{2} + 6x &= 9 \\
- x^{2} + 6x - 9 &= 0 \\
- (x^{2} - 6x + 9) &= 0 \\
- (x - 3)(x - 3) &= 0 \\
x &= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the $x$-coordinate of the intersection point is $x=3$.

We know the $y$-coordinate of the intersection point from the equation of the line. So, the only point of intersection is $(3, 9)$. Therefore, $p=3,\; q=9$, and

$$
p+q = 3+9 = 12
$$

## Finding the Range of an Unknown Parameter Given That There Are No Intersection Points

**Example:** Given that the line $y=x +2k$ does **not** intersect the parabola $y=x^2-x+3$, what is the range of possible values of $k$?

**Explanation**

To determine whether the line

$$
y=x +2k
$$

intersects the parabola

$$
y=x^2-x+3
$$

we need to solve the following system of equations for the two curves:

$$
{y = x^{2} - x + 3; y = x + 2k
$$

Setting the equations equal to one another and writing in standard form, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} - x + 3 &= x + 2k \\
x^{2} - 2x + 3 - 2k &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we solve this equation using the quadratic formula with

$$
a=1,\, b=-2
$$

and $c=3-2k{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= (- (-2) ± \sqrt{(-2)^{2} - 4(1)(3 - 2k)})/(2(1)) \\
&= \frac{2 ± \sqrt{4 - 12 + 8k}}{2} \\
&= \frac{2 ± \sqrt{8k - 8}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the line and parabola do not intersect, our quadratic equation must have no real solutions; hence, the expression inside the square root must be negative.
Setting up and solving this inequality, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8k - 8 & < 0 \\ 8k & < 8 \\ k & < 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the range of possible values of $k$ is $k \lt 1$.
