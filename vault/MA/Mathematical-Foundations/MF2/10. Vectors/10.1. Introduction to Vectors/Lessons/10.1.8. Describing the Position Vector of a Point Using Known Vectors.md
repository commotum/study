## Introduction

Given a fixed **origin** in the plane (often denoted $O$), and another point $X$, the vector $\overrightarrow{OX}$ is called the **position vector** of the point $X$. The vector $\mathbf{x}$ defines the position of $X$ on the plane in relation to $O$.

For example, suppose we have two vectors

$$
\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf{a}
$$

and

$$
\overrightarrow{OB}=\mathbf{b}
$$

and a parallelogram $OAXB_1$, where

$$
{OB_1}=2{OB}
$$

How can we find the vector $\mathbf{x}$, the diagonal of the parallelogram, expressed in terms of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$?

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/353e534f2f011d6ef99504860421ba2e.png>)

From the $\triangle OB_1X$ in the above figure, we can see that

$$
\mathbf{x} = \overrightarrow{OB}_1+\overrightarrow{B_1X}
$$

We know that

$$
\overrightarrow{OB}_1=2\overrightarrow{OB}=2\mathbf{b}
$$

Also, since $OAXB_1$ is a parallelogram, we have $OA \parallel B_1X$ and

$$
\overrightarrow{B_1X}=\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf{a}
$$

Substituting these values into the original equality, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= OB^(→)_{1} + B_{1}X^(→) \\
&= 2b + a \\
&= a + 2b
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding a Scalar Factor in a Linear Combination

**Example:** Consider the picture below. Given that $\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}$, find $\lambda$.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/c2827bf97f6d17572e9ac2b808d16a69.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/0b673cb49bd6ea0955e57952763b7458.png>)

In $\bigtriangleup OAC$, we have:

- $AC=2 \mid \mathbf{b} \mid$
- $\overrightarrow{AC}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ have the same direction

Hence,

$$
\overrightarrow{AC}=2\mathbf{b}
$$

Additionally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= OA^(→) + AC^(→) \\
&= a + 2b
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{b}
$$

Since $\mathbf{a} \nparallel \mathbf{b}$, the linear combination can only be written in one way. Equating the coefficients of $\mathbf{b}$, we get:
$\lambda=2$

<a id="finding-two-scalar-factors-in-a-linear-combination"></a>
## Finding Two Scalar Factors in a Linear Combination

**Example:** Consider the picture below. Given that $\mathbf{m}=\lambda\mathbf{n}+\mu\mathbf{k}$, find $\lambda$ and $\mu$.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/bfa592bb1f4e5a292e1e94476ab6e5f1.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/49fbbe1f5dd4731054af33666b88cb64.png>)

In $\bigtriangleup ONM$ shown above, we have:

- $ON=2 \mid \mathbf{n} \mid$;
- $\overrightarrow{ON}$ and $\mathbf{n}$ have the same direction

Hence,

$$
\overrightarrow{ON}=2\mathbf{n}
$$

.

Similarly:

- $NM=2 \mid \mathbf{k} \mid$
- $\overrightarrow{NM}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ have the same direction

Hence,

$$
\overrightarrow{NM}=2\mathbf{k}
$$

.

Additionally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &= ON^(→) + NM^(→) \\
&= 2n + 2k
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\mathbf{m}=\lambda\mathbf{n}+\mu\mathbf{k}=2\mathbf{n}+2\mathbf{k}
$$

.

Since $\mathbf{n} \nparallel \mathbf{k}$, the linear combination can only be written in one way. Equating the coefficients of $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{k}$, we get:

- $\lambda=2$ and $\mu=2$

## Expressing a Vector That Satisfies a Ratio in Terms of Known Vectors

**Example:** Consider the following diagram, where $\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{OB}=\mathbf{b}$, and $X$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $3:1$. Find $\overrightarrow{OX}$.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Describing the Position Vector of a Point Using Known Vectors/Images/8512f8ab1660315b7e5f0aa8beb302ff.png>)

**Explanation**

From the diagram, we see that

$$
\overrightarrow{OX} = \overrightarrow{OA}+\overrightarrow{AX}
$$

We're told that

$$
\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}
$$

so we just need to work out $\overrightarrow{AX}$.

First, from $\triangle OAB$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB^(→) &= AO^(→) + OB^(→) \\
&=-OA^(→) + OB^(→) \\
&=-a + b \\
&= b - a
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $AX:XB=3:1$, we have

$$
\overrightarrow{AX}=\dfrac{3}{4}\overrightarrow{AB}=\dfrac{3}{4}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
OX^(→) &= OA^(→) + AX^(→) \\
&= a + \frac{3}{4}(b - a) \\
&= \frac{1}{4}a + \frac{3}{4}b
\end{aligned}
$$
