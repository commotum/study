## Introduction

Suppose we have a vector

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=\textbf{a}
$$

We denote its **magnitude**, also known as its *modulus* or *length*, as $\mid \overrightarrow{AB} \mid$ or $\mid \textbf{a} \mid$.

For example, the vector below has a magnitude of $8$, denoted

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \overrightarrow{AB} &= 8
\end{aligned}
$$

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/01bb2c548e7af38799d5e510083415be.png>)

Now, let's consider the following situation. Suppose that the vector $\textbf{a}$ is directed vertically upwards and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \textbf{a} &= 6
\end{aligned}
$$

while $\textbf{b}$ is directed horizontally to the right and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \textbf{b} &= 8
\end{aligned}
$$

How can we find $\mid \textbf{a}+\textbf{b} \mid$?

First, we use the triangle law of addition to find the resultant $\textbf{a}+\textbf{b}$:

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/7b0cf516ba3d37310d266bddeb6d9757.png>)

Notice that we obtain a right triangle. So, we can use the Pythagorean theorem as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a + b & ^{2} &= & a & ^{2} + & b\end{vmatrix}^{2} \\
&= 6^{2} + 8^{2} \\
&= 100
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \textbf{a}+\textbf{b} &= \sqrt{100}=10
\end{aligned}
$$

**Important**: In the above calculation we wrote

$$
\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b} & ^2 = & \mathbf{a} & ^2+ & \mathbf{b}\end{vmatrix}^2
$$

. This works only when the vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are at right angles, and the relationship is not true in general. We'll see how to deal with non-right-angled vectors soon.

## Calculating the Magnitude of a Sum of Vectors Using the Pythagorean Theorem

**Example:** The vector $\mathbf{a}$ is directed due north-east and $\mid \mathbf{a}|=11$. The vector $\mathbf{b}$ is directed due south-east and $\mid \mathbf{b}|=8$. Find the exact value of $\mid \mathbf{a}+\textbf{b} \mid$.

**Explanation**

We use the triangle law of addition to find the sum $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$:

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/2ef890dcf068631ee7b6b362e6fc31fb.png>)

Notice that we obtain a right triangle. So, we can use Pythagorean theorem as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a + b & ^{2} &= & a & ^{2} + & b\end{vmatrix}^{2} \\
&= 11^{2} + 8^{2} \\
&= 185
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{a}+\textbf{b} &= \sqrt{185}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying the Magnitude of a Vector From a Diagram

**Example:** Consider the picture shown below. Find the exact value of $\mid \mathbf{a} \mid$.

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/a274cb522e2b52a80b5a0d047308873b.png>)

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/25d18d6e9b7ac756a4f39330b63c359a.png>)

In $\bigtriangleup AHB$ we have

$$
\angle AHB = 90^\circ
$$

,

$$
AH=35\:\textrm{mm}
$$

and

$$
BH=25\:\textrm{mm}
$$

So, the Pythagorean theorem gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB &= \sqrt{AH^{2} + BH^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{35^{2} + 25^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1850} \\
&= 5\sqrt{74}mm
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-magnitude-of-a-vector-using-the-law-of-cosines"></a>
## Calculating the Magnitude of a Vector Using the Law of Cosines

When the triangle law for addition does *not* give a right triangle, we *cannot* use the Pythagorean theorem to compute the magnitude of the sum.

For example, using the triangle law to draw the sum $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$ for the vectors below, we do not get a right triangle. Instead, we get a triangle with an obtuse angle $\theta$.

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/2c45d99520f09fb1356b8998b226fccb.png>)

We do not obtain a right triangle here, so we cannot use the Pythagorean theorem. However, we can use the law of cosines:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a + b & ^{2} &= & a & ^{2} + & b & ^{2} - 2 & a\end{vmatrix} \mid b \mid \cos θ
\end{aligned}
$$

In general, given any two vectors that form a triangle using the triangle addition law, we can always use the law of cosines to compute the magnitude of the sum.

<a id="a-worked-example"></a>
## A Worked Example

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/b9298657a70c2a1dd525070fc93c49f9.png>)

Consider the diagram above, where

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{a} &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{b} &= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

How can we find $\mid \mathbf{a+b} \mid$?

First, we use the triangle law of addition to find $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$:

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/9d70d2c97d838b648ee231f5e50d1a8a.png>)

We do not obtain a right triangle here, so we cannot use the Pythagorean theorem. But we can use the law of cosines, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a + b & ^{2} &= & a & ^{2} + & b & ^{2} - 2 & a\end{vmatrix} \mid b \mid \cos (120^{∘}) \\
&= 4^{2} + 5^{2} - 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot (-\frac{1}{2}) \\
&= 16 + 25 + 20 \\
&= 61
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{a+b} &= \sqrt{61}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-magnitude-of-a-sum-of-vectors-using-the-law-of-cosines"></a>
## Calculating the Magnitude of a Sum of Vectors Using the Law of Cosines

**Example:** Consider the diagram shown above. If $\mid \mathbf{a}|=4$ and $\mid \mathbf{b}|=3$, Find the exact value of$\mid \mathbf{a-b} \mid$.

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/a3b3542aea04e6067d60f10d85044a15.png>)

**Explanation**

We can interpret the subtraction
$\mid \mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} \mid$
as the addition
$\mid \mathbf{a} + (-\mathbf{b}) \mid$.

The vector $-\mathbf{b}$ has the same magnitude as $\mathbf{b}$ and the direction that is opposite to $\mathbf{b}$. We use the triangle law of addition to find $\mathbf{a}$ and $-\mathbf{b}$. The resultant goes from the start of $\mathbf{a}$ to the head of $-\mathbf{b}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.1.3. The Magnitude of a Vector/Images/bb1f699a74c6c22cbfe448efd7d12922.png>)

So, we can use the law of cosines as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a - b & ^{2} &= & a + (-b) & ^{2} \\ = & a & ^{2} + & -b\end{vmatrix}^{2} - 2 \cdot\begin{vmatrix}a & \cdot & -b & \cdot \cos (180^{∘} - 120^{∘}) \\ = & a & ^{2} + & b\end{vmatrix}^{2} - 2 \mid a \mid \mid b \mid \cos (60^{∘}) \\
&= 3^{2} + 4^{2} - 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \\
&= 9 + 16 - 12 \\
&= 13
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid \mathbf{a-b} &= \sqrt{13}
\end{aligned}
$$
