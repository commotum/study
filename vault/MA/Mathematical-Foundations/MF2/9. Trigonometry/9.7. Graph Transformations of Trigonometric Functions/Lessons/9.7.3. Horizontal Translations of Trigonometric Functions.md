## Introduction

Let's consider the graph of $y=\sin x$, graphed below.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/3ebf2e82beec5a617c33f9d8c0ced560.png>)

We can move the graph right (or left) by subtracting (or adding) a number from $x$ inside the sine function. These are **horizontal translations**.

To demonstrate, let's plot the graph of

$$
y=\sin \left(x-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$

To do this, we shift the sine function

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

units to the *right*.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/4eaef0ed73d8420efa0fbbd5f468c5c3.png>)

This might seem counterintuitive: *subtracting* a number from $x$ inside the sine function shifts the graph to the *right*, in the *positive* direction of the $x$-axis. But this is in fact true.

To demonstrate, consider the point $x=\pi$. By substituting it into our function, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= \sin (x - \frac{π}{2}) \\
&= \sin (π - \frac{π}{2}) \\
&= \sin (\frac{π}{2}) \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the point

$$
\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2},1\right)
$$

on the graph is now

$$
\left(\pi,1\right)
$$

and we obtain a shift to the right.

Similarly, we plot the graph of

$$
y=\sin\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)
$$

by shifting the sine function

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{3}
$$

units to the *left*.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/ee21d1733ae0ec5a645d055007d35f3e.png>)

When a sine wave is shifted horizontally from its usual position, we say that the function has undergone a **phase shift.**

## Identifying the Equation of a Sine Function With a Phase Shift

**Example:** What is the equation of the transformed sine curve shown below?

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/9221528d233e13a54236c8d18893843d.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's add the graph of $y=\sin x$ to the plot.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/6be0c3537ba52371cd5315a656d0ac55.png>)

To plot the given curve, we follow these steps:

- Start with the graph of $y=\sin x$.
- Then, shift it by $1$ unit **right.**

Therefore, the given graph is

$$
y = \sin\left(x-1\right)
$$

## Horizontally Translating the Cosine Function

Let's see how to horizontally shift the cosine function $y=\cos x$, shown below.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/8f6fa76fbe024ae140f6024d01ba81e2.png>)

We obtain the graph of

$$
y=\cos\left(x-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)
$$

by shifting the cosine function

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

units to the *right*.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/5960573cedd3ac357ffc22eefd531ffb.png>)

And we obtain the graph of

$$
y=\cos\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)
$$

by shifting the cosine function

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{3}
$$

units to the *left*.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/96b106ff25c28058ea4975c2025a25ab.png>)

Again, when a cosine curve has been shifted horizontally, we say that it has undergone a phase shift.

<a id="horizontally-translating-the-tangent-function"></a>
## Horizontally Translating the Tangent Function

Finally, let's consider the tangent function $y=\tan x$, shown below.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/d7ad1cf996f288ba7335dac9eb9e4d32.png>)

To obtain the graph of

$$
y=\tan \left(x - \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)
$$

we translate the tangent function by

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

units to the right, as follows:

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/abf51bf53ed88e2fc187aaee64823468.png>)

Note that the asymptotes of

$$
y=\tan\left(x-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \right)
$$

are also shifted to the right by

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

For example, the first asymptote to the right of the $y$-axis is now

$$
x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}+ \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \dfrac{3\pi}{4}
$$

Similarly, to obtain the graph of

$$
y=\tan \left(x+1\right)
$$

we translate the tangent function by $1$ unit to the left, as follows:

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/3065aef745350f81365002fc05fb8eea.png>)

Note that the asymptotes of

$$
y=\tan\left(x+1 \right)
$$

are also shifted to the left by $1$. For example, the first asymptote to the right of the $y$-axis is now

$$
x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}-1
$$

## Identifying the Equation of a Tangent Function With a Horizontal Translation

**Example:** The graph below shows the function $y=\tan\left(x+C\right)$ for $-\dfrac{\pi}{2} < C < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. What is the value of $C$?

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/bc54da2c9a90d49807a2fc61fe5938c0.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's add the graph of $y=\tan x$ to the plot.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/46a63a07bc551e59b6062f90fd01a09d.png>)

To plot the given curve, we follow these steps:

- Start with the graph of $y=\tan x$.
- Then, shift it by $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ units **right**.

Therefore, the given graph is

$$
y = \tan\left(x-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)
$$

and hence the value of

$$
C=-\dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

.

<a id="finding-a-vertical-asymptote-for-a-horizontally-translated-tangent-function"></a>
## Finding a Vertical Asymptote for a Horizontally Translated Tangent Function

**Example:** The graph below shows one branch of the curve $y=\tan(x+C)$ for some number $C$, where $-\dfrac\pi 2 < C < \dfrac \pi 2$, and a vertical asymptote of the curve. What is the equation of the vertical asymptote?

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/be7bd3276dc8a4b0640619dd6d67150e.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's add the graph of $y=\tan x$ to the plot.

![](<../Source/9.7.3. Horizontal Translations of Trigonometric Functions/Images/ee98ef0113a80965b7e45b717a595d11.png>)

To plot the given curve, we follow these steps:

- Start with the graph of $y=\tan x$.
- Then, shift it by $\dfrac 12$ units **left**.

Therefore, the given graph is

$$
y = \tan\left(x+\dfrac 1 2\right)
$$

which means

$$
C=\dfrac 1 2
$$

Since the graph is shifted by

$$
\dfrac 12
$$

units left, the asymptote is also shifted by

$$
\dfrac 12
$$

units left.

The corresponding asymptote on the graph of $y=\tan x$ is

$$
x=-\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

Therefore, the equation of the asymptote shown is

$$
x = -\dfrac{\pi}{2} - \dfrac 12 = -\dfrac{\pi+1}{2}
$$
