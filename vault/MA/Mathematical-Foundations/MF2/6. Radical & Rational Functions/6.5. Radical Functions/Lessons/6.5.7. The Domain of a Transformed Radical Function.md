## Introduction

In general, the domain of a radical function

$$
f(x)=\sqrt[n]{x}
$$

depends on whether $n$ (called the **index**) is even or odd.

If $n$ is *odd*, then the domain of $f(x)$ is *always* $x\in (-\infty, \infty)$. For example, the domain of

$$
f(x)= \sqrt[3]{x}
$$

is $x\in (-\infty, \infty)$.

We can see this in the graph below: the function

$$
f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}
$$

extends across all values of $x$.

![](<../Source/6.5.7. The Domain of a Transformed Radical Function/Images/4549d3c5ac194ce235d2053fc943a50f.png>)

Now, suppose that we want to find the domain of a transformed radical function, such as

$$
g(x)= -\sqrt[3]{-x+2}
$$

The domain of $\sqrt[3]{x}$ is $x\in (-\infty, \infty)$, and graph transformations will not change this interval. Therefore, we conclude that the domain of $g(x)$ is also $x \in (-\infty,\infty)$.

<a id="finding-the-domain-of-a-transformed-radical-function-with-an-odd-index"></a>
## Finding the Domain of a Transformed Radical Function With an Odd Index

**Example:** Find the domain of $f(x) = -4\sqrt[9]{-x-5} +10$.

**Explanation**

The domain of

$$
y = \sqrt[9]{x}
$$

is
$x \in (-\infty,\infty)$.

Transformations do not change this interval. Therefore, the domain of $f(x)$ is $x \in (-\infty,\infty)$.

## The Domain of a Radical Function With an Even Index

Let's go back to considering

$$
f(x)=\sqrt[n]{x}
$$

If $n$ is *even*, then the domain of $f(x)$ is *always* $[MATH: x\in [0, \infty).]$ For example, the domain of

$$
f(x)= \sqrt[4]{x}\,
$$

is $[MATH: x\in [0, \infty).]$

We can see this in the graph below: the function

$$
f(x) = \sqrt[4]{x}
$$

starts at $x=0$ and extends forever to the right.

![](<../Source/6.5.7. The Domain of a Transformed Radical Function/Images/6630892e7f6ce94151a031ef58628cae.png>)

Notice how similar this looks to the square root function! All curves of the form

$$
y=\sqrt[n]{x}
$$

for even $n$ have this shape.

Now let's consider the function

$$
g(x)= 3\sqrt[4]{x+2} -2
$$

To find the domain of

$$
g(x) = 3\sqrt[4]{x+2} -2
$$

we proceed in exactly the same way as with square roots: we set the expression under the radical sign to be greater than or equal to zero. This gives

$$
x+2 \ge 0
$$

Solving this inequality, we get

$$
x \mid \ge - 2
$$

Therefore, the domain of $y = g(x)$ is $[MATH: x \in [-2, \infty).]$

We can also see that this is true from the graph of the function. The graph of $y=g(x)$ is shown below.

![](<../Source/6.5.7. The Domain of a Transformed Radical Function/Images/e712e5f0c118cb60c3903a7f680d4222.png>)

In general, to find the domain of a radical function of the form

$$
y = \sqrt[n]{f(x)}
$$

where $n$ is an even integer, we need to solve the inequality

$$
f(x) \geq 0
$$

<a id="finding-the-domain-of-a-transformed-radical-function-with-an-even-index"></a>
## Finding the Domain of a Transformed Radical Function With an Even Index

**Example:** Find the domain of $g(x) = -2\sqrt[6]{x+1}-2$.

**Explanation**

The domain of

$$
y = \sqrt[6]{x}
$$

is

$$
x \ge 0
$$

To find the domain of

$$
g(x) = -2\sqrt[6]{x+1}-2
$$

we replace $x$ with $x+1$ in the above inequality. This gives

$$
{x+1} \ge 0
$$

Solving this inequality, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x + 1 & \ge 0 \\ x & \ge - 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the domain of $y = g(x)$ is $[MATH: x \in [-1, \infty).]$

We can also see that this is true from the graph of the function. The graph of $y=g(x)$ is shown below.

![](<../Source/6.5.7. The Domain of a Transformed Radical Function/Images/c911a2e0961c241b6d4ae53f0caaec79.png>)
