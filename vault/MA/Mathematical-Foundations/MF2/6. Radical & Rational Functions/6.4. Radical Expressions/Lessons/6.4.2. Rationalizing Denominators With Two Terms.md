## Introduction

When simplifying numerical expressions, it is generally preferable to avoid having radicals in the denominator. In cases where we wish to remove a radical from a denominator, we must **rationalize the denominator**.

If the denominator of an expression consists of two terms and includes one or more radicals, we rationalize it by multiplying both the numerator and the denominator by the **conjugate** of the denominator.

To illustrate, consider the following expression:

$$
\dfrac{1}{1+\sqrt{2}}
$$

To find the conjugate of the denominator $1+\sqrt{2}$, we *flip the sign* between the two terms. So, the conjugate of the denominator is

$$
1\,{\color{blue}{-}}\,\sqrt{2}
$$

Now, to rationalize the denominator, we start by multiplying both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate $1-\sqrt{2}$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}} &=  \\
\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1 - \sqrt{2}} &=  \\
(1 \cdot (1 - \sqrt{2}))/((1 + \sqrt{2})(1 - \sqrt{2})) &=  \\
(1 - \sqrt{2})/((1 + \sqrt{2})(1 - \sqrt{2})) &=
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we recall the difference of squares formula:

$$
(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2
$$

Applying the difference of squares formula with

$$
a=1, b=\sqrt 2
$$

to simplify the denominator, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1 - \sqrt{2})/((1 + \sqrt{2})(1 - \sqrt{2})) &=  \\
(1 - \sqrt{2})/(1^{2} - (\sqrt{2})^{2}) &=  \\
\frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1 - 2} &=  \\
\frac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{-1} &=  \\
&= \sqrt{2} - 1
\end{aligned}
$$

## Rationalizing Denominators Containing Two Terms

**Example:** Rationalize the denominator of $\dfrac {2}{\sqrt{3} - 1}$.

**Explanation**

First, note that the denominator's conjugate is $\sqrt{3} + 1$.

Now, to rationalize the denominator, we multiply both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{3} + 1$, and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned} \dfrac {2}{\sqrt{3} - 1} \\[5pt] &= \dfrac {2}{\sqrt{3} - 1} \cdot \dfrac {\sqrt{3} + 1} {\sqrt{3} + 1} \\[5pt] &= \dfrac {2\left(\sqrt 3 + 1\right)} {\left(\sqrt{3}\right)^2 - 1^2} \\[5pt] &= \dfrac {2\left(\sqrt 3 + 1\right)} {3 - 1} \\[5pt] &= \dfrac {2\left(\sqrt 3 + 1\right)} {2} \\[5pt] &= \sqrt 3 + 1 & \end{aligned}
$$

## Rationalizing Denominators Containing Two Surds

**Example:** Rationalize the denominator of $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{10} - \sqrt{6}}$.

**Explanation**

First, note that the denominator's conjugate is $\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}$.

Now, to rationalize the denominator, we multiply both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}$, and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{6}{\sqrt{10} - \sqrt{6}} &=  \\
\frac{6}{\sqrt{10} - \sqrt{6}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}} &=  \\
(6(\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}))/((\sqrt{10})^{2} - (\sqrt{6})^{2}) &=  \\
(6(\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}))/(10 - 6) &=  \\
(6(\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}))/(4) &=  \\
&= (3(\sqrt{10} + \sqrt{6}))/(2)
\end{aligned}
$$

## Rationalizing Denominators Containing Surds in the Numerator and Denominator

**Example:** Rationalize the denominator of $\dfrac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} + 1}$.

**Explanation**

First, note that the denominator's conjugate is $\sqrt{2} - 1$.

Now, to rationalize the denominator, we multiply both the numerator and denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{2} - 1$, and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} + 1} &=  \\
\frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2} + 1} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2} - 1}{\sqrt{2} - 1} &=  \\
((2 - \sqrt{2})(\sqrt{2} - 1))/((\sqrt{2} + 1)(\sqrt{2} - 1)) &=  \\
(2\sqrt{2} - 2 - (\sqrt{2})^{2} + \sqrt{2})/((\sqrt{2})^{2} - (1)^{2}) &=  \\
\frac{2\sqrt{2} - 2 - 2 + \sqrt{2}}{2 - 1} &=  \\
\frac{2\sqrt{2} - 4 + \sqrt{2}}{1} &=  \\
&= 3\sqrt{2} - 4
\end{aligned}
$$
