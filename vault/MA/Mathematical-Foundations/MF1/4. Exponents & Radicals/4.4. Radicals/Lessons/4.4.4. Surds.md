## Introduction

We know how to find the square root of a perfect square. But how do we find square roots of numbers that are not perfect squares?

For example, what is the square root of $5$?

$$
\sqrt 5 = \:
$$

Even though $5$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{5}$ is still a number. Such numbers are called **surds**. Surds are a new type of number that *cannot be written as a whole number or regular fraction*!

Surds can, however, be written as infinite, non-repeating decimals.

We can find a decimal approximation to the value of $\sqrt 5$ using the **square root button** $\bbox[lightgray, 2pt]{\sqrt{\phantom{x}}}$ on our calculator.

Different calculator models work differently. However, one of the following sequences should work with your calculator:

- Press $\bbox[lightgray, 2pt]{\sqrt{\phantom{x}}}$, type $\bbox[lightgray, 4pt]{\:5\:}$, and then press $\bbox[lightgray, 4pt]{\:=\:}$.
- Alternatively, you may need to type $\bbox[lightgray, 4pt]{\:5\:}$ first, then press $\bbox[lightgray, 2pt]{\sqrt{\phantom{x}}}$, followed by $\bbox[lightgray, 4pt]{\:=\:}$.

If you find yourself stuck, search for an online video that shows how to use the square root button with your calculator model.

When we do this, we get the following on our calculator's display.

$$
\sqrt{5} = 2.236\,067\,977
$$

The value given by the calculator is an *approximation* to the true value of $\sqrt 5$. In reality, the digits continue forever, and the pattern of decimal digits does not repeat. It is *impossible* to express $\sqrt 5$ precisely using decimals.

---

<a id="finding-a-decimal-approximation-of-a-square-root-surd"></a>
## Finding a Decimal Approximation of a Square Root Surd

**Example:** What is the value of $\sqrt{126}$ rounded to $3$ decimal places?

**Explanation**

Using a calculator, we find that

$$
\sqrt{126}=11.224\,972\,160\,..
$$

Now, we round to $3$ decimal places:

$$
\sqrt{126} \approx 11.225
$$

<a id="numbers-that-are-not-surds"></a>
## Numbers That Are Not Surds

A number is *not* a surd if we can write it as a positive or negative whole number or fraction.

For example, $\sqrt 9$ is *not* a surd because it is a perfect square and can be expressed as a whole number:

$$
\sqrt 9 = \sqrt{3^2} = 3
$$

Similarly,

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{16}}
$$

is *not* a surd because it can be written as a regular fraction (i.e., as the ratio of two whole numbers):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{\frac{1}{16}} &= \sqrt{\frac{1 \cdot 1}{4 \cdot 4}} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{4}} \\
&= \sqrt{(\frac{1}{4})^{2}} \\
&= \frac{1}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we cannot have surds involving square roots of negative numbers. In other words, the numbers

$\sqrt{-2}, \qquad \sqrt{-5}, \qquad \sqrt{-14}$
are *not* surds because they are *not real numbers*!

<a id="identifying-surds-square-roots-of-integers"></a>
## Identifying Surds: Square Roots of Integers

**Example:** Which of the following numbers are surds?

$\sqrt{2},\qquad \sqrt{8},\qquad \sqrt{16}$

**Explanation**

A surd is a number containing a radical symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{0}}$ that cannot be written as a positive or negative whole number or fraction.

With that in mind, let's look at each number in turn.

- $\sqrt{2}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt{2}=1.414\,213\,562\,..$.
- $\sqrt{8}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt{8}=2.828\,427\,124\,..$.
- $\sqrt{16}$ is **NOT** a surd. Since $16$ is a perfect square, we can get rid of the square root symbol ($\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$) and obtain a whole number:
$\sqrt{16}=\sqrt{4^2}=4$

Therefore, the correct answer is: $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8}$ only.

<a id="identifying-surds-square-roots-of-fractions"></a>
## Identifying Surds: Square Roots of Fractions

**Example:** Which of the following numbers are surds?
$\sqrt{\dfrac{5}{12}}, \qquad \sqrt{\dfrac{64}{81}}, \qquad \sqrt{\dfrac{10}{7}}$

**Explanation**

A surd is a number containing a radical symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{0}}$ that cannot be written as a positive or negative whole number or fraction.

With that in mind, let's look at each number in turn.

- $\sqrt{\dfrac{5}{12}}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt{\dfrac{5}{12}} = 0.645\,497\,\ldots$
- $\sqrt{\dfrac{64}{81}}$ is **NOT** a surd. Since $64$ and $81$ are perfect squares, we can get rid of the square root symbol $(\sqrt{\phantom{0}})$ and obtain a regular fraction.
$\sqrt{\frac{64}{81}}|= \sqrt{\frac{8^{2}}{9^{2}}}; = \sqrt{\frac{8 \cdot 8}{9 \cdot 9}}; = \sqrt{\frac{8}{9} \cdot \frac{8}{9}}; = \sqrt{(\frac{8}{9})^{2}}; = \frac{8}{9}$
- $\sqrt{\dfrac{10}{7}}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt{\dfrac{10}{7}} = 1.195\,228\,\ldots$

Therefore, the correct answer is:

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{5}{12}}
$$

and

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{10}{7}}
$$

only.

<a id="surds-with-cube-roots"></a>
## Surds With Cube Roots

Similarly, we can have surds involving the cube root sign ($\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$). For example,
$\sqrt[3]{2}, \quad\sqrt[3]{9}, \quad \sqrt[3]{11}, \quad \sqrt[3]{129}, \quad \ldots$
But $\sqrt[3]{8}$ is **not** a surd since we can get rid of the cube root:

$$
\sqrt[3]{8} = \sqrt[3]{2^3} = 2
$$

Most calculators have a "cube root button" $\bbox[lightgray, 2pt]{\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}}$ that can be used to compute cube roots.

Note that we can have surds involving cube roots ($\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$) of negative numbers, such as
$\sqrt[3]{-2}, \quad \sqrt[3]{-5}, \quad \sqrt[3]{-14}$,
because it's fine to take the cube root of a negative number.

---

<a id="identifying-cube-root-surds"></a>
## Identifying Cube Root Surds

**Example:** Which of the following numbers are surds?

$\sqrt[3]{2},\qquad \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{1}{27}},\qquad \sqrt[3]{11}$

**Explanation**

A surd is a number containing a radical symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{0}}$ that cannot be written as a positive or negative whole number or fraction.

Surds can be square roots $\sqrt{\phantom{0}}$, cube roots $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{0}}$, or higher roots such as $\sqrt[4]{\phantom{0}}, \sqrt[5]{\phantom{0}},\ldots$, etc.

With that in mind, let's look at each number in turn.

- $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt[3]{2} = 1.259\,921\,\ldots$
- $\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{1}{27}}$ is **NOT** a surd. Since $1$ and $27$ are perfect cubes, we can get rid of the cube root symbol $(\sqrt[3]{\phantom{0}})$ and obtain a regular fraction.
$\sqrt[3]{\frac{1}{27}}|= \sqrt[3]{\frac{1^{3}}{3^{3}}}; = \sqrt[3]{\frac{1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1}{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3}}; = \sqrt[3]{\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{3}}; = \sqrt[3]{(\frac{1}{3})^{3}}; = \frac{1}{3}$
- $\sqrt[3]{11}$ is a surd. Using a calculator, we find that $\sqrt[3]{11} = 2.223\,980\,\ldots$

Therefore, the correct answer is: $\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\sqrt[3]{11}$ only.
