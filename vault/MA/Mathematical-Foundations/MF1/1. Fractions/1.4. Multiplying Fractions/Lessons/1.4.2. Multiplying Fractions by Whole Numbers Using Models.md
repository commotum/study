## Introduction

We can use our knowledge of fraction models to multiply fractions by whole numbers.

For example, let's consider the following product:

$$
\dfrac{3}{10} \times 2
$$

We start by drawing a fraction model for

$$
\dfrac{3}{10}{:}
$$

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/78d672e6b186b8ed870451ab287a54fe.png>)

Multiplying

$$
\dfrac{\color{red}3}{10}
$$

by $\color{blue}2$ means that we need to repeat the fraction $\color{blue}2$ times.

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/58b6ed5286c1a9929cbab16aa05f72c6.png>)

Now, we count the number of shaded pieces.

There are

$$
{\color{red}3} \times {\color{blue}2} = 6
$$

shaded pieces in total. Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{3}{10} \times 2 = \dfrac{6}{10}
$$

## Multiplying a Fraction by a Whole Number Using Repeated Addition

**Example:** Use the model above to calculate $\dfrac{5}{6} \times 3$.

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/32aa2a93d1c7c7973930e45194f900c4.png>)

**Explanation**

Since we need to multiply

$$
\dfrac{\color{red}5}{6}
$$

by $\color{blue}3$, we repeat the fraction model $\color{blue}3$ times, as shown below.

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/c6f4b725fd2594cb23061e1b02d1c937.png>)

There are

$$
{\color{red}5} \times {\color{blue}3} = 15
$$

shaded pieces in total. Therefore:

$$
\dfrac{5}{6} \times 3 = \dfrac{15}{6}
$$

<a id="a-faster-method-for-multiplying-fractions-by-whole-numbers"></a>
## A Faster Method for Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers

There's another way we can use fraction models to multiply fractions by whole numbers.

To demonstrate, let's consider the following multiplication problem:

$$
\dfrac{2}{9} \times 4
$$

We start by drawing a fraction model to represent

$$
\dfrac{2}{9}
$$

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/a60df41b8c4219a6a72378fdeb6141f6.png>)

Now, rather than repeating our model $4$ times (which would take a long time), we can instead multiply the number of shaded pieces by $4$.

Our model has $2$ shaded pieces. So, the resulting model must have

$$
2 \times 4 = 8
$$

shaded pieces:

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/3cc3dcf7947a3e95129c5e84ddf4f160.png>)

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\dfrac{2}{9}\times 4 = \dfrac{8}{9}
$$

<a id="multiplying-a-fraction-by-a-whole-number-by-repeating-the-shaded-parts"></a>
## Multiplying a Fraction by a Whole Number by Repeating the Shaded Parts

**Example:** What number is missing from the multiplication problem above?

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/fdf4f7f5a19e46dcdc43c45ae386b52f.png>)

**Explanation**

We have

$$
\dfrac{3}{7}
$$

on the left and

$$
\dfrac{6}{7}
$$

on the right.

![](<../Source/1.4.2. Multiplying Fractions by Whole Numbers Using Models/Images/22a84f682e98111b66740a8c15b357fe.png>)

The shape on the left has $3$ shaded pieces, and the shape on the right has $6$ shaded pieces. So the number of shaded pieces has increased by a factor of ${\color{blue}2}$. (That is, the number of shaded pieces has been multiplied by ${\color{blue}2}$.)

Therefore, the multiplication problem shown is

$$
\dfrac{3}{7} \times {\color{blue}2} = \dfrac{6}{7}
$$

So, the missing number is ${\color{blue}2}$.
