## Introduction

How do we find the determinant of a $3\times3$ matrix such as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 2 & 4 \\ 5 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

One way to do this is to take the alternating sum of the entries in the top row of the matrix, multiplied by their respective minors, as shown below:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}7 & 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 2 & 4 \\ 5 & 0 & -1\end{vmatrix} &= (7)_(⏟)_(a_{11}) \cdot\begin{vmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 0 & -1\end{vmatrix}^(⏞)^(M_{11}) - (1)_(⏟)_(a_{12}) \cdot\begin{vmatrix}3 & 4 \\ 5 & -1\end{vmatrix}^(⏞)^(M_{12}) + (-3)_(⏟)_(a_{13}) \cdot\begin{vmatrix}3 & 2 \\ 5 & 0\end{vmatrix}^(⏞)^(M_{13}) \\
&= 7(-2 - 0) - 1(-3 - 20) - 3(0 - 10) \\
&=-14 + 23 + 30 \\
&= 39
\end{aligned}
$$

In the above, we have denoted $\color{blue}M_{1j}$ as the minor of the entry ${\color{red}a_{1j}}$.

In general, the determinant of a $3\times 3$ matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\det(A) = {\color{red}a_{11}}{\color{blue}M_{11}} - {\color{red}a_{12}}{\color{blue}M_{12}} + {\color{red}a_{13}}{\color{blue}M_{13}}
$$

That is, if $\begin{bmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i\end{bmatrix}$ then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i\end{vmatrix} &= a\begin{vmatrix}e & f \\ h & i\end{vmatrix}- b\begin{vmatrix}d & f \\ g & i\end{vmatrix}+ c\begin{vmatrix}d & e \\ g & h\end{vmatrix} \\
&= a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Important:** Do not forget about the *minus sign* in front of the *second term* in the formula!

<a id="calculating-the-determinant-of-a-3x3-matrix"></a>
## Calculating the Determinant of a 3x3 Matrix

**Example:** Find the value of $\begin{bmatrix}0 & -3 & 6 \\ 8 & 8 & 8 \\ -6 & -12 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Using the formula, we take the alternating sum of the entries in the top row of the matrix, multiplied by their respective minors, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}0 & -3 & 6 \\ 8 & 8 & 8 \\ -6 & -12 & 1\end{vmatrix} &= 0\begin{vmatrix}8 & 8 \\ -12 & 1\end{vmatrix}- (-3)\begin{vmatrix}8 & 8 \\ -6 & 1\end{vmatrix}+ 6\begin{vmatrix}8 & 8 \\ -6 & -12\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 0(8 + 96) + 3(8 + 48) + 6(-96 + 48) \\
&= 0 + 3 \cdot 56 + 6 \cdot (-48) \\
&= 168 - 288 \\
&=-120
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="singular-matrices"></a>
## Singular Matrices

If the determinant of a square matrix $A$ is equal to zero, then we say that $A$ is a **singular** matrix.

If a matrix is singular, then it does not have a multiplicative inverse.

---

<a id="identifying-singular-matrices"></a>
## Identifying Singular Matrices

**Example:** Which of the following statements are true regarding the matrix $A$ given below?

1. $\det(A) = 0$
2. $A$ is **not** a singular matrix
3. $A$ is a singular matrix

$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 & 7\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Remember that a square matrix is singular if its determinant is equal to $0$.

Using the formula, we take the alternating sum of the entries in the top row of the matrix, multiplied by their respective minors, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A) &= \begin{vmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 & 7\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 1\begin{vmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 4 & 7\end{vmatrix}- 2\begin{vmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 3 & 7\end{vmatrix}+ 3\begin{vmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 1(0 - 4) - 2(7 - 3) + 3(4 - 0) \\
&=-4 - 2 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 4 \\
&=-4 - 8 + 12 \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\det(A)=0$, the matrix is singular.

Therefore, only statements I and III are true.

<a id="solving-for-a-variable"></a>
## Solving for a Variable

Suppose we are given the $3\times 3$ matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ k & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
that has an unknown constant $k$ as one of the entries, and we are given that $A$ is singular. How do we find all the possible values of $k$?

One way is to use the formula for the determinant of a $3 \times 3$ matrix to find an expression for the determinant in terms of $k$, and then make an equation with the given determinant to solve for $k$.

So first, let's use the formula for the determinant on our matrix to get an expression in terms of $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ k & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1\end{vmatrix} &= 1\begin{vmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{vmatrix}- 2\begin{vmatrix}k & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{vmatrix}+ 3\begin{vmatrix}k & -1 \\ 0 & 2\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (-1 - 2) - 2(k - 0) + 3(2k - 0) \\
&=-3 - 2k + 6k \\
&= 4k - 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since we are told that $\det(A) =0$, let's equate the above expression to $0$ and solve for $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 \\ k & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
4k - 3 &= 0 \\
4k &= 3 \\
k &= \frac{3}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the only possible value for $k$ is

$$
\dfrac{3}{4}
$$

<a id="solving-for-an-unknown-given-the-determinant"></a>
## Solving for an Unknown Given the Determinant

**Example:** Given that the determinant of $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1 & 2 \\ k & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$ is $2$, find the value of the constant $k$.

**Explanation**

Let's use our formula to find an expression for the determinant in terms of $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}3 & 1 & 2 \\ k & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 2 & 3\end{vmatrix} &= 3\begin{vmatrix}4 & 5 \\ 2 & 3\end{vmatrix}- 1\begin{vmatrix}k & 5 \\ 0 & 3\end{vmatrix}+ 2\begin{vmatrix}k & 4 \\ 0 & 2\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 3(12 - 10) - 1(3k - 0) + 2(2k - 0) \\
&= 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 3k + 2 \cdot 2k \\
&= 6 - 3k + 4k \\
&= k + 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the determinant of the matrix is $2$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k + 6 &= 2 \\
k &= -4
\end{aligned}
$$
