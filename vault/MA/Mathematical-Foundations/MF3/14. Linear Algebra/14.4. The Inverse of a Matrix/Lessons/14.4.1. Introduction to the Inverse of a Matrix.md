## Introduction

Given the scalar $a$, the **multiplicative inverse** of $a$ is defined as the scalar $a^{-1}$ such that

$$
a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = 1
$$

For example, the multiplicative inverse of $2$ is the number

$$
2^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

because

$$
\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2 = 2\cdot \dfrac{1}{2} = 1
$$

Similarly, given a square $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the **inverse** of $A$ is defined as the $n \times n$ matrix $A^{-1}$ such that

$$
A \cdot A^{-1} = A^{-1} \cdot A = I_n
$$

where $I_n$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix.

For example, suppose we're given the following $2\times 2$ matrix:
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$

The inverse of the above matrix is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

To verify that the above matrix is in fact the inverse of $A$, first observe that if we multiply $A$ by the above matrix $A^{-1}$, then we get the identity matrix $I_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(A)\begin{bmatrix}-2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(A^{-1}) &= \begin{bmatrix}-1 \cdot (-2) + (-1) \cdot 1 & -1 \cdot (-1) + (-1) \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Likewise, if we multiply $A^{-1}$ by $A$, then we also get the identity matrix $I_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}_(⏟)_(A) &= I_{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

We do not yet know how to find the inverse of a matrix. However, we can verify that one matrix is the inverse of the other by carrying out the same procedure as we did here.

## Determining the Inverse of a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Using the method of direct verification, determine whether the matrix $B$ is the inverse of $A$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We must check whether

$$
AB = BA = I_2
$$

Computing $AB$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Likewise, computing $BA$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
BA &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{2}.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $B = A^{-1}$.

## Determining the Inverse of a Larger Matrix

**Example:** Using the method of direct verification, determine whether the matrix $B$ is the inverse of $A$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we check whether

$$
AB = BA = I_3
$$

Computing $AB$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
AB &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{3}.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Likewise, computing $BA$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
BA &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= I_{3}.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $B = A^{-1}$.

## Not Every Matrix Has an Inverse

The number $0$ does not have an inverse for multiplication over the real numbers, because there's no such number $a$ that

$$
a \cdot 0 = 1
$$

Instead, for every $a$ we have

$$
a \cdot 0 = 0
$$

Similarly, not every matrix has an inverse.

- If a matrix is not square, an inverse does not exist.
- Some square matrices do not have an inverse. If a square matrix does not have an inverse, then it is said to be **singular**. An example of a singular matrix is the zero matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$

We will soon learn how to calculate the inverse of a square matrix, and we'll also soon learn how to determine when a square matrix does not have an inverse.
