## Introduction

Any curve defined using polar coordinates $(r(\theta), \theta)$ is a parametric curve in the parameter $\theta$. For this reason, we can use the formula for differentiating parametric curves to compute the derivative of a polar curve:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

As an example, suppose we want to find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

for the polar curve

$$
r = \sin4\theta
$$

shown below.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Differentiating Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/d6d940fd7c73fc6fbabc561f770e4f31.png>)

First, recall that to convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x(\theta) = r \cos \theta, \qquad y(\theta) = r \sin \theta
$$

Now, using the fact that

$$
r=\sin 4\theta
$$

for this particular curve, we get

$$
x(\theta) = \sin 4\theta \cos\theta, \qquad y(\theta) =\sin4\theta \sin\theta
$$

To compute our derivative, we first need to calculate $x'(\theta)$ and $y'(\theta)$:

- First, we compute the derivative of $x$ with respect to $\theta$ using the product rule:
$x^{′}(θ)|= (\sin 4θ\cos θ)^{′}; = (\sin 4θ)^{′} \cdot \cos θ + \sin 4θ \cdot (\cos θ)^{′}; = (4\cos 4θ) \cdot \cos θ + \sin 4θ \cdot (-\sin θ); = 4\cos 4θ\cos θ - \sin 4θ\sin θ$
- Then, we compute the derivative of $y$ with respect to $\theta$ using the product rule:
$y^{′}(θ)|= (\sin 4θ\sin θ)^{′}; = (\sin 4θ)^{′} \cdot \sin θ + \sin 4θ \cdot (\sin θ)^{′}; = 4\cos 4θ \cdot \sin θ + \sin 4θ \cdot (\cos θ); = 4\cos 4θ\sin θ + \sin 4θ\cos θ$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= (y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)) \\
&= \frac{4\cos 4θ\sin θ + \sin 4θ\cos θ}{4\cos 4θ\cos θ - \sin 4θ\sin θ}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Derivative of a Polar Curve Given the Derivatives With Respect To X and Y

**Example:** Find $\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}$ for the polar curve $r = 2^{\theta}$ (shown above) given that
$y'(\theta) = 2^{\theta}(\ln 2\sin\theta+ \cos\theta), \qquad x'(\theta) = 2^{\theta}(\ln 2\cos\theta-\sin\theta)$.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Differentiating Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/e2076809a74372e836b4592936b7585b.png>)

**Explanation**

To find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the rule for differentiating parametric equations:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= (2^{θ}(\ln 2\sin θ + \cos θ))/(2^{θ}(\ln 2\cos θ - \sin θ)) \\
&= \frac{\ln 2\sin θ + \cos θ}{\ln 2\cos θ - \sin θ}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Derivative of a Polar Curve at a Point Given Derivatives With Respect To X and Y

**Example:** Find the slope of the tangent line to the polar curve $r = 1-\cos\theta$ at $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$, given that
$y'(\theta) = \cos\theta - \cos2\theta, \qquad x'(\theta) = -\sin\theta + \sin2\theta$.

**Explanation**

To find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the rule for differentiating parametric equations:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

Evaluating the numerator and denominator at

$$
\theta=\dfrac\pi4
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^{′}(\frac{π}{4}) &= \cos (\frac{π}{4}) - \cos (\frac{π}{2}) \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - 0 \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{′}(\frac{π}{4}) &= -\sin (\frac{π}{4}) + \sin (\frac{π}{2}) \\
&=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the slope of the tangent line is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} \mid _{θ = π/4} &= (y^{′}(\frac{π}{4}))/(x^{′}(\frac{π}{4})) \\
&= ((\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}))/((-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + 1))
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying the numerator and denominator by $2$ and simplifying, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} \mid _{θ = π/4} &= ((\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}))/((-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + 1)) \\
&= (2 \cdot (\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}))/(2 \cdot (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + 1)) \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{-\sqrt{2} + 2} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2 - \sqrt{2}} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2 - \sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{2 + \sqrt{2}}{2 + \sqrt{2}} \\
&= \frac{2\sqrt{2} + 2}{2} \\
&= \sqrt{2} + 1
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Derivative of a Constant Polar Curve

**Example:** Find $\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}$ for the polar curve $r = 2$ (shown above) at $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Differentiating Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/b6c3d21c49c399c4407247101d36d938.png>)

**Explanation**

To find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the formula for differentiating parametric curves:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

First, we recall that to convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta
$$

Using the fact that $r = 2$, we get

$$
x = 2\cos θ, y = 2\sin θ
$$

Calculating the necessary derivatives, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{′}(θ) &= -2\sin θ, y^{′}(θ) = 2\cos θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2\cos θ}{-2\sin θ}; =-\cot θ
$$

Finally, we arrive at

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} \mid _{θ = π/3} &= -\cot (\frac{π}{3}) \\
&=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Derivative of a Polar Curve

**Example:** Find $\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}$ for the polar curve $r = \theta$, shown above.

![](<../Source/3.2.5. Differentiating Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/11712254a9347c799840b9c88b9a8ee1.png>)

**Explanation**

To find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the formula for differentiating parametric curves:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

First, we recall that to convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta
$$

Using the fact that $r=\theta$, we get

$$
x = \theta\cos\theta, \qquad y=\theta\sin\theta
$$

To compute our derivative, we first need to calculate $x'(\theta)$ and $y'(\theta)$:

- First, we compute the derivative of $x$ with respect to $\theta$ using the product rule:
$x^{′}(θ)|= (θ\cos θ)^{′}; = (θ)^{′} \cdot \cos θ + θ \cdot (\cos θ)^{′}; = 1 \cdot \cos θ + θ \cdot (-\sin θ); = \cos θ - θ\sin θ$
- Then, we compute the derivative of $y$ with respect to $\theta$ using the product rule:
$y^{′}(θ)|= (θ\sin θ)^{′}; = (θ)^{′} \cdot \sin θ + θ \cdot (\sin θ)^{′}; = 1 \cdot \sin θ + θ \cdot \cos θ; = \sin θ + θ\cos θ$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= (y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)) \\
&= \frac{\sin θ + θ\cos θ}{\cos θ - θ\sin θ}
\end{aligned}
$$
