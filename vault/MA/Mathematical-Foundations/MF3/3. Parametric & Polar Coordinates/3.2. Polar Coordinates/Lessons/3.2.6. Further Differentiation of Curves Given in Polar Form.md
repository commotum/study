## Introduction

It's often helpful to use the double-angle formula for sine when computing the slope of the tangent to a polar curve, as this often makes the differentiation easier.

Let's consider the polar curve

$$
r = 1-2\cos\theta
$$

whose graph is shown below

![](<../Source/3.2.6. Further Differentiation of Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/54cbf289b2f4d6b8fd247ac436046c85.png>)

Recall that the slope of the tangent (in terms of $\theta$) is given by

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

First, to convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta
$$

Using the fact that

$$
r = 1-2\cos\theta
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= (1 - 2\cos θ)\cos θ \\
&= \cos θ - 2\cos^{2} θ, \\
y &= (1 - 2\cos θ)\sin θ \\
&= \sin θ - 2\sin θ\cos θ \\
&= \sin θ - \sin 2θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that we used the double-angle formula

$$
\color{black}\sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta
$$

to simplify our expression for $y$.

To compute our derivative, we need to calculate $x'(\theta)$ and $y'(\theta)$.

- First, we compute the derivative of $x$ with respect to $\theta$:
$x^{′}(θ)|= (\cos θ - 2\cos^{2} θ)^{′}; = (\cos θ)^{′} - (2\cos^{2} θ)^{′}; = (-\sin θ) - (2 \cdot 2\cos θ \cdot (-\sin θ)); =-\sin θ + 2 \cdot 2\sin θ\cos θ; =-\sin θ + 2 \cdot \sin 2θ; = 2\sin 2θ - \sin θ$
- Then, we compute the derivative of $y$ with respect to $\theta$:
$y^{′}(θ)|= (\sin θ - \sin 2θ)^{′}; = (\sin θ)^{′} - (\sin 2θ)^{′}; = \cos θ - 2\cos 2θ$

Finally, the slope of the tangent is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= (y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)) \\
&= \frac{\cos θ - 2\cos 2θ}{2\sin 2θ - \sin θ}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Differentiating Polar Curves Using the Double-Angle Formula for Sine

**Example:** Find $\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}$ for the polar curve $r =-2 \cos\theta$, shown above.

![](<../Source/3.2.6. Further Differentiation of Curves Given in Polar Form/Images/dc31fd8c1852c7552760d954f4a6b91b.png>)

**Explanation**

To find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the formula for differentiating parametric curves:

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

First, we recall that to convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta
$$

Using the fact that

$$
r=-2\cos\theta
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= -2\cos θ\cos θ =-2\cos^{2} θ, \\
y &= -2\cos θ\sin θ =-\sin 2θ
\end{aligned}
$$

where we used the identity

$$
\sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta
$$

To compute our derivative, we first need to calculate $x'(\theta)$ and $y'(\theta)$.

- First, we compute the derivative of $x$ with respect to $\theta$:
$x^{′}(θ)|= (-2\cos^{2} θ)^{′}; =-2 \cdot 2\cos θ \cdot (-\sin θ); = 4\sin θ\cos θ; = 2\sin 2θ$
- Then, we compute the derivative of $y$ with respect to $\theta$:
$y^{′}(θ)|= (-\sin 2θ)^{′}; = - (\cos 2θ \cdot 2); =-2\cos 2θ$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= (y^{′}(θ))/(x^{′}(θ)) \\
&= \frac{-2\cos 2θ}{2\sin 2θ} \\
&=-\cot 2θ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating Second Derivatives of Polar Curves

To find the first derivative of a parametric curve, we use the formula

$$
\dfrac{\textrm dy}{\textrm dx} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

A formula for the second derivative of a parametric curve can be found by applying the chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} &= \frac{d}{dx}(\frac{dy}{dx}) \\
&= \frac{d}{dθ}(\frac{dy}{dx}) \cdot \frac{dθ}{dx} \\
&= (\frac{d}{dθ}(\frac{dy}{dx}))/((\frac{dx}{dθ}))
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's see an example of how to apply this.

---

<a id="finding-the-second-derivative-of-a-polar-curve"></a>
## Finding the Second Derivative of a Polar Curve

**Example:** Calculate $\dfrac{\textrm d^2 y}{\textrm d x^2}$ for the polar curve $r=5$.

**Explanation**

We will use the formula

$$
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = (\frac{d}{dθ}(\frac{dy}{dx}))/((\frac{dx}{dθ}))
$$

First, we need to find

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}
$$

and

$$
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\theta}
$$

To convert from polar coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, we use

$$
x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta
$$

as parametric equations of a curve in terms of $\theta$.

Using the fact that $r=5$, we get

$$
x =5\cos\theta, \qquad y=5\sin\theta
$$

Then, to find

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x}
$$

we use the rule

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{y'(\theta)}{x'(\theta)}
$$

Calculating the necessary derivatives, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{′}(θ) &= -5\sin θ, \\
y^{′}(θ) &= 5\cos θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{5\cos θ}{-5\sin θ} \\
&=-\cot θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting into our formula for the second derivative, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} &= (\frac{d}{dθ}(-\cot θ))/(-5\sin θ) \\
&=-\frac{1}{5}\frac{\csc^{2} θ}{\sin θ} \\
&=-\frac{1}{5}\csc^{2} θ \cdot \frac{1}{\sin θ} \\
&=-\frac{1}{5}\csc^{2} θ \cdot \csc θ \\
&=-\frac{1}{5}\csc^{3} θ
\end{aligned}
$$
