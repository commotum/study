## Introduction

Suppose we're trying to find the **arc length** of a curve $y=f(x)$ between two points where $x=a$ and $x=b$. The arc length of a typical function between two points is shown below:

![](<../Source/9.5.3. The Arc Length of a Planar Curve/Images/8ba1aa711da92648de9b396e5344c86c.png>)

In this image, the solid line represents the length that we're trying to find, and the dashed line represents the continuation of the curve $y=f(x)$ beyond the points of interest.

The arc length of a curve $y=f(x)$ between the points where $x=a$ and $x=b$ is given by the formula

$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+\left[f'(x)\right]^2}\,\mathrm{d} x
$$

## Finding an Integral Representation For an Arc Length

**Example:** What is the arc length of the curve $y = \arccos{x}$ on $\left[0,\dfrac{1}{2}\right]$?

**Explanation**

The arc length of a curve $y=f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ can be found using the formula

$$
L = \int _a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, \textrm{d}x
$$

In our case,

$$
f(x)= \arccos{x}
$$

$a=0$, and

$$
b=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

First, we find the derivative of $f(x)$:

$$
f'(x) =-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}
$$

Substituting

$$
f'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}
$$

into the arc length formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1 + [f^{′}(x)]^{2}}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{1 - x^{2}}}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{1 - x^{2} + 1}{1 - x^{2}}}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{2 - x^{2}}{1 - x^{2}}}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-the-arc-length-of-a-smooth-curve-using-the-arc-length-formula"></a>
## Calculating the Arc Length of a Smooth Curve Using the Arc Length Formula

**Example:** Compute the arc length of the curve $y=x^{3/2}$ between the points $x=0$ and $x=\dfrac{4}{3}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/9.5.3. The Arc Length of a Planar Curve/Images/d295aff6e58a4512c5e355673a1589a0.png>)

**Explanation**

The arc length of a curve $y=f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ can be found using the formula

$$
L = \int _a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, \textrm{d}x
$$

In our case,

$$
f(x)= x^{3/2}
$$

$a=0$, and

$$
b=\dfrac{4}{3}
$$

First, we find the derivative of $f(x)$:

$$
f'(x) = \dfrac{3}{2}x^{1/2}
$$

Now, let's compute $\sqrt {1 + [f'(x)]^2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{1 + [f^{′}(x)]^{2}} &= \sqrt{1 + [\frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}]^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{1 + \frac{9}{4}x} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{4 + 9x}{4}} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(4 + 9x)} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{4 + 9x} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4 + 9x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting

$$
\sqrt {1 + [f'(x)]^2} = \dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{4 + 9x}
$$

to the formula for the arc length, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1 + [f^{′}(x)]^{2}}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{\frac{4}{3}}\sqrt{4 + 9x}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

We evaluate this integral using the substitution

$$
u= 4+9x
$$

Then,

$$
\textrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{9}\textrm{d}u
$$

We also need to compute new limits:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x & 0 & \frac{4}{3} \\ u & 4 & 16\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{\frac{4}{3}}\sqrt{4 + 9x}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{4}^{16}\sqrt{u} \cdot \frac{1}{9}du \\
&= \frac{1}{18}∫_{4}^{16}u^{\frac{1}{2}}du \\
&= \frac{1}{18}[\frac{2}{3}u^{\frac{3}{2}}]_{4}^{16} \\
&= \frac{1}{18} \cdot \frac{2}{3}[u^{\frac{3}{2}}]_{4}^{16} \\
&= \frac{1}{27}([(16)^{\frac{3}{2}}] - [(4)^{\frac{3}{2}}]) \\
&= \frac{1}{27}([64] - [8]) \\
&= \frac{56}{27}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying a Possible Curve Given Its Arc Length As an Integral

**Example:** The length of a curve $y=f(x)$ is given by ${\displaystyle \int_{5}^{6}} \sqrt{1+\dfrac {x^2}{x^2-16}} \,\textrm{d}x$. Which of the following could be an equation for this curve?

1. $y= \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-16}}$
2. $y= \sqrt{x^2-16} + \sqrt 5$
3. $y= \dfrac{x}{x^2-16} + 1$

**Explanation**

The arc length of a curve $y=f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ can be found using the formula

$$
L = \int _a^b \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, \textrm{d}x
$$

We're given that

$$
L=\int_{5}^{6} \sqrt{1+\dfrac {x^2}{x^2-16}} \,\textrm{d}x
$$

Comparing the above with the arc length formula, we see that we must have

$$
[f'(x)]^2=\dfrac {x^2}{x^2-16}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(x) = \pm \dfrac {x}{\sqrt{x^2-16}}
$$

Integrating the above equation with respect to $x$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= ± ∫\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2} - 16}}dx \\
&= ± \sqrt{x^{2} - 16} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

From the given options, the only one that has the required form is

$$
y= \sqrt{x^2-16} + \sqrt 5
$$

## The Intuition Behind the Main Formula

We've been using the following formula for the arc length of a curve $y=f(x)$ between the points where $x=a$ and $x=b{:}$

$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+\left[f'(x)\right]^2}\,\mathrm{d} x
$$

But where does this formula come from?

First, we divide the curve into small subintervals. Consider a part of the blue arc that corresponds to one of those subintervals and let the length of that part be $\Delta{s}$.

![](<../Source/9.5.3. The Arc Length of a Planar Curve/Images/c6df7895246f14aa221b90c4493d15b0.png>)

We can now denote the horizontal displacement as $\Delta{x}$ and the vertical shift as $\Delta{y}$.

![](<../Source/9.5.3. The Arc Length of a Planar Curve/Images/3df555891cea96717b74519d06b583d2.png>)

To approximate the length $\Delta{s}$ we apply the Pythagorean theorem, as follows:

$$
Δs \approx \sqrt{(Δx)^{2} + (Δy)^{2}} = \sqrt{1 + (\frac{Δy}{Δx})^{2}} \cdot Δx
$$

To approximate the total length $L$, we need to sum up all of the little $\Delta s$ terms, so

$$
L \approx \sum \Delta s = \sum \sqrt{\left(\Delta{x} \right)^2 + \left(\Delta{y} \right)^2} = \sum \left(\sqrt{1 + \left(\dfrac{\Delta{y}}{\Delta{x}} \right)^2} \cdot \Delta{x} \right)
$$

We then take the limit as $\Delta x, \Delta y \rightarrow 0$. As we do this, we obtain that

$$
\begin{aligned}
ds &= \sqrt{1 + (\frac{dy}{dx})^{2}}dx = \sqrt{1 + [f^{′}(x)]^{2}}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

the sum becomes a definite integral, and our approximation approaches the exact value of $L{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫ds \\
&= ∫_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1 + (\frac{dy}{dx})^{2}}dx \\
&= ∫_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1 + [f^{′}(x)]^{2}}dx
\end{aligned}
$$
