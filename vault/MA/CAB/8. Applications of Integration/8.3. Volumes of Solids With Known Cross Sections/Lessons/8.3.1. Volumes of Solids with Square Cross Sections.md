## Introduction

Consider the region that lies below the $x$-axis and is enclosed by the line $y=x-2$, between the vertical lines $x=0$ and $x=2$, as shown in the following picture.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/98ee7e9880e4d8906e5a911d7bf6095c.png>)

Suppose that we construct a solid with the following properties:

- The above triangle forms the base of the solid.
- The cross-sections are perpendicular to the $x$-axis and are all squares.

The solid is sketched below, with a few of the cross-sections shown. As we can see, the resulting solid looks a bit like a loudspeaker.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/24f3ce9b1966bc7591b2344774a06c3d.png>)

How do we find the volume of this solid?

If we are given the formula $A(x)$ for the area of the region determined by each cross-section, we find the volume as follows:

$$
V = \int_{a}^{b} A(x)\:\textrm{d}x
$$

To work out $A(x)$, let's first consider a cross-section at an arbitrary point $x$, where

$$
x \in \left(0,2\right)
$$

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/b5767caca77375655eeb5b042f5965c1.png>)

If we project this cross-section onto the $xy$-plane, then we get the following line segment which represents the base of the square:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/69d28eae920213cfcfabe8dbbd4a9849.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of the square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= \begin{vmatrix}y|= & x - 2\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= l^{2} \\
&= \mid x - 2 \mid ^{2} \\
&= x^{2} - 4x + 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Finallly, we calculate the volume $V$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}(x^{2} - 4x + 4)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}x^{2}dx - 4∫_{0}^{2}xdx + 4∫_{0}^{2}dx \\
&= \frac{x^{3}}{3}\begin{vmatrix}_{0}^{2} - 4 \cdot \frac{x^{2}}{2} & _{0}^{2} + 4x\end{vmatrix}_{0}^{2} \\
&= (\frac{8}{3} - 0) - 4(\frac{4}{2} - 0) + 4(2 - 0) \\
&= \frac{8}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the volume of the solid is

$$
V = \dfrac{8}{3}
$$

cubic units.

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by a Curve

**Example:** The base of a solid is a semicircle that lies below the $x$-axis and is enclosed by the curve $y=-\sqrt {4-x^2}$ from $x=-2$ to $x=2$, as shown below. Cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are squares. Calculate the volume of the solid.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/560ddb44393fb8c50a186bc4b90b6820.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider a cross-section at some point $x$, where $x \in (-2,2)$, and its projection onto the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/07bbc07e3202f573b7a694a753721be7.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of the square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l = \mid y &= \mid - \sqrt{4 - x^{2}}|= \sqrt{4 - x^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The area of a square is $A = l^2$. So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= l^{2} \\
&= (\sqrt{4 - x^{2}})^{2} \\
&= 4 - x^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now calculate the volume $V$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}(4 - x^{2})dx \\
&= 2∫_{0}^{2}(4 - x^{2})dx \\
&= 2(4x - \frac{x^{3}}{3}) \mid _{0}^{2} \\
&= 2([8 - \frac{8}{3}] - 0) \\
&= \frac{32}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by Two Curves

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region enclosed by the curves $y=x^2-2$ and $y=2$, as shown in the picture. If cross-sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are squares, what integral gives the volume of the solid?

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/b4fae96ff66ed45f3bab39240c54ecf6.png>)

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(x)=2
$$

and $g(x)=x^2-2$.

First, we determine the limits of integration. To do that, we find the intersections of the two curves by setting the functions equal to one another and solving for $x$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= g(x) \\
2 &= x^{2} - 2 \\
x^{2} - 4 &= 0 \\
(x - 2)(x + 2) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x=-2$ and $x=2$.

Now, consider a cross-section at some point $x$, where $x \in (-2,2)$, and its projection onto the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/bb0d2824a151c0588e4f9c1fc73923ea.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of the square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= \mid f(x) - g(x) \mid \\
&= \mid 2 - (x^{2} - 2) \mid \\
&= \mid 4 - x^{2} \mid
\end{aligned}
$$

The area of a square is $A = l^2$. So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x) &= l^{2} \\
&= (4 - x^{2})^{2} \\
&= 16 - 8x^{2} + x^{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can calculate the volume $V$ using the following integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{a}^{b}A(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}(16 - 8x^{2} + x^{4})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Cross Sections Perpendicular to the y-Axis

Now suppose that the base of our solid is the quarter-circle enclosed by the curve

$$
x=-\sqrt{1-y^2}
$$

and the $y$-axis from $y=-1$ to $y=0$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/a7976457c00b7b5d35e76ad5ab7f0d93.png>)

If the cross-sections perpendicular to the *$y$-axis* are squares, how do we find the volume of that solid?

The solid is sketched below, with a few of the cross-sections shown.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/e0cf8a1d88a70f33c03beccbb7ea0a04.png>)

If we are given the formula $A(y)$ for the region determined by each cross-section, then we can find the volume as follows:

$$
V = \int_{c}^{d} A(y)\:\textrm{d}y
$$

To work out $A(y)$, let's consider a cross-section at an arbitrary point $y$, where $y \in (-1,0)$:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/f4c4f4a722ce5a132f1a262c0a938e48.png>)

So, if we project this cross-section onto the $xy$-plane, we get the following line segment which represents the base of the square:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/cd82415411fa546e6ac42b09b9a2320e.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of the square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= \mid x \mid \\
&= \mid - \sqrt{1 - y^{2}} \mid \\
&= \sqrt{1 - y^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The area of a square is $A = l^2$. So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= l^{2} \\
&= (\sqrt{1 - y^{2}})^{2} \\
&= 1 - y^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can calculate the volume $V$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{c}^{d}A(y)dy \\
&= ∫_{-1}^{0}(1 - y^{2})dy \\
&= ∫_{-1}^{0}dy - ∫_{-1}^{0}y^{2}dy \\
&= y \mid _{-1}^{0} - \frac{y^{3}}{3} \mid _{-1}^{0} \\
&= (0 - (-1)) - (0 - ((-1)^{3})/(3)) \\
&= \frac{2}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Volume When the Cross Sections Are Perpendicular to the y-Axis

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region enclosed by the curve $x=y^2-1$ and the $y$-axis, $-1\le y \le 1$, as shown in the picture. Cross-sections perpendicular to the $y$-axis are squares. Calculate the volume of the solid.

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/8be87c1dfede38b59733193d135bdf5b.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider a cross-section at some point $y$, where $y \in (-1,1)$, and its projection onto the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/b1b315bc98ec5d56c44e5769aad05c44.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of the square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= \begin{vmatrix}x|= & y^{2} - 1\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The area of a square is $A = l^2$. So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= l^{2} \\
&= \mid y^{2} - 1 \mid ^{2} \\
&= (1 - 2y^{2} + y^{4})
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can calculate the volume $V$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{c}^{d}A(y)dy \\
&= ∫_{-1}^{1}(1 - 2y^{2} + y^{4})dy \\
&= (y - \frac{2y^{3}}{3} + \frac{y^{5}}{5}) \mid _{-1}^{1} \\
&= (1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5}) - (-1 + \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{5}) \\
&= \frac{8}{15} - (-\frac{8}{15}) \\
&= \frac{16}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Volume When the Base Is Bounded by Two Curves and Cross Sections are Perpendicular to the y-Axis

**Example:** The base of a solid is the region bounded by the curves $x=(y-1)^2$ and $x=2-(y-1)^2$, from $y=0$ to $y=2$, as shown in the picture. If cross-sections perpendicular to the $y$-axis are squares, what integral gives the volume of the solid?

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/6dd2e05889b665f339b99839db360e80.png>)

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(y)=2-(y-1)^2
$$

and

$$
g(y)=(y-1)^2
$$

Consider a cross-section at some point $y$, where

$$
y \in \left(0,2\right)
$$

, and its projection onto the $xy$-plane:

![](<../Source/8.3.1. Volumes of Solids with Square Cross Sections/Images/834e74c735418596ddfc36e33995ce3f.png>)

The length $l$ of a side of a square (cross-section) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
l &= \mid f(x) - g(x) \mid \\
&= \mid 2 - (y - 1)^{2} - (y - 1)^{2} \mid \\
&= \mid 2 - 2(y - 1)^{2} \mid \\
&= 2 \mid 1 - (y - 1)^{2} \mid \\
&= 2 \mid y^{2} - 2y \mid
\end{aligned}
$$

The area of a square is $A = l^2$. So, the expression for the area of the cross-section is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(y) &= l^{2} \\
&= (2 \mid y^{2} - 2y \mid)^{2} \\
&= 4(y^{4} - 4y^{3} + 4y^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now calculate the volume $V$ using the following integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{c}^{d}A(y)dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}4(y^{4} - 4y^{3} + 4y^{2})dy \\
&= 4∫_{0}^{2}(y^{4} - 4y^{3} + 4y^{2})dy
\end{aligned}
$$
