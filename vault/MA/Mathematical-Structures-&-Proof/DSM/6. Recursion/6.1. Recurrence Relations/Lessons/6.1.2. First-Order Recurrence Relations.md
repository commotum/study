## Introduction

A first-order linear homogeneous recurrence relation takes the form

$$
a_n = \lambda a_{n-1}
$$

where $\lambda$ is a constant.

The **general solution** of a recurrence relation expresses $a_n$ in terms of $n$, eliminating the need for recursion. Consider, for example,

$$
a_n = 2a_{n-1}, \qquad a_1=1, \qquad n \geq 2
$$

The sequence generated by this recurrence relation is:

$$
a_1 =1, \qquad a_2 = 2, \qquad a_3 = 4,\qquad a_4 = 8, \quad \ldots
$$

and this sequence can be represented by the general solution

$$
a_n = 2^{n-1}, \qquad n \geq 1
$$

Why do we want general solutions?

- Computing $a_n$ using the recurrence relation requires iterating the recurrence about $n$ times, computing all previous terms. If $n$ is large this can be slow and laborious. This can also make it hard to understand the qualitative behavior of $a_n$, like how fast it grows.
- Computing $a_n$ using the general solution gives $a_n$ more directly, which is faster. The qualitative behavior of $a_n$ is also usually easier to understand this way.

The general solution of a linear homogeneous recurrence relation

$$
a_n = \lambda \cdot a_{n-1}
$$

is given by

$$
a_n = A\cdot\lambda^n
$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant.

To see why this is true, note that according to the underlying recurrence relation, we have that

$$
a_{n-1} =\lambda \cdot a_{n-2}
$$

Substituting this into the initial recurrence relation, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= λa_{n - 1} \\
&= λ \cdot (λ \cdot a_{n - 2}) \\
&= λ^{2} \cdot a_{n - 2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
a_{n-2} = \lambda \cdot a_{n-3}
$$

Thus,

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= λ^{2}a_{n - 2} \\
&= λ^{2} \cdot (λ \cdot a_{n - 3}) \\
&= λ^{3} \cdot a_{n - 3}
\end{aligned}
$$

And so on. Repeating this process, we get that:

$$
a_n = \lambda^{n-1}\cdot a_1
$$

This can be formally proved using a technique known as **mathematical induction,** which is discussed in a separate lesson.

Finally, we conclude that the general solution of a linear homogeneous recurrence relation

$$
a_n = \lambda a_{n-1}
$$

is given by

$$
a_n = A\cdot\lambda^n
$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant that can be fixed if some particular value of $a_n$ is known.

In particular, from the last relation, we have

$$
a_1 = A\cdot\lambda \quad \Rightarrow \quad A=\dfrac{a_1}{\lambda}
$$

so we see that

$$
a_n = A\cdot\lambda^n
$$

and

$$
a_n = \lambda^{n-1}\cdot a_1
$$

are equivalent.

## Homogeneous First-Order Recurrence Relations

**Example:** Solve the following recurrence relation for $n\geq 1$.
$a_n - 6a_{n-1} = 0, \qquad a_1=7, \quad n \geq 2$.

**Explanation**

The general solution of a linear homogeneous recurrence relation

$$
a_n = \lambda a_{n-1}
$$

is given by

$$
a_n = A\cdot\lambda^n
$$

where $A$ is a constant.

So, the general solution of

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} - 6a_{n - 1} &= 0⇔a_{n} = 6a_{n - 1}
\end{aligned}
$$

is given by

$$
a_n = A \cdot 6^n
$$

Now, we find the constant $A$ by substituting the initial condition $a_1=7$ into the general solution:

$$
7 = A \cdot 6^1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A = \dfrac76
$$

Therefore, the solution is

$$
a_n = \dfrac76\cdot 6^n = \bbox[3pt, gainsboro]{\color{blue}7(6)^{n-1}}
$$

## Solutions to Inhomogeneous First-Order Recurrence Relations With Linear Coefficient 1

The **general solution** of a linear inhomogeneous recurrence relation

$$
a_n = a_{n-1} + g(n)
$$

with linear coefficient $1$ and **forcing function** $g(n)$ is

$$
a_n = a_1 + \sum_{k=2}^n g(k)
$$

To see why this is true, note that according to the underlying recurrence relation, we have that

$$
a_{n-1} = a_{n-2}+g(n-1)
$$

Substituting this into the initial recurrence relation, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= a_{n - 1} + g(n) \\
&= a_{n - 2} + g(n - 1) + g(n)
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
a_{n-2} = a_{n-3} + g(n-2)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= a_{n - 2} + g(n - 1) + g(n) \\
&= a_{n - 3} + g(n - 2) + g(n - 1) + g(n)
\end{aligned}
$$

And so on. Repeating this process, we get that

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= a_{1} + g(2) + g(3) + … + g(n - 2) + g(n - 1) + g(n) \\
&= a_{1} + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)g(k)
\end{aligned}
$$

which is the required solution to the linear recurrence relation with linear coefficient $1$ and inhomogeneous function $g(n)$. Again, this can be formally proved using mathematical induction.

## Inhomogeneous First-Order Recurrence Relations with Linear Coefficient 1: Exponential Forcing

**Example:** Solve the recurrence relation
$a_n = a_{n-1} + 6\cdot 7^n, \qquad a_1=8, \quad n \geq 2$.

**Explanation**

The solution of a linear recurrence relation

$$
a_n = a_{n-1} + g(n)
$$

with linear coefficient $1$ is given by

$$
a_n = a_1 + \sum_{k=2}^n g(k)
$$

Therefore, the solution of the recurrence relation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= a_{1} + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)g(k) \\
&= 8 + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)(6 \cdot 7^{k}) \\
&= 8 + 6∑_(k = 2)^(n)7^{k} \\
&= 8 + 6[(∑_(k = 1)^(n)7^{k}) - 7^{1}] \\
&= 8 + 6((7(7^{n} - 1))/(7 - 1) - 7) \\
&= 8 + 7(7^{n} - 1) - 42 \\
&= 7 \cdot 7 n - 41
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that we made use of the following result:

$$
\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n r^k = \dfrac{r(r^n-1)}{r-1}
$$

## Inhomogeneous First-Order Recurrence Relations with Linear Coefficient 1: Polynomial Forcing

**Example:** Solve the recurrence relation
$a_n = a_{n-1} + 3n^2 - n + 1, \qquad a_1= 3, \quad n \geq 2$.

**Explanation**

The solution of a linear recurrence relation

$$
a_n = a_{n-1} + g(n)
$$

with linear coefficient $1$ is given by

$$
a_n = a_1 + \sum_{k=2}^n g(k)
$$

Therefore, the solution of the recurrence relation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{n} &= a_{1} + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)g(k) \\
&= 3 + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)(3k^{2} - k + 1) \\
&= 3 + 3∑_(k = 2)^(n)k^{2} - ∑_(k = 2)^(n)k + ∑_(k = 2)^(n)1 \\
&= 3 + 3(∑_(k = 1)^(n)k^{2} - 1^{2}) - (∑_(k = 1)^(n)k - 1) + (n - 1) \\
&= 3 + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{6}(2n^{3} + 3n^{2} + n) - 3 - \frac{1}{2}(n^{2} + n) + 1 + n - 1 \\
&= n 3 + n 2 + n
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that we made use of the following results:

$$
\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n k^2 = \dfrac16(2n^3+3n^2+n), \qquad \displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^n k = \dfrac12(n^2+n)
$$
