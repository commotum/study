## Introduction

In a previous lesson, we learned how to convert between decimal integers and integers in base $b$, where

$$
b= 2,\, 3\, \ldots, \, 9
$$

Let's now learn how we can write a fractional number in any base.

For example, consider the following floating point fractional number in base $4{:}$

$$
\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SandyBrown}1}\,.\,\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{Purple}2}\,\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SteelBlue}3}
$$

To interpret this number, we use the place value system as usual. But now, instead of *decimal* place values (that correspond to powers of *ten*), we will use a base-$4$ place values (that correspond to the powers of *four*):

| **Place Values** | $4^0$ | . | $4^{-1}$ | $4^{-2}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Digits** | $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SandyBrown}1}$ | . | $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{Purple}2}$ | $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SteelBlue}3}$ |

In the place-value chart above:

- The digit $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SandyBrown}1}$ is in the $4^0$s place.
- The digit $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{Purple}2}$ is in the $4^{-1}$s place.
- The digit $\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{SteelBlue}3}$ is in the $4^{-2}$s place.

Notice that place values to the right of the dot $(\,.)$ correspond to negative powers of $4$ in descending order.

Therefore, our number can be written in base $10$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1.23)_{4} &= 1 \cdot 4^{0}^(⏞)^(whole) + 2 \cdot 4^{-1} + 3 \cdot 4^{-2}^(⏞)^(fraction) \\
&= 1 + 0.5 + 0.1875 \\
&= (1.6875)_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Any base $b$ number can be converted into a decimal number, and any decimal number can be written as a base $b$ number.

## Converting Fractions in Bases 4, 5, and 8 to Decimal

**Example:** What is $(56.6)_8$ written in base $10$?

**Explanation**

We can write down the number using a place value chart:

| **Place Values** | $8^1$ | $8^0$ | . | $8^{-1}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Digits** | $5$ | $6$ | . | $6$ |

Therefore, our number can be written in base $10$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(56.6)_{8} &= 5 \cdot 8^{1} + 6 \cdot 8^{0}^(⏞)^(whole) + 6 \cdot 8^{-1}^(⏞)^(fraction) \\
&= 40 + 6 + 0.75 \\
&= (46.75)_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="fractions-in-bases-3-6-7-and-9"></a>
## Fractions in Bases 3, 6, 7, and 9

Notice that finite floating-point fractions base $3, 6, 7, 9$ give infinite decimal representations.

Consider, for example, the number $(0.1)_3$, which is written in base $10$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(0.1)_{3} &= 0^{1}^(⏞)^(whole) + 1 \cdot 3^{-1}^(⏞)^(fraction) \\
&= 0 + \frac{1}{3} \\
&= (\frac{1}{3})_{10} \\
&= (0.333333…)_{10}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Converting Fractions in Bases 3, 6, 7, and 9 to Decimal

**Example:** What is $(4.31)_7$ written as a fraction in base $10$?

**Explanation**

We can write down the number using a place value chart:

| **Place Values** | $7^0$ | . | $7^{-1}$ | $7^{-2}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Digits** | $4$ | . | $3$ | $1$ |

Therefore, our number can be written in base $10$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(4.31)_{7} &= 4 \cdot 7^{0}^(⏞)^(whole) + 3 \cdot 7^{-1} + 1 \cdot 7^{-2}^(⏞)^(fraction) \\
&= 4 + \frac{3}{7} + \frac{1}{49} \\
&= \frac{218}{49}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="converting-a-decimal-fraction-to-a-fraction-in-another-base"></a>
## Converting a Decimal Fraction to a Fraction in Another Base

**Example:** What is $\left(74 + \dfrac{4}{5} + \dfrac{18}{25}\right)_{10}$ written in base $5$?

**Explanation**

First, we rewrite our decimal in descending powers of $5$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
74 + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{18}{25} &= 2 \cdot 25 + 4 \cdot 5 + 4 + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{18}{25} \\
&= 2 \cdot 25 + 4 \cdot 5 + 4 + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{15 + 3}{25} \\
&= 2 \cdot 25 + 4 \cdot 5 + 4 + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{3}{5} + \frac{3}{25} \\
&= 2 \cdot 25 + 4 \cdot 5 + 4 + 1 + \frac{2}{5} + \frac{3}{25} \\
&= 3 \cdot 25 + \frac{2}{5} + \frac{3}{25} \\
&= 3 \cdot 5^{2} + 0 \cdot 5^{1} + 0 \cdot 5^{0} + 2 \cdot 5^{-1} + 3 \cdot 5^{-2}
\end{aligned}
$$

The corresponding place value chart is given below:

| **Place Values** | $5^2$ | $5^1$ | $5^0$ | . | $5^{-1}$ | $5^{-2}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Digits** | $3$ | $0$ | $0$ | . | $2$ | $3$ |

Therefore,

$$
\left(74 + \dfrac{4}{5} + \dfrac{18}{25}\right)_{10} = (300.23)_5
$$

## Finite and Infinite Representations in Different Bases

Some rational numbers give a finite floating-point representation in some base and an infinite floating-point representation in another base.

For example, earlier, we saw that

$$
(0.1)_3 = \left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right)_{10} = (0.333\,333\ldots)_{10}
$$

Let's try to convert the base $3$ number $(0.1)_3$, into a base-$4$ number.

We extract the powers of $4$ from the number (starting from the largest possible one):

$$
\begin{aligned}
(0.1)_{3} &= (\frac{1}{3})_{10} \\
&= 1 \cdot 4^{-1} + \frac{1}{12} \\
&= 1 \cdot 4^{-1} + 1 \cdot 4^{-2} + \frac{1}{48} \\
&= 1 \cdot 4^{-1} + 1 \cdot 4^{-2} + 1 \cdot 4^{-3} + \frac{1}{192}
\end{aligned}
$$

However, this process will never end (at each step the remainder fraction becomes one-fourth of the previous step). So,

$$
(0.1)_3 = (0.\,111\,111\,\ldots)_4
$$
