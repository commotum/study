## Introduction

Recall that two sets $A$ and $B$ have the same cardinality if there exists a bijection from $A$ onto $B$. In such cases, we write

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

We've shown that two sets have the same cardinality by finding a bijection between them. However, finding a bijection between two sets can be difficult. So, is there another way to show that two sets have the same cardinality?

To answer this, we take inspiration from a well-known property of the real numbers.

Recall that if

$$
x\leq y
$$

*and*

$$
y\leq x
$$

for real $x$ and $y$, then $x$ and $y$ must be equal. We can write this symbolically as follows:

$$
(x\leq y) \land (y\leq x) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x = y
$$

As it turns out, this straightforward idea provides the key. So, let's start by thinking about what inequalities mean in the context of set cardinalities:

Consider the following sets:

$$
A = \{x,y,z\}, \qquad B = \{1,2,3,4\}
$$

For *finite* sets $A$ and $B$, the notation $\begin{vmatrix}A & < & B\end{vmatrix}$ means that $A$ contains fewer elements than $B$.

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/c869467961ce62523b444df8378560bf.png>)

As the image suggests, another way of thinking about the statement $\begin{vmatrix}A & < & B\end{vmatrix}$ is in terms of the function $f: A\rightarrow B$ indicated by the arrows in the diagram.

- The function $f$ is injective since no two distinct elements of $A$ map to the same element of $B$. In other words, $f$ is one-to-one.
- However, $f$ is *not* surjective since the element $4\in B$ is not paired with any element of $A$.
- Since $f$ is injective but not surjective, it's not bijective.

Moreover, it is *impossible* to find a bijective function that maps $A$ onto $B$, although finding injective functions is easy. This leads us to our first definition:

> *If there exists an injection $f:A \to B$ but there is no bijection between $A$ and $B$, we say that the cardinality of $A$ is (strictly) **smaller than** the cardinality of $B$.*
> $\begin{vmatrix}A & < & B\end{vmatrix}$

Following similar lines, we can also define

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}{:}
$$

> *If there exists an injection $f:A \to B$ that maps $A$ into $B$, we write*
> $\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}$.

We can illustrate

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

for two (possibly infinite) sets $A$ and $B$ as follows:

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/dfb72f200c9feadbe98f9a3ff8f12e67.png>)

In previous lessons, we saw that the cardinality of the natural numbers is

$$
\mid \mathbb N \mid = \aleph_0
$$

the cardinality of the reals is

$$
\mid \mathbb R \mid = \mathfrak{c}
$$

and that these two cardinalities are different. We're now able to state that
$\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb N & < & \mathbb R\end{vmatrix}$.
To see why, note the following:

- It's easy to find an injective function $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$. For example $f(n) = n$ is injective.
- However, Cantor's diagonal argument shows no bijection exists between $\mathbb N$ and $\mathbb R$.
- Therefore, by definition, we have $\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb N & < & \mathbb R\end{vmatrix}$, i.e.,
$\aleph_0 < \mathfrak{c}$.

<a id="the-cantor-bernstein-schrder-theorem"></a>
## The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem

We're now able to discuss the main result of this lesson.

> *If $\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}$ and $\begin{vmatrix}B & \leq & A\end{vmatrix}$, then $\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}$. In other words, if there exist injections $f:A \to B$ and $g:B \to A$, then there exists a bijection $h$ between $A$ and $B$.*

This theorem is known as the **Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem.** It gives us a handy method of showing that two sets have the same cardinality without explicitly finding a bijection between them.

Consider the following diagram:

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/d90eacf7f32725b0b9ab3561b174636a.png>)

Note the following:

- The injection $f$ maps $A$ to $f(A)$, where $f(A)\subseteq B$.
- The injection $g$ maps $B$ to $g(B)$, where $g(B)\subseteq A$.
- Since there is an injection from $A$ to $B$, *and* an injection from $B$ to $A$, the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem guarantees there must be a bijection $h$ between $A$ and $B$.
- Since a bijection exists between $A$ and $B$, these sets must have the same cardinality.

## Cardinality of Closed and Semi-Closed Intervals of Real Numbers

We saw previously that for any $a, b\in \mathbb{R} \cup \{\pm \infty \}$ with $a < b$, we have that

$$
\big \mid (a,b) \big \mid = \mathfrak{c}
$$

We can use the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem to prove that the following sets also have cardinality $\mathfrak c{:}$

$$
[a,b), \qquad (a,b], \qquad [a,b], \qquad [a,\infty), \qquad (-\infty,b]
$$

For example, given that

$$
\big \mid (2,4) \big \mid = \mathfrak{c}
$$

let's prove that $\begin{bmatrix}MATH: \big & (2,4\end{bmatrix} \big \mid = \mathfrak{c}.]$

According to the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, if

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\begin{vmatrix}B & \leq & A\end{vmatrix}
$$

then

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

In other words, if there exist injections $f:A \to B$ and $g: B \to A$, then there exists a bijection between $A$ and $B$.

With that in mind, we note the following:

- Notice that the function $f: x \to x$ is an injection from $(2,4)$ into $[MATH: (2,4].]$ So, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}MATH: \big & (2,4) \big & \leq \big & (2,4\end{bmatrix} \big \mid.]$

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/fa911d9c8d98cf05db8dc0199c214728.png>)

- On the other hand, the function
$x \to 0.5x$
injectively maps $[MATH: (2,4]]$ onto $[MATH: (1, 2].]$ Thus,
$g: x \to 0.5x + 1$
is an injection from $[MATH: (2,4]]$ onto $[MATH: (2,3] \subseteq (2,4).]$ So, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}MATH: \big & (2,4\end{bmatrix} \big\begin{vmatrix}\leq \big & (2,4) \big\end{vmatrix}.]$

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/7ea7b0ec878dc947aa243736d8a47068.png>)

Therefore, by the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem,

$\begin{bmatrix}MATH: \big & (2,4\end{bmatrix} \big\begin{vmatrix}= \big & (2,4) \big\end{vmatrix} = \mathfrak{c}.]$

$$

$$
\begin{vmatrix}B & \leq & A\end{vmatrix}

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}

According to the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, if

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}

$$

$$

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}

$[MATH: \big \mid (-\infty,-4) \cup \begin{bmatrix}1,\infty) \big & \leq & \mathbb{R} &.\end{bmatrix}$

$[MATH: \big \mid (-\infty,-4) \cup \begin{bmatrix}1,\infty) \big & = & \mathbb{R} & = \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\mathfrak{c}}.\end{bmatrix}$

## Proving the Existence of a Bijection Between a Square and an Interval

As an example of an application of the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, let's prove that

$$
\big\begin{vmatrix}(0,1) \times (0,1) \big & = \big & (0,1) \big\end{vmatrix} = \mathfrak{c}
$$

Obviously, the function $f: x \to (x, 0.5)$ for $x\in (0,1)$ is an injection from $(0,1)$ into $(0,1) \times (0,1)$.

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/36e78ab7f194381775a54a78621355b9.png>)

Now, let's construct an injection from $(0,1) \times (0,1)$ to $(0,1)$.

Recall that

$$
(0,1) \times (0,1) = \{(a,b) \mid a,b \in (0,1) \}
$$

and any real number from the interval $(0,1)$ can be represented as a decimal with zero whole part. We will consider only the decimal representations that do not have repeating $9$'s at the end. For example,

$$
0.5=0.4\overline{9}
$$

but we take only the representation $0.5$.

As a result, any element from $(0,1) \times (0,1)$ can be uniquely represented by the decimal pair

$$
\big(0.a_1a_2a_3 \ldots, \: 0.b_1b_2b_3 \ldots \big)
$$

Next, let's define a function $g: (0,1) \times (0,1) \to (0,1)$ that maps the above pair to the number

$$
0.a_1b_1a_2b_2a_3b_3 \ldots
$$

This function is an injection since

$$
g(x) \neq g(y)
$$

whenever

$$
x \neq y
$$

![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/cf26071700e67ecfd272f92b6f4b859f.png>)

Therefore, according to the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, we have that

$$
\big\begin{vmatrix}(0,1) \times (0,1) \big & = \big & (0,1) \big\end{vmatrix}
$$

Similarly, we can prove that

$$
\big\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb{R}^2 \big & = \big & \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \big & = \big & \mathbb{R} \big\end{vmatrix} = \mathfrak{c}
$$

## Applying the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem to Cartesian Products

**Example:** Given that $\big \mid \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \big \mid = \mathfrak{c}$, what are the missing entries in the reasoning below regarding the cardinality of the set $\mathbb R\times [-2,3]$.

> Since the function $f: (x,y) \to (x, y)$ (that maps any ordered pair to itself) is an injection from $\mathbb{R} \times [-2,3]$ into $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$, and
> $g: (x,y) \to \bigg(x, \bbox[2pt, border: 1pt solid black]{\phantom{AAAAAA}} \bigg)$
> is an injection from $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ **onto** $\mathbb{R} \times (-1,1) \subseteq \mathbb{R} \times [-2,3]$, according to Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, we have that
> $\big \mid \mathbb{R} \times [-2,3] \big \mid = \bbox[2pt, border: 1pt solid black]{\phantom{AAA}}$.

**Explanation**

According to the Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem, if

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & \leq & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\begin{vmatrix}B & \leq & A\end{vmatrix}
$$

then

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

In other words, if there exist injections $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to A$, then there exists a bijection between $A$ and $B$.

With that in mind, we note the following:

- The function $f: (x,y) \to (x, y)$ (that maps any ordered pair to itself) is an injection from $\mathbb{R} \times [-2,3]$ into $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$. So, we have
$\big\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb{R} \times [-2,3] \big & \leq \big & \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \big\end{vmatrix}$.
- On the other hand, the function
$x \to x$
injectively maps $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, and
$y \to \dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(y)$
injectively maps $\mathbb{R}$ onto $(-1,1) \subseteq [-2,3]$. Thus,
$g: (x,y) \to \bigg(x, \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\dfrac{2}{\pi}} \, \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\arctan(y)} \bigg)$
is an injection from $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ onto $\mathbb{R} \times (-1,1) \subseteq \mathbb{R} \times [-2,3]$, as shown below.![](<../Source/5.3.5. The Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder Theorem/Images/6cf0f78c4e17a4223868f949a11c3411.png>)
So, we have
$\big\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \big & \leq \big & \mathbb{R} \times [-2,3]\big\end{vmatrix}$.

Therefore, by Cantor-Bernstein-Schröder theorem,

$$
\big\begin{vmatrix}\mathbb{R} \times [-2,3] \big & = \big & \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \big\end{vmatrix} = \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\mathfrak{c}}
$$
