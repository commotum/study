## Introduction

Recall that the **principle of induction** states the following:

> *Suppose we have a sequence of mathematical statements $P(1), \, P(2), \, P(3), \, \ldots$, where*
> - *$P(1)$ is true, and*
> - *$P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)$ for every $k\in\mathbb N$.*
> *Then, $P(n)$ is true for every $n\in\mathbb N$.*

Let's recall our general strategy for proving results using induction:

- **Step 1**: Base Case
- **Step 2**: Inductive Hypothesis
- **Step 3**: Inductive Step
- **Step 4**: Conclusion

In this lesson, we'll learn how induction can be used to prove matrix identities.

## A Worked Example

Let's prove the following result using mathematical induction:

> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$

In this case, the general statement $P(n)$ we're required to prove is the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

We start by proving the base case, $P(1)$. To do this, we substitute $n=1$ into both the left-hand side and right-hand side and show that they're equal:

> **Base Case**: We first prove our statement is true for the base case $n=1$.
> For the left-hand side, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{1} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$,
> and for the right-hand side, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2(1) \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
> Thus, our statement is true for $n=1$.

Next, we state our inductive hypothesis. Here, we assume that the given statement is true for some

$$
n=k\geq 1
$$

> **Inductive Hypothesis**: We assume that for some $n=k\geq 1$, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

We must show that $P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)$. Let's start by writing down $P(k+1)$. This is the first part of the **inductive step**.

> **Inductive Step**: We're required to prove that
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

The idea is to rewrite the left-hand side of $(\ast)$ *using the inductive hypothesis* and apply algebraic techniques to show that this equals the right-hand side.

When proving matrix identities using induction, the key to unlocking the proof is to factor out one instance of the matrix, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Let's apply this technique in this case:

> We write
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{k + 1}|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{k} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
> By the inductive hypothesis, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{k} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2k \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 + 2k \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2(k + 1) \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$,
> as required.

Since we've shown that the left-hand side of $(\ast)$ equals the right-hand side, our proof is complete. So, let's write down our conclusion.

> Therefore, by the principle of induction,
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

## Completing a Proof Template

**Example:** Suppose we wish to prove the following statement using mathematical induction:

$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

What are the missing entries in the proof template below?

> **Base Case**: We first prove that our statement is true for the base case $n=1$.
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> **Inductive Hypothesis**: We assume that for some $000000$ we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> **Inductive Step**: We're required to prove that
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> **Conclusion**: Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Explanation**

To prove a statement using induction, we use the following framework:

- **Step 1**: Base Case
- **Step 2**: Inductive Hypothesis
- **Step 3**: Inductive Step
- **Step 4**: Conclusion

In this case, the general statement $P(n)$ we're required to prove the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

We start by proving the **base case**, $P(1)$. To do this, we substitute $n=1$ into both the left-hand side and right-hand side.

In our case, this gives the following:

> **Base Case**: We first prove that our statement is true for the base case $n=1$.
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Proving that the base case is true is usually straightforward. We simply calculate the left-hand and right-hand sides independently and show they're equal.

Next, we write down our **inductive hypothesis**. We assume that $P(k)$ is true for some

$$
n=k \geq 1
$$

To do this, we simply replace $n$ with $k$ in our original statement $P(n)$.

In our case, this gives the following:

> **Inductive Hypothesis**: We assume that for some $n = \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}k \geq 1}$, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

Then, we move onto the **inductive step**: We must show that $P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)$. We start by writing down $P(k+1)$.

> **Inductive Step**: We're required to prove that
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

The inductive step is where most of the work lies. The idea is to rewrite the left-hand side of this equation using the inductive hypothesis and apply algebraic techniques to show that this equals the right-hand side.

Once we're shown that $P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)$, the proof is complete, and we state our **conclusion**.

> **Conclusion**: Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 7 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
> for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

## Proving Matrix Identities

**Example:** Use mathematical induction to prove that
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$
for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Explanation**

The principle of induction states the following: Suppose we have a sequence of mathematical statements $P(1), P(2), P(3), \ldots$, where

- $P(1)$ is true, and
- $P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)$ for every $k\in\mathbb N$.

Then, $P(n)$ is true for every $n\in\mathbb N$.

In this case, the general statement $P(n)$ we're required to prove is the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

We start by proving the base case, $P(1)$. To do this, we substitute $n=1$ into both the left-hand side and right-hand side and show that they're equal:

> We proceed using induction on $n$.
> **Base Case**: We first prove our statement is true for the base case $n=1$.
> For the left-hand side, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}^{1} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$,
> and for the right-hand side, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4^{1} - 1 & 4^{1}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$.
> Thus, our statement is true for $n=1$.

Next, we state our inductive hypothesis. Here, we assume that the given statement is true for some

$$
n=k\geq 1
$$

> **Inductive Hypothesis**: We assume that for some $n=k\geq 1$, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$

We must show that $P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)$. Let's start by writing down $P(k+1)$. This is the first part of the **inductive step**.

> **Inductive Step**: We're required to prove that
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$

The idea is to rewrite the left-hand side of $(\ast)$ *using the inductive hypothesis* and apply algebraic techniques to show that this equals the right-hand side.

When proving matrix identities using induction, the key to unlocking the proof is to factor out one instance of the matrix, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Let's apply this technique in this case:

> We write
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}^{k + 1}|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}^{k} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$.
> By the inductive hypothesis, we have
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}^{k} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4^{k} - 1 & 4^{k}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4^{k} - 1 + 3 \cdot 4^{k} & 4 \cdot 4^{k}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4 \cdot 4^{k} - 1 & 4 \cdot 4^{k}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 4^{k + 1} - 1 & 4^{k + 1}\end{bmatrix}$,

as required.

Since we've shown that the left-hand side of $(\ast)$ equals the right-hand side, our proof is complete. So let's write down our conclusion.

> Therefore, by the principle of induction,
> $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$
> is true for all $n\geq 1$.
