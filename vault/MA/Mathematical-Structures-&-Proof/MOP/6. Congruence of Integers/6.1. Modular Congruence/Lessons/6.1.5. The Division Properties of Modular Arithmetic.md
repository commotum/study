## Introduction

In a previous lesson, we saw that congruences obey the following scalar multiplication property:

$$
a\equiv b \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,n) \quad \Longrightarrow\quad ka \equiv kb \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,kn)
$$

It turns out that for

$$
k\neq 0
$$

we can perform this operation in reverse. That is,

$$
ka \equiv kb \quad(\text{mod}\,kn) \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,n)
$$

Therefore, for

$$
k\neq 0
$$

we have the following **division property,** which we can write as an equivalence:

$$
ka \equiv kb \quad(\text{mod}\,kn) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,n)
$$

For example, consider the following true statement:

$$
12 \equiv 6 \quad (\text{mod}\:6)
$$

Note that all three numbers in our congruence have a common factor of ${\color{blue}3}$.

$$
{\color{blue}3} \cdot 4 \equiv {\color{blue}3} \cdot 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:{\color{blue}3} \cdot 2)
$$

Therefore, by the division property, we can cancel a factor of ${\color{blue}3}$ from both sides and the modulus.
$\begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 4≡3 \cdot 2 & (mod3 \cdot 2) \\ 4≡2 & (mod2)\end{bmatrix}$

It's important to note that the division property does not hold if $k=0$. For example, consider the following counterexample.

$$
0\cdot 2 \equiv 0\cdot 1 \qquad \Longrightarrow\qquad 2\equiv 1 \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,2)
$$

The antecedent is true, the consequent is false, and hence the implication is *false*.

## Using the First Division Property

**Example:** Given that $4a \equiv 4b \,(\textrm{mod}\,8)$, which of the following statements **must** be true?

1. $a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,4)$
2. $a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,2)$
3. $a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,8)$

**Explanation**

The division property of modular arithmetic states that for

$$
k\neq 0
$$

we have

$$
ka\equiv kb\quad(\textrm{mod} \,kn) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow\qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\, n)
$$

We're asked to consider the following statement:

$$
4a \equiv 4b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,8)
$$

Notice that

$$
\text{gcd}(4,8) =4
$$

Applying the division property, we have the following:
$\begin{bmatrix}4a & ≡4b & (mod8) \\ 4 \cdot a & ≡4 \cdot b & (mod4 \cdot 2) \\ 4 \cdot a & ≡4 \cdot b & (mod4 \cdot 2) \\ a & ≡b & (mod2)\end{bmatrix}$

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only".

<a id="the-second-division-property"></a>
## The Second Division Property

In a previous lesson, we saw that congruences obey another scalar multiplication property:

$$
a\equiv b \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,n) \quad \Longrightarrow\quad ka \equiv kb \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,n)
$$

The (second) **division property** of modular arithmetic tells us that

$$
ka\equiv kb\quad(\textrm{mod} \,n) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow\qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\, n)
$$

*provided that $k$ and $n$ are coprime*. In other words, we require

$$
\textrm{gcd}(k,n)=1
$$

**Caution:** The condition that $k$ and $n$ are coprime is *very* important! The division property does *not* work when $k$ and $n$ are not coprime!

This might seem a little puzzling, so let's look at some concrete examples:

- Consider the following true statement:
$12 \equiv 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:5)$
Notice that the two numbers in our congruence have a common factor of ${\color{blue}2}{:}$
${\color{blue}2} \cdot 6 \equiv {\color{blue}2} \cdot 1 \quad (\text{mod}\:5)$
Since $\textrm{gcd}({\color{blue}2},5)=1$, we can cancel out ${\color{blue}2}$ from both sides, keeping the modulus unchanged.
$6 \equiv 1 \quad (\text{mod}\:5)$
- Next, consider the following true statement:
$12 \equiv 6 \quad (\text{mod}\:6)$
Notice that the two numbers in our congruence, *and the modulus*, have a common factor of ${\color{red}3}{:}$
${\color{red}3} \cdot 4 \equiv {\color{red}3} \cdot 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:6)$
Because $\textrm{gcd}({\color{red}3},6) = 3 \neq 1$, we *cannot* cancel out ${\color{red}3}$ from both sides. If we do, we get the following *false* statement!
$4\equiv 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:6)$.
In such cases, we should use the first division property to simplify the congruence.

## Using the Second Division Property

**Example:** If $ka \equiv kb \: (\textrm{mod} \,7)$
is equivalent to $%\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad a \equiv b \:(\textrm{mod} \,7)$,
which of the following could be the value of $k$?

1. $k=-12$
2. $k=4$
3. $k=-14$

**Explanation**

The division property of modular arithmetic states that

$$
ka\equiv kb\quad(\textrm{mod} \,n) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow\qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\, n)
$$

provided that $k$ and $n$ are coprime. In other words, we require

$$
\text{gcd}(k,n)=1
$$

In this case, the modulus $n=7$. Let's consider each value of $k$ in turn.

- If $k=-12$, then $\text{gcd}(k,n) = \text{gcd}(-12,7) = 1$. Therefore, our equivalence is valid for $k=-12$.
- If $k=4$, then $\text{gcd}(k,n) = \text{gcd}(4,7) = 1$. Therefore, our equivalence is valid for $k=4$.
- If $k=-14$, then $\text{gcd}(k,n) = \text{gcd}(-14,7) = 7\neq 1$. Therefore, our equivalence is *not* valid for $k=-14$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only".

<a id="combining-the-division-properties"></a>
## Combining the Division Properties

**Example:** Given that $15a \equiv 15b \,(\textrm{mod}\,20)$, which of the following statements **must** be true?

1. $a \equiv b \quad (\textrm{mod}\, 20)$
2. $a \equiv b \quad (\textrm{mod}\,4)$
3. $7a \equiv 7b \quad (\textrm{mod}\,4)$

**Explanation**

The division properties of modular arithmetic state the following:

- For $k\neq 0$, we have
$ka \equiv kb \quad(\text{mod}\,kn) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,n)$.
- For $\text{gcd}(k, n) = 1$, we have
$ka \equiv kb \quad(\text{mod}\,n) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,n)$.

With that in mind, let's consider each statement.

- Statement I is false, while statement II is true. Applying the first property, we have the following:
$\begin{bmatrix}15a & ≡15b & (mod20) \\ 5 \cdot 3 \cdot a & ≡5 \cdot 3 \cdot b & (mod5 \cdot 4) \\ 5 \cdot 3 \cdot a & ≡5 \cdot 3 \cdot b & (mod5 \cdot 4) \\ 3a & ≡3b & (mod4)\end{bmatrix}$
Since $\gcd(3,4)=1$, by the second property, we have:
$3a \equiv 3b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,4) \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad a \equiv b \quad(\textrm{mod}\,4)$
- Statement III is true. By statement II and the second property, we have:
$a≡b(mod4)⟺7a≡7b(mod4)$

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."

<a id="proving-the-division-properties"></a>
## Proving the Division Properties

Let's see why the division properties work.

- The proof of the first property is straightforward:
$ka≡kb(modkn) \mid ⟺kn\begin{vmatrix}(ka - kb) \\ ⟺kn\end{vmatrix}k(a - b); ⟺kn\begin{vmatrix}k(a - b) \\ ⟺n\end{vmatrix}(a - b); ⟺a≡b(modn)$

- To prove the second division property, suppose that $\textrm{gcd}(k,n)=1$ and ${\color{blue}k}a\equiv {\color{blue}k}b \: (\textrm{mod} \,n)$. Then, according to the definition of congruent integers, we obtain
$ka≡kb(modn) \mid ⟺n\begin{vmatrix}(ka - kb) \\ ⟺n\end{vmatrix}k(a - b)$.
Since $k$ and $n$ are coprime and $k(a - b)$ is divisible by $n$, we have that $(a - b)$ must be divisible by $n$. Now, using the definition again, we get
$n \mid (a - b) \mid ⟺a≡b(modn)$.
