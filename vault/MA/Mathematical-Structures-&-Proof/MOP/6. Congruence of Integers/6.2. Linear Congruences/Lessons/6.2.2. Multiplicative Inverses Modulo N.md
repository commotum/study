## Introduction

The multiplicative inverse of an integer $a$ modulo $n$ is the unique number $x$ such that

$$
a \cdot x \equiv 1 \quad (\textrm{mod}\:n)
$$

The word "unique" in the statement above means with respect to the inequality

$$
0 \leq x < n
$$

For example,

$$
x={\color{red}{4}}
$$

is the multiplicative inverse of

$$
a={\color{blue}{6}}
$$

modulo $23$ since
$6 \cdot 4\begin{vmatrix}≡24 & (mod23) \\ ≡23 + 1 & (mod23) \\ ≡0 + 1 & (mod23) \\ ≡1 & (mod23)\end{vmatrix}$.

Note the following:

- We sometimes denote the multiplicative inverse of $a$ as $a^{-1}$. So, ${\color{blue}{6}}^{-1} = {\color{red}{4}}$ in this example.
- Be careful not confuse the notation $a^{-1}$ with the reciprocal of $a$. In this context, both $a$ and $a^{-1}$ are *integers.*
- The uniqueness of $a^{-1}$ is up to congruence modulo $n$. For example, since ${\color{red}{27}}\equiv {\color{red}{4}}\:(\textrm{mod}23)$, we have
$27 \cdot 6\begin{vmatrix}≡162 & (mod23) \\ ≡7 \cdot 23 + 1 & (mod23) \\ ≡7 \cdot 0 + 1 & (mod23) \\ ≡1 & (mod23)\end{vmatrix}$.

There are circumstances when a multiplicative inverse does not exist. To this end, we have the following theorem:

> *An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ if and only if $a$ and $n$ are coprime, i.e., $\textrm{gcd}(a,n)=1$.*

For example:

- We saw earlier that $6^{-1} = 4$ modulo $23$. Indeed, $\textrm{gcd}(6,23) = 1$. Moreover, since $n=23$ is prime, *every* integer $1 \leq x \leq 22$ modulo $23$ has a multiplicative inverse. More on that in future lessons.
- In contrast, the integer $a=2$ has no multiplicative inverse modulo $14$ since $\textrm{gcd}(2,14) = 2\neq 1$. Intuitively, multiplying $a = 2$ by any integer $x$ modulo $14$ gives an even number, and taking the result modulo $14$ (also even) will never give us $1$ (an odd integer).

## Determining the Existence of a Multiplicative Inverse

**Example:** Which of the following integers has a multiplicative inverse modulo $25$?

1. $7$
2. $10$
3. $12$

**Explanation**

An integer $a$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $n$ if and only if $a$ and $n$ are coprime, i.e.,

$$
\textrm{gcd}(a,n)=1
$$

With this in mind, let's examine the given options.

- $7$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $25$ since $\textrm{gcd}(7,25)=1. \:\:{\color{darkgreen}\checkmark}$
- $10$ does *not* have a multiplicative inverse modulo $25$ since $\textrm{gcd}(10,25)=5\neq1. \:\:{\color{red}\times}$
- $12$ has a multiplicative inverse modulo $25$ since $\textrm{gcd}(12,25)=1. \:\:{\color{darkgreen}\checkmark}$

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and III only."

## Finding a Multiplicative Inverse Using Trial and Error

Suppose we wish to calculate the multiplicative inverse of $3$ modulo $14$. In other words, we want to find a number $x$ such that

$$
3 \cdot x \equiv 1 \quad (\textrm{mod}\:14)
$$

Firstly, we note that since

$$
\textrm{gcd}(3,14) = 1
$$

a unique multiplicative inverse exists.

Since the modulus $n=14$ is fairly small, one way to efficiently compute the inverse is to use trial and error. To do this, we compute powers of $3$ and take the residue modulo $14$ until we get a result of $1$.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3^{2} & ≡3 \cdot 3≡9 & (mod14) \\ 3^{3} & ≡3 \cdot 3^{2}≡3 \cdot 9≡27≡1 \cdot 14 + 13≡13 & (mod14) \\ 3^{4} & ≡3 \cdot 3^{3}≡3 \cdot 13≡39≡2 \cdot 14 + 11≡11 & (mod14) \\ 3^{5} & ≡3 \cdot 3^{4}≡3 \cdot 11≡33≡2 \cdot 14 + 5≡5 & (mod14) \\ 3^{6} & ≡3 \cdot 3^{5}≡3 \cdot 5≡15≡1 \cdot 14 + 1≡1 & (mod14)\end{bmatrix}
$$

According to the last line, we have
$3 \cdot {\color{magenta}{5}} \equiv 1\qquad (\textrm{mod}\:14)$.

Therefore, the multiplicative inverse of $3$ modulo $14$ is ${\color{magenta}{5}}$, and we can write

$$
3^{-1} = {\color{magenta}{5}}\qquad (\textrm{mod}\:14)
$$

**Note:** We could get the same result by substituting

$$
x=1,2,\ldots, 13
$$

into
$3 \cdot x \equiv 1 \quad (\textrm{mod}\:14)$
until we get the correct congruence relation. However, the exponentiation method described above typically allows us to find the solution with fewer trials since the multiplicative inverse of $a$ (if exists) must appear among the powers of $a$ modulo $n$.

## Computing Inverses Using Exponentiation

**Example:** Use trial and error to compute the multiplicative inverse of $8$ modulo $15$.

**Explanation**

The multiplicative inverse of $8$ modulo $15$, denoted $8^{-1}$, is the number $x$ such that

$$
8 \cdot x \equiv 1 \quad (\textrm{mod}\:15)
$$

Note that since

$$
\text{gcd}(8,15) = 1
$$

an inverse element exists and is unique up to congruence modulo $15$.

Since the modulus $n=15$ is small, we can find the inverse $x$ by computing exponents of $8$ (modulo $15$) until we get a result of $1$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8^{2} & ≡8 \cdot 8≡64≡4 \cdot 15 + 4≡4 & (mod15) \\ 8^{3} & ≡8 \cdot 4≡32≡2 \cdot 15 + 2≡2 & (mod15) \\ 8^{4} & ≡8 \cdot 2≡16≡15 + 1≡1 & (mod15)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $8 \cdot 2 \equiv 1 \, (\textrm{mod} \:15)$. Therefore, the multiplicative inverse of $8$ modulo $15$ is $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}2}$.

## Using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Now, suppose we want to compute the multiplicative inverse of $23$ modulo $79$.

Since

$$
\textrm{gcd}(23,79)=1
$$

the inverse exists. However, the modulus $n=79$ is quite big, so finding the inverse by trial and error is inefficient. So, what do we do?

To answer this, recall that Bézout's identity states that there exist integers $u$ and $v$ such that

$$
23u + 79v = \textrm{gcd}(23,79)
$$

Now, since

$$
\textrm{gcd}(23,79)=1
$$

we have

$$
23u + 79v = 1
$$

We can find the integers $u$ and $v$ using the extended Euclidian algorithm.

Applying the Euclidian algorithm (we'll skip over the details for now), we find that

$$
u=-24, v = 7
$$

Therefore,

$$
23 \cdot (-24) + 7 \cdot 79 = 1
$$

To find the multiplicative inverse of $23$, the key is to write this result modulo $79$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}23 \cdot (-24) + 7 \cdot 79 & ≡1 & (mod79) \\ 23 \cdot (-24) + 7 \cdot 0 & ≡1 & (mod79) \\ 23 \cdot (-24) & ≡1 & (mod79) \\ 23 \cdot (0 - 24) & ≡1 & (mod79) \\ 23 \cdot (79 - 24) & ≡1 & (mod79) \\ 23 \cdot 55 & ≡1 & (mod79)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, we have $23 \cdot 55 \equiv 1 \: (\textrm{mod}\:79)$. Therefore, $55$ is the multiplicative inverse of $23$ (modulo $79$).

Finally, we can express this result using the residue function as follows:

$$
23^{-1} \: \textrm{mod} \: 79 = 55
$$

## Computing Inverses Using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm

**Example:** Use the extended Euclidean algorithm to compute the multiplicative inverse of $15$ modulo $86$.

**Explanation**

First of all, notice that

$$
\textrm{gcd}(15,86)=1
$$

So, the multiplicative inverse of $15$ modulo $86$ exists.

The multiplicative inverse of $15$ modulo $86$ is the number $x$ such that

$$
15\cdot x \equiv 1 \quad (\textrm{mod}\:86)
$$

To find the value of $x$, we will use the extended Euclidean algorithm. First, we apply the forward reduction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
86 &= \begin{vmatrix}15 \cdot 5 & +\end{vmatrix}11 \\
↙\begin{vmatrix}↙ \\ 15 &= \end{vmatrix}11 \cdot 1\begin{vmatrix}+ & 4 \\ ↙ & ↙ \\ 11|= & 4 \cdot 2\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}3 \\ ↙\end{vmatrix}↙ \\
4 &= \begin{vmatrix}3 \cdot 1 & +\end{vmatrix}1
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving for the rightmost terms in the equations above, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
11 &= 86 - 15 \cdot 5 \\
4 &= 15 - 11 \\
3 &= 11 - 4 \cdot 2 \\
1 &= 4 - 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we back-substitute:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= 4 - 3 \\
&= 4 - (11 - 4 \cdot 2) \\
&= 4 \cdot 3 - 11 \\
&= (15 - 11) \cdot 3 - 11 \\
&= 15 \cdot 3 - 11 \cdot 4 \\
&= 15 \cdot 3 - (86 - 15 \cdot 5) \cdot 4 \\
&= 15 \cdot 23 - 86 \cdot 4 \\
&= 15 \cdot 23 + 86 \cdot (-4)
\end{aligned}
$$

We can write this result modulo $86$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}15 \cdot 23 + 86 \cdot (-4) & ≡1 & (mod86) \\ 15 \cdot 23 & ≡1 & (mod86)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, we have $15\cdot 23\equiv 1 \: (\textrm{mod}\:86)$, which means that

$$
15^{-1} \: \textrm{mod} \: 86=\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}23}
$$
