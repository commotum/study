## Introduction

Suppose we want to solve the following linear congruence:
$4x \equiv 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:5)$

Since the modulus $n=5$ is fairly small, we can solve the congruence by trial and error.

Substituting the trial solutions

$$
x = 0,1,2,3,4
$$

into the equation, we obtain the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4 \cdot 0 & ≡0≢2 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 4 \cdot 1 & ≡4≢2 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 4 \cdot 2 & ≡8≡5 + 3≡3≢2 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 4 \cdot 3 & ≡12≡2 \cdot 5 + 2≡2 & (mod5) & ✓ \\ 4 \cdot 4 & ≡16≡3 \cdot 5 + 1≡1≢2 & (mod5) & \cdot\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solution is $x \equiv {\color{blue}{3}} \:(\text{mod}\:5)$.

**Note**: Any number congruent to $3$ modulo $5$ is a solution. For example, note that $8 \equiv 3$, and this also satisfies our congruence:

$$
4x \mid ≡4 \cdot 8\begin{vmatrix}(mod5) \\ ≡32\end{vmatrix}(mod5); ≡6 \cdot 5 + 2\begin{vmatrix}(mod5) \\ ≡0 + 2\end{vmatrix}(mod5); ≡2✓ \mid (mod5)
$$

**Example:** Solve the linear congruence $2x \equiv 1 \:(\text{mod}\:5)$ using trial and error.

**Explanation**

Since the modulus $n=5$ is fairly small, we can solve the congruence by trial and error.

Substituting

$$
x = 0,1,2,3,4
$$

into the equation, we obtain the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 \cdot 0 & ≡0≢1 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 1 & ≡2≢1 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 2 & ≡4≢1 & (mod5) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 3 & ≡6≡5 + 1≡1 & (mod5) & ✓ \\ 2 \cdot 4 & ≡8≡5 + 3≡3≢1 & (mod5) & \cdot\end{bmatrix}

Therefore, the solution is is $x\equiv {\color{blue}{3}} \:(\text{mod}\:5)$.

**Note**: Any number congruent to $3$ modulo $5$ is a solution. For example, note that $8\equiv 3$, and this also satisfies our congruence:

$$

$$

## Using the Multiplicative Inverse

Now, let's consider the following linear congruence:

$$
4x \equiv 5 \qquad (\text{mod}\:21)
$$

We can solve this congruence by multiplying both sides by the *multiplicative inverse* of $4$ modulo $21$.

In this case, we can find the multiplicative inverse by raising $4$ to positive integer exponents until we get an answer of $1{:}$

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4^{2} & ≡16 & (mod21) \\ 4^{3} & ≡4 \cdot 16≡64≡3 \cdot 21 + 1≡3 \cdot 0 + 1≡1 & (mod21)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $4 \cdot {\color{blue}{16}} \equiv 1 \:(\textrm{mod}\:21)$, which means that $16$ is the multiplicative inverse of $4$ (modulo $21$).

Now, multiplying both sides of our congruence by $16$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4x & ≡5 & (mod21) \\ 16 \cdot 4x & ≡16 \cdot 5 & (mod21) \\ (16 \cdot 4)x & ≡80 & (mod21) \\ 1 \cdot x & ≡3 \cdot 21 + 17 & (mod21) \\ x & ≡17 & (mod21)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solution is $x \equiv 17 \: (\text{mod}\:21)$.

<a id="uniqueness-of-solutions"></a>
## Uniqueness of Solutions

Not every linear congruence has a unique solution!

To determine whether a linear congruence has a single unique solution, we can use the following rule:

> *A linear congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ has a unique solution modulo $n$ if and only if $\text{gcd}(a,n)=1$.*

We'll prove this theorem at the end of the lesson.

So, whenever we solve a linear congruence, we should first use the above rule to check that there is a unique solution. In the linear congruence $4x \equiv 5 \:(\text{mod}\:21)$, we had $a=4$ and $n=21$. Therefore,

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(4,21)=1
$$

which means that our congruence does indeed have a unique solution.

In a future lesson, we'll discuss cases where linear congruences have multiple or no solutions.

## Solving Congruences Using Multiplicative Inverses

**Example:** Solve the modular equation $7x \equiv 3 \:(\text{mod}\:15)$.

*Hint: You may wish to use trial and error to find the appropriate multiplicative inverse.*

**Explanation**

Recall that a linear congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ has a unique solution modulo $n$ if and only if

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=1
$$

In the given congruence, we have $a=7$ and $n=15$, so

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(7,15)=1
$$

This means our congruence does indeed have a unique solution.

To find the solution, we need to multiply both sides of the equation by the multiplicative inverse of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:15)$. To find the inverse of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:15)$, we compute the exponents of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:15)$ until we get a result of $1 \,(\textrm{mod}\:15)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7^{2} & ≡49≡3 \cdot 15 + 4≡4 & (mod15) \\ 7^{3} & ≡4 \cdot 7≡28≡15 + 13≡13 & (mod15) \\ 7^{4} & ≡13 \cdot 7≡91≡6 \cdot 15 + 1≡1 & (mod15)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $13 \cdot 7 \equiv 1 \:(\textrm{mod}\:15)$, which means that $13$ is the multiplicative inverse of $7$ (modulo $15$).

Now, multiplying both sides of our congruence by $13$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7x & ≡3 & (mod15) \\ 13 \cdot 7x & ≡13 \cdot 3 & (mod15) \\ (13 \cdot 7)x & ≡39 & (mod15) \\ 1 \cdot x & ≡2 \cdot 15 + 9 & (mod15) \\ x & ≡9 & (mod15)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solution is $x\equiv 9 \: (\text{mod}\:15)$.

## Solving Congruences Using Additive and Multiplicative Inverses

**Example:** Solve the following congruence:
$7x - 11 \equiv 29 \quad (\text{mod}\:19)$

*Hint: You may wish to use trial and error to find the appropriate multiplicative inverse.*

**Explanation**

First, we reduce our congruence to a congruence of the form $ax\equiv b$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7x - 11 & ≡29 & (mod19) \\ 7x - 11 + 11 & ≡29 + 11 & (mod19) \\ 7x & ≡40 & (mod19) \\ 7x & ≡38 + 2 & (mod19) \\ 7x & ≡2 \cdot 19 + 2 & (mod19) \\ 7x & ≡2 & (mod19)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Recall that a linear congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ has a unique solution modulo $n$ if and only if

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=1
$$

In the given congruence, we have $a=7$ and $n=19$, and

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(7,19)=1
$$

So, our congruence does indeed have a unique solution.

To find the solution, we need to multiply both sides of the equation by the multiplicative inverse of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:19)$. To find the inverse of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:19)$, we compute the exponents of $7\,(\textrm{mod}\:19)$ until we get a result of $1 \,(\textrm{mod}\:19)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7^{2} & ≡49≡2 \cdot 19 + 11≡11 & (mod19) \\ 7^{3} & ≡11 \cdot 7≡77≡4 \cdot 19 + 1≡1 & (mod19)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $11 \cdot 7 \equiv 1 \:(\textrm{mod}\:19)$, which means that $11$ is the multiplicative inverse of $7$ (modulo $19$).

Now, multiplying both sides of our congruence by $11$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}7x & ≡2 & (mod19) \\ 11 \cdot 7x & ≡11 \cdot 2 & (mod19) \\ (11 \cdot 7)x & ≡22 & (mod19) \\ 1 \cdot x & ≡19 + 3 & (mod19) \\ x & ≡3 & (mod19)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solution is $x \equiv 3 \: (\text{mod}\:19)$.

## Solving Congruences Using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm

**Example:** Solve the congruence $15x \equiv 17 \:(\text{mod}\:44)$.

**Explanation**

Recall that a linear congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ has a unique solution modulo $n$ if and only if

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=1
$$

In the given congruence, we have $a=15$ and $n=44$, where

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(15,44)=1
$$

So, our congruence does indeed have a unique solution.

To find the solution, we need to multiply both sides of the equation by the multiplicative inverse of $15\,(\textrm{mod}\:44)$. To find the inverse of $15\,(\textrm{mod}\:44)$, we use the extended Euclidean algorithm.

First, we apply the forward reduction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
44 &= \begin{vmatrix}15 \cdot 2 & +\end{vmatrix}14 \\
↙\begin{vmatrix}↙ \\ 15 &= \end{vmatrix}14 \cdot 1 \mid + \mid 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving for the rightmost terms in the equations above, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
14 &= 44 - 15 \cdot 2 \\
1 &= 15 - 14
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we back-substitute:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= 15 - 14 \\
&= 15 - (44 - 15 \cdot 2) \\
&= 15 \cdot 3 - 44
\end{aligned}
$$

We can write this result in modulo $44$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}15 \cdot 3 - 44 & ≡1 & (mod44) \\ 15 \cdot 3 & ≡1 & (mod44)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $15 \cdot 3 \equiv 1 \:(\textrm{mod}\:44)$, which means that $3$ is the multiplicative inverse of $15$ (modulo $44$).

Now, multiplying both sides of our original congruence by $3$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}15x & ≡17 & (mod44) \\ 3 \cdot 15x & ≡3 \cdot 17 & (mod44) \\ (3 \cdot 15)x & ≡51 & (mod44) \\ 1 \cdot x & ≡44 + 7 & (mod44) \\ x & ≡7 & (mod44)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solution is $x \equiv 7 \: (\text{mod}\:44)$.

<a id="proof-of-the-theorem"></a>
## Proof of the Theorem

Let's prove the following theorem:

> *Let $a$, $b$, and $n$ integers. If $\text{gcd}(a,n)=1$, then the linear congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ has a unique solution.*

**Proof**.

To prove this theorem, we must show that a solution exists and that it is unique.

First, we prove that a solution exists:

- If $\text{gcd}(a,n)=1$, then by Bézout's identity, there exist integers $u$ and $v$ such that
$au+nv=1$.
We can write this result in modulo $n$ as follows:
$\begin{bmatrix}au + nv & ≡1 & (modn) \\ au & ≡1 & (modn)\end{bmatrix}$
This means that $u$ is the multiplicative inverse of $a$ (modulo $n$).So, multiplying both sides of the original congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n)$ by $u$, we get
$\begin{bmatrix}ax & ≡b & (modn) \\ u \cdot ax & ≡u \cdot b & (modn) \\ (u \cdot a)x & ≡ub & (modn) \\ 1 \cdot x & ≡ub & (modn) \\ x & ≡ub & (modn)\end{bmatrix}$
Therefore, $x \equiv ub \: (\text{mod}\:n)$ is a solution.

Next, we prove the solution is unique:

- To prove uniqueness, we show that two solutions $x_1$ and $x_2$ to our congruence must be congruent. Suppose $x_1$ and $x_2$ are two solutions to the congruence, i.e., $ax_1 \equiv b\: (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$ and $ax_2 \equiv b\: (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$. We want to show that $x_1 \equiv x_2$.Since $ax_1 \equiv b\: (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$ and $ax_2 \equiv b\: (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$, we have
$ax_1 \equiv ax_2 \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$.Now, since $\text{gcd}(a,n) = 1$, we can multiply both sides by the modular inverse of $a$ modulo $n$, which we previously denoted $u$.
$(u \cdot a) x_1 \equiv (u \cdot a) x_2 \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$Since $a$ and $n$ are relatively prime, $u$ exists. Therefore, $u \cdot a \equiv 1\:(\textrm{mod}\,{n})$, and we have
$x_1 \equiv x_2 \qquad (\textrm{mod}\,{n})$,
as required.

Thus, we have shown both the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the congruence $ax \equiv b \pmod{n}$ when

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n) = 1
$$
