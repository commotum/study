## Introduction

Not every linear congruence has precisely one solution. Some linear congruences have no solutions, while others have multiple solutions.

For example, consider the following linear congruence:

$$
3x \equiv 5 \qquad (\text{mod}\:6)
$$

Since the modulus $n=6$ is fairly small, we can try to solve this congruence by trial and error.

Substituting the trial solutions

$$
x = 0,1,2,3,4,5
$$

into the congruence, we obtain the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 0 & ≡0≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 3 \cdot 1 & ≡3≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 3 \cdot 2 & ≡0≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 3 \cdot 3 & ≡3≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 3 \cdot 4 & ≡0≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 3 \cdot 5 & ≡3≢5 & (mod6) & \cdot\end{bmatrix}
$$

As we can see from the list above, the congruence

$$
3x = 5 \: (\text{mod} \, 6)
$$

has no solution.

Let's see some more examples.

## Solving by Trial and Error

**Example:** Use trial and error to find the solutions, if any, to the congruence $2x \equiv 4 \:(\text{mod}\:6)$.

**Explanation**

Since the modulus $n=6$ is fairly small, we can solve the congruence by trial and error.

Substituting

$$
x = 0,1,2,3,4,5
$$

into the equation, we obtain the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 \cdot 0 & ≡0≢4 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 1 & ≡2≢4 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 2 & ≡4 & (mod6) & ✓ \\ 2 \cdot 3 & ≡0≢4 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 4 & ≡2≢4 & (mod6) & \cdot \\ 2 \cdot 5 & ≡4 & (mod6) & ✓\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x\equiv 2, 5$ only $(\textrm{mod}\:6)$.

<a id="determining-the-number-of-solutions-of-a-linear-congruence"></a>
## Determining the Number of Solutions of a Linear Congruence

The number of solutions to a modular equation
$ax \equiv b \qquad (\text{mod}\:n)$

all depends on $\textrm{gcd}(a,n)$:

- If $\textrm{gcd}(a,n) \not\mid b$, then the congruence has no
solutions.
- If $\textrm{gcd}(a,n) \: \mid \:b$, then the congruence has exactly $\textrm{gcd}(a,n)$ solutions in the interval $0 \leq x \leq n-1$.

We can summarize these rules using a flow chart, as shown below.

![](<../Source/6.2.4. Solving Advanced Linear Congruences/Images/2d4664596db13b88799c5a1379f2e15d.png>)

For example, consider the linear congruence

$$
6x \equiv 9 \qquad (\text{mod}\:15)
$$

In this congruence, we have $a=6$ and $n=15$, and their greatest common divisor is

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(6,15)=3
$$

Since $b=9$ is also divisible by $3$, we conclude that our congruence has $3$ solutions in the interval

$$
0 \leq x \leq 14
$$

We can easily verify that the three solutions are $4, 9$, and $14$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}6 \cdot 4 & ≡24≡9 & (mod15) & ✓ \\ 6 \cdot 9 & ≡54≡9 & (mod15) & ✓ \\ 6 \cdot 14 & ≡84≡9 & (mod15) & ✓\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="identifying-linear-congruences-with-no-solutions-and-many-solutions"></a>
## Identifying Linear Congruences With No Solutions and Many Solutions

**Example:** Which of the following congruences have *precisely two* solutions modulo $16$?

1. $6x \equiv 14 \quad (\text{mod}\:16)$
2. $4x \equiv 8 \quad (\text{mod}\:16)$
3. $2x \equiv 9 \quad (\text{mod}\:16)$

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/6.2.4. Solving Advanced Linear Congruences/Images/907f1ed6ee970d5bd8efc16b6eaff963.png>)

Recall the following facts regarding the congruence $ax \equiv b \:(\text{mod}\:n){:}$

- The congruence has no solutions modulo $n$ if and only if
$\text{gcd}(a,n) \!\not{\mid}\:b$.
- The congruence has $\textrm{gcd}(a,n)$ solutions modulo $n$ if and only if
$\text{gcd}(a,n) \:{\mid}\:b$.

With that in mind, let's examine our congruences.

- Congruence I has two solutions modulo $16$. We have $a=6$ and $n=16$, and
$\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(6,16)=2$.
Since $b=14$ is divisible by $2$, this congruence has $2$ solutions.
- Congruence II has four solutions modulo $16$. We have $a=4$ and $n=16$, and
$\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(4,16)=4$.
Since $b=8$ is divisible by $4$, this congruence has $4$ solutions.
- Congruence III has no solutions. We have $a=2$ and $n=16$, and
$\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(2,16)=2$.
Since $b=9$ is not divisible by $2$, this congruence has no solutions.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only".

<a id="solving-linear-congruences-using-the-division-property"></a>
## Solving Linear Congruences Using the Division Property

Consider the following congruence:

$$
ax \equiv b \qquad (\text{mod}\:n)
$$

In cases where $\textrm{gcd}(a,n) > 1$ and $\textrm{gcd}(a,n) \mid b$, it's possible to reduce the congruence to a simpler congruence with a unique solution. However, the solution to the simpler congruence is only *part* of the solution to the original congruence. An extra step is needed to find the complete solution.

To demonstrate, let's find all the solutions to the following congruence:

$$
2x \equiv 6 \qquad (\text{mod}\:10)
$$

Here, we have $a=2$ and $n=10$, so

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(2,10)=2
$$

Since $b=6$ is divisible by $2$, our congruence has $2$ solutions modulo $10$.

Notice that we can simplify the congruence using the division property of congruences:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2x & ≡6 & (mod10) \\ 2 \cdot x & ≡2 \cdot 3 & (mod2 \cdot 5) \\ 2 \cdot x & ≡2 \cdot 3 & (mod2 \cdot 5) \\ x & ≡3 & (mod5)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus, our simplified congruence has the unique solution, $x \equiv {\color{blue}3} \: (\text{mod}\:{\color{red}5})$.

But remember, we expect $2$ solutions modulo $10$. To find all the solutions, we repeatedly add the reduced modulus (${\color{red}5}$) to our reduced solution (${\color{blue}3}$) modulo $10$ until we reach our reduced solution (${\color{blue}3}$) again:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & ≡3 & (mod10) \\ x_{2} & ≡3 + 5≡8 & (mod10) \\ x_{3} & ≡8 + 5≡13≡10 + 3≡3 & (mod10)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x\equiv {\color{blue}3} \: (\text{mod}\:10)$ and $x\equiv {\color{purple}{8}} \: (\text{mod}\:10)$.

<a id="solving-linear-congruences-with-many-solutions"></a>
## Solving Linear Congruences With Many Solutions

**Example:** Find the sum modulo $20$ of the solutions to the congruence $8x \equiv 4 \:(\text{mod}\:20)$.

*Hint: You may wish to use trial and error to find the appropriate multiplicative inverse.*

**Explanation**

In the given congruence, we have $a=8$ and $n=20$, so

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(8,20)=4
$$

Since $b=4$ is divisible by $4$, our congruence has $4$ solutions.

First, we reduce the congruence using the division property of congruences:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}8x & ≡4 & (mod20) \\ 4 \cdot 2x & ≡4 \cdot 1 & (mod4 \cdot 5) \\ 4 \cdot 2x & ≡4 \cdot 1 & (mod4 \cdot 5) \\ 2x & ≡1 & (mod5)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice that in the new congruence, we have $a_1=2$ and $n_1=5$, so

$$
\text{gcd}(a_1,n_1)=\text{gcd}(2,5)=1
$$

This means our congruence has a unique solution modulo $5$.

To find the solution, we need to multiply both sides of the equation by the multiplicative inverse of $2\,(\textrm{mod}\:5)$. To find the inverse of $2\,(\textrm{mod}\:5)$, we compute the exponents of $2\,(\textrm{mod}\:5)$ until we get a result of $1 \,(\textrm{mod}\:5)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2^{2} & ≡4 & (mod5) \\ 2^{3} & ≡4 \cdot 2≡8≡1 \cdot 5 + 3≡3 & (mod5) \\ 2^{4} & ≡3 \cdot 2≡6≡1 \cdot 5 + 1≡1 & (mod5)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 1 \:(\textrm{mod}\:5)$, which means that $3$ is the multiplicative inverse of $2$ (modulo $5$).

Now, multiplying both sides of our congruence by $3$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2x & ≡1 & (mod5) \\ 3 \cdot 2x & ≡3 \cdot 1 & (mod5) \\ (3 \cdot 2)x & ≡3 & (mod5) \\ x & ≡3 & (mod5)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, we get one solution, $x \equiv 3 \: (\text{mod}\:5)$.

But our original congruence was modulo $20$ (not modulo $5$). To find all the solutions modulo $20$, we repeatedly add $5$ modulo $20$ until we reach the number $3$ again:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & ≡3 & (mod20) \\ x_{2} & ≡3 + 5≡8 & (mod20) \\ x_{3} & ≡8 + 5≡13 & (mod20) \\ x_{4} & ≡13 + 5≡18 & (mod20) \\ x_{5} & ≡18 + 5≡23≡1 \cdot 20 + 3≡3≡x_{1} & (mod20)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x\equiv 3, 8, 13, 18 \: (\text{mod}\:20)$.

Finally,
$3+8+13+18 \equiv 2 \quad (\text{mod}\:20)$.

<a id="using-the-extended-euclidean-algorithm"></a>
## Using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm

**Example:** Find the solutions of $50x \equiv 120 \:(\text{mod}\:298)$.

*Hint: You may use the fact that $\text{gcd}(50,298) =2$.*

**Explanation**

In the given congruence, we have $a=50$ and $n=298$, and we're given that

$$
\text{gcd}(a,n)=\text{gcd}(50,298)=2
$$

Since $b=120$ is divisible by $2$, our congruence has $2$ solutions.

Next, we use the division property of congruences:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}50x & ≡120 & (mod298) \\ 2 \cdot 25x & ≡2 \cdot 60 & (mod2 \cdot 149) \\ 2 \cdot 25x & ≡2 \cdot 60 & (mod2 \cdot 149) \\ 25x & ≡60 & (mod149)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice that in this new congruence, we have $a_1=25$ and $n_1=149$, where

$$
\text{gcd}(a_1,n_1)=\text{gcd}(25,149)=1
$$

So, our congruence has a unique solution modulo $149$.

To find the solution, we must multiply both sides of the equation by the multiplicative inverse of $25\,(\textrm{mod}\:149)$. To find the inverse of $25\,(\textrm{mod}\:149)$, we apply the extended Euclidean algorithm.

First, we apply the forward reduction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
149 &= \begin{vmatrix}25 \cdot 5 & + & 24 & ⇔ & 24|=\end{vmatrix}149 - 25 \cdot 5 \\
↙\begin{vmatrix}↙ \\ 25 &= \end{vmatrix}24 \cdot 1\begin{vmatrix}+ & 1 & ⇔ & 1|=\end{vmatrix}25 - 24 \cdot 1 \\
↙\begin{vmatrix}↙ \\ 24 &= \end{vmatrix}24 \cdot 1\begin{vmatrix}+ & 0 & ⇔ & 0|=\end{vmatrix}14 - 14 \cdot 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, back-substituting in the usual way gives the following:

$$
1 = 6\cdot 25 -149
$$

We can write this result in modulo $149$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}6 \cdot 25 - 149 & ≡1 & (mod149) \\ 6 \cdot 25 & ≡1 & (mod149)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the inverse of $25$ modulo $149$ is $6$.

Now, multiplying both sides of our original congruence by $6$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}25x & ≡60 & (mod149) \\ 6 \cdot 25x & ≡6 \cdot 60 & (mod149) \\ x & ≡360 & (mod149) \\ x & ≡149 \cdot 2 + 62 & (mod149) \\ x & ≡62 & (mod149)\end{bmatrix}
$$

So, we get the solution $x \equiv 62 \: (\text{mod}\:149)$.

But our original congruence was modulo $298$ (not modulo $149$). To find all the solutions modulo $298$, we repeatedly add $149$ to $62$ modulo $298$ until we reach the number $62$ again:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & ≡62 & (mod298) \\ x_{2} & ≡62 + 149≡211 & (mod298) \\ x_{3} & ≡211 + 149≡360≡149 \cdot 2 + 62≡x_{1} & (mod298)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
x_{1}=62\: (\text{mod}\:298) \quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}= 211 \: (\text{mod}\:298)
$$
