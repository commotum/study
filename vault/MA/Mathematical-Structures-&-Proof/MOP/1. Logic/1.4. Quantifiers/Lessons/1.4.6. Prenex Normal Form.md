## Introduction

We have the following formulas that allow us to "factor out" quantifiers to the front of a logical expression:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∀xP(x)∧Q & ≡∀x(P(x)∧Q) \\ ∀xP(x)∨Q & ≡∀x(P(x)∨Q) \\ ∃xP(x)∧Q & ≡∃x(P(x)∧Q) \\ ∃xP(x)∨Q & ≡∃x(P(x)∨Q)\end{bmatrix}
$$

In these formulas, note that

- $P(x)$ is a predicate that depends on $x$ and is within the scope of the quantifier,
- $Q$ is a predicate that does not depend on $x$.

Note that both $P$ and $Q$ can include variables other than $x$, but we've omitted them to simplify these expressions.

Moving all the quantifiers to the front of a logical expression, we obtain a so-called **prenex normal form**. Mathematical expressions involving quantifiers are often written in this form.

For example, let's write the following statement in prenex normal form.

$$
\exists m \in \mathbb{Z} \: (m > 1) \Rightarrow \forall n \in \mathbb{N} \: (10 \mid n)
$$

To write this expression in prenex normal form, we need to move all the quantifiers to the front of the expression:

First, let's introduce the following notation:

- $P(m){:} \: m > 1$
- $Q(n){:} \: 10 \mid n$

Then, our statement can be written as

$$
\exists m \, P(m) \Rightarrow \forall n \, Q(n)
$$

By eliminating the implication, we obtain

$$
∃mP(m)⇒∀nQ(n) \mid ≡¬(∃mP(m))∨∀nQ(n); ≡∀m¬P(m)∨∀nQ(n)
$$

Now, since $P(m)$ does not depend on $n$ and $Q(n)$ does not depend on $m$, we can move the quantifiers to the front, as follows:

$$
∀m¬P(m)∨∀nQ(n) \mid ≡∀m(¬P(m)∨∀nQ(n)); ≡∀m∀n(¬P(m)∨Q(n))
$$

The final expression gives us a prenex normal form:

$$
\forall m \in \mathbb{Z}, \, \forall n \in \mathbb{N} \, (m \leq 1 \lor 10 \mid n)
$$

<a id="when-both-predicates-depend-on-the-same-variable"></a>
## When Both Predicates Depend on the Same Variable

If our predicates depend on the variable used with a quantifier, we have the following two formulas:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∀xP(x)∧∀xQ(x) & ≡∀x(P(x)∧Q(x)) \\ ∃xP(x)∨∃xQ(x) & ≡∃x(P(x)∨Q(x))\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Watch out!** What about $\forall \lor \forall$ and $\exists \land \exists$? It turns out that, in general:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∀xP(x)∨∀xQ(x) & ≢∀x(P(x)∨Q(x)) \\ ∃xP(x)∧∃xQ(x) & ≢∃x(P(x)∧Q(x))\end{bmatrix}
$$

To see why the $\forall \lor \forall$ case does not hold, consider the following predicates defined over $\mathbb{Z}{:}$

- $P(x){:}$ $x$ is odd
- $Q(x){:}$ $x$ is even

Then, we have

$$
∀xP(x)∨∀xQ(x) \mid ≡(∀x ∈ Z, [math]x[/math] is odd)∨(∀x ∈ Z, [math]x[/math] is even); = false∨false; = false
$$

Indeed, not all integers are odd, and not all integers are even. Also, the disjunction of two false statements is false.

On the other hand,

$$
∀x(P(x)∨Q(x)) \mid ≡∀x ∈ Z([math]x[/math] is odd∨[math]x[/math] is even); = true
$$

Indeed, any integer is either even or odd. This statement is true.

Therefore, $\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x) \not\equiv \forall x \, (P(x) \lor Q(x))$.

The proof that $\exists x \, P(x) \land \exists x \, Q(x) \not\equiv \exists x \, (P(x) \land Q(x))$ is similar (in fact, we can use the same counterexample).

Remember that two logical formulas are equivalent if they give the same truth values "for any interpretation," meaning that the left-hand side must be true *if and only if* the right-hand side is true given any possible predicates and universal sets.

## Identifying Equivalent Formulas

**Example:** Complete the missing entries in the following statements:

1. $\forall x \, P(x) \land Q \,\, \bbox[2pt, border: 1pt solid black]{\phantom{AA}}\,\, \forall x \, (P(x) \land Q)$
2. $P \land \forall x \, Q(x) \,\,\bbox[2pt, border: 1pt solid black]{\phantom{AA}} \,\,\forall x \, (P\land Q(x))$
3. $\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x) \,\,\bbox[2pt, border: 1pt solid black]{\phantom{AA}} \,\, \forall x \, (P(x) \lor Q(x))$

**Explanation**

Recall that two formulas are equivalent if they have the same truth values for all possible concrete predicates.

Let's examine our formulas.

- We can move the quantifier to the beginning of the formula since the second predicate does not depend on $x{:}$
$\forall x \, P(x) \land Q \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\equiv} \forall x \, (P(x) \land Q)$
- We can move the quantifier to the beginning of the formula since the first predicate does not depend on $x{:}$
$P \land \forall x \, Q(x) \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\equiv} \forall x \, (P\land Q(x))$
- We can't move a universal quantifier to the beginning of the formula in a disjunction:
$\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x) \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}\not\equiv} \forall x \, (P(x) \lor Q(x))$

## Finding a Prenex Normal Form When the Predicates Have No Common Variables

**Example:** Re-write the following statement by moving all the quantifiers to the front of the expression.

$\qquad$ $\forall x \in \mathbb{R} \, (e^x \gt 0) \Rightarrow \exists y \in \mathbb{R}_{0}^{+} \, (\ln{y} = 1)$.

**Explanation**

Let's denote:

- $P(x)$: $e^x \gt 0$
- $Q(y)$: $\ln{y} = 1$

Then, our statement can be written as
$\forall x \, P(x) \Rightarrow \exists y \, Q(y)$.

By eliminating implication, we obtain
$∀xP(x)⇒∃yQ(y) \mid ≡¬(∀xP(x))∨∃yQ(y); ≡∃x¬P(x)∨∃yQ(y)$.

Now, since $P(x)$ does not depend on $y$ and $Q(y)$ does not depend on $x$, we can move the quantifiers to the front, as follows:
$∃x¬P(x)∨∃yQ(y) \mid ≡∃x(¬P(x)∨∃yQ(y)); ≡∃x∃y(¬P(x)∨Q(y))$

The final expression reads

$\qquad$

$$
\exists x \in \mathbb{R}, \, \exists y \in \mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}, \, (e^{x} \leq 0 \lor \ln{y} = 1)
$$

<a id="renaming-of-bound-variables"></a>
## Renaming of Bound Variables

Let's find a prenex normal form of the following expression:

$$
x
$$

Denoting

- $P(x){:} \: 4 \mid x$,
- $x$

our statement can be written as

$$
\exists x \, P(x) \land \exists x \, Q(x)
$$

We can't "factor out" the quantifier since, in general,

$$
\exists x \, P(x) \land \exists x \, Q(x) \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}\not\equiv}\exists x \, (P(x) \land Q(x))
$$

However, there is a workaround. If a variable is bound, we can't substitute anything into it. So, we can change any such variables in one of the predicates to some other variable. For example, let's change $x$ to $y$ in the second predicate, as follows:

$$
\exists x \, P(x) \land \exists x \, Q(x) \equiv \exists x \, P(x) \land \exists \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}y} \, Q(\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}y})
$$

Now, since $P(x)$ does not depend on $y$ and $Q(y)$ does not depend on $x$, we can move the quantifiers to the front, as follows:

$$
\exists x \, P(x) \land \exists y \, Q(y) \equiv \exists x \, \exists y \, (P(x) \land Q(y))
$$

The final expression reads

$$
y
$$

So, we get our prenex normal form, but we now have two variables instead of one. Finally, notice that the prenex form is not unique since we could change other occurrences of $x$, or use $t$ instead of $y$ for the new variable, etc. As a result, we can obtain slightly different resulting expressions.

<a id="finding-a-prenex-normal-form-when-the-predicates-have-common-variables"></a>
## Finding a Prenex Normal Form When the Predicates Have Common Variables

**Example:** Re-write the following statement by moving all the quantifiers to the front of the expression.

$\qquad$ $\exists x \in \mathbb{Q} \, (x^2<2) \Rightarrow \forall x \in \mathbb{Q} \, (x^3 \neq 3)$.

**Explanation**

Let's denote:

- $P(x):\,x^2<2$
- $Q(x):\,x^3 \neq 3$

Then, our statement can be written as

$$
\exists x \, P(x) \Rightarrow \forall x \, Q(x)
$$

By eliminating implication, we obtain

$$
∃xP(x)⇒∀xQ(x) \mid ≡¬(∃xP(x))∨∀xQ(x); ≡∀x¬P(x)∨∀xQ(x)
$$

We can't "factor out" the quantifier since, in general,

$$
\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x) \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{red}\not\equiv} \forall x \, (P(x) \lor Q(x))
$$

However, we can change the variable in one of the predicates. For example, in the second one:

$$
\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x) \equiv \forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}y} \, Q(\bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}y})
$$

Now, since $P(x)$ does not depend on $y$ and $Q(y)$ does not depend on $x$, we can move the quantifiers to the front, as follows:

$$
\forall x \, \neg P(x) \lor \forall y \, Q(y) \equiv \forall x \, \forall y \, (\neg P(x) \lor Q(y))
$$

The final expression reads

$\qquad$

$$
\forall x,y \in \mathbb{Q} \, (x^2\geq 2 \lor y^3 \neq 3)
$$
