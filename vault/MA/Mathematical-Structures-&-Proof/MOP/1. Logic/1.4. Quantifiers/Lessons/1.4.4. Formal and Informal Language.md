## Introduction

In day-to-day life, we use informal language that is linguistically more expressive but may lack the rigor needed in formal mathematics.

For instance, consider the following example of a sentence in informal language:

> *The weather is fine.*

Notice that this statement is a bit vague:

- The weather is fine where? It could be fine in some places and not so much in others.
- What is meant by "fine"? Some people like sunny weather, while others prefer less hot, cloudy days.

In contrast, formal language, as seen in mathematical proofs, follows strict syntax (rules for constructing sentences) and semantics (rules for interpreting sentences).

In formal language, statements are sometimes expressed symbolically using the logical quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$. For example:

> $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n^2 \geq 0$.

In this lesson, we will practice translating from plain English to formal mathematical expressions.

For example, let's express the statement below in formal language:

> *Every real number is either positive or negative*.

Let's examine our statement:

- The word "every" indicates we should use a universal quantifier, $\forall$.
- The sentence refers to real numbers. So, by denoting a real number as $x$, we have that
$x \in \mathbb{R}$.
- Finally, the condition in the statement can be written as
$x > 0 \: \textrm{or} \: x < 0$.

Therefore, we write the statement as follows:

$$
\forall x \in \mathbb{R}, \, x > 0 \lor \: x < 0
$$

**Note:** We can use $\lor$ (disjunction) instead of "or". Also, this statement is false (since $0$ is also a real number), though this does not affect the translation process.

## Expressing a Plain English Sentence as a Formal Statement

**Example:** Express the following statement in formal language:

> There is a prime $p$ that is even.

**Explanation**

Let's examine our statement:

- The words "there is" indicate we use an existential quantifier, $\exists$.
- The sentence refers to the prime numbers. So, by denoting a prime number as $p$, we have that
$p$
- Finally, the condition in the statement can be written as
$p \textrm{is even.}$

Therefore, we write the statement as follows:

> $\exists$ $p$ prime, $p$ is even.

We could also write

> $\exists$ $p$ prime, $2\mid p$.

## Expressing a Plain English Sentence With Two Quantifiers as a Formal Statement

**Example:** Express the following statement in formal language.

> For each natural number, there is an integer such that their sum is $2$.

**Explanation**

Let's examine our statement:

- The word "each" indicates that we use a universal quantifier, $\forall$.
- The words "there is" indicate that we use an existential quantifier, $\exists$.
- The sentence refers to a natural number and an integer number. So, by denoting our numbers as $n$ and $k$, respectively, we have
$\forall \, n \in \mathbb{N}, \, \exists \, k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
- Finally, the condition in the statement can be written as follows:
$n + k = 2$

Therefore, we write the statement as follows:

> $\forall \, n \in \mathbb{N}, \, \exists \, k \in \mathbb{Z}, \, n + k = 2$

## Implicit Quantification

If a sentence contains a variable but does not have any quantifiers, it's *implicitly* implied that there must be a universal quantifier:

$$
P(x) \: \equiv \: \forall x \, P(x)
$$

where the domain of $x$ is usually clear from the context.

For example, given that $x$ is an integer, the sentence

> *If $6 \mid x$, then $3 \mid x$*

is equivalent to

$$
\forall x \in \mathbb{Z}, \, (6 \mid x) \Rightarrow (3 \mid x)
$$

Let's now consider an example of an *implicit* existential quantifier.

> *The square root of $5$ is a real number.*

Notice that this sentence does not contain any variables. However, it can be re-phased as follows:

> *There exists a real number $x$ such that $\sqrt{5}=x$.*

We can also write this as:

> *There exists a real number $x$ such that $x^2=5$.*

In formal language, we write this as follows:

$$
\exists x \in \mathbb{R}, \, x^2=5
$$

<a id="identifying-implicit-quantification"></a>
## Identifying Implicit Quantification

**Example:** Express the following sentence in formal language.

> The square of a natural number is also a natural number.

**Explanation**

Notice that our sentence can be re-phrased as:

> *For any natural number $n$, we have that $n^2$ is also a natural number.*

Now, "$n^2$ is also a natural number" means:

> *There exists a natural number $m$ such that $n^2 = m$.*

Therefore, we get the following statement:

> *For any natural number $n$, there exists another natural number $m$ such that $n^2 = m$.*

More formally, this reads as

$$
\forall \, n \in \mathbb{N}, \, \exists \, m \in \mathbb{N}, \, n^2 = m
$$
