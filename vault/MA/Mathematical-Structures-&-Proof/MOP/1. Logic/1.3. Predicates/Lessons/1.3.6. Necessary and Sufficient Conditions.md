## Introduction

Consider the truth table for $S \Rightarrow N$ below. Note that the implication is true $({\color{blue}\textrm{T}})$ in the $1$st, $3$rd, and $4$th rows.

| $S$ | $N$ | $S \Rightarrow N$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $\color{red}\textrm T$ | $\color{red}\textrm T$ | $\color{blue}\textrm T$ |
| $\textrm T$ | $\textrm F$ | $\textrm F$ |
| $\textrm F$ | $\textrm T$ | $\color{blue}\textrm T$ |
| $\textrm F$ | $\textrm F$ | $\color{blue}\textrm T$ |

Of the three rows where the implication is true, $S$ is true $({\color{red}\textrm{T}})$ only in the $1$st row, and $N$ is also true $({\color{red}\textrm{T}})$ in this row.

So, if the implication is true, $S$ being true *guarantees* that $N$ must also be true. Therefore, $S$ is called a **sufficient condition** for $N$.

On the other hand, if $S$ is true, it is *necessary* that $N$ is also true. Therefore, $N$ is called a **necessary condition** for $S$.
However, $N$ being true *does not guarantee* that $S$ is true, as we can see from the $3$rd row in the truth table.

These ideas can be summarized as follows:

$$
\textbf{S}\textrm{ufficient} \: \Rightarrow \: \textbf{N}\textrm{ecessary}
$$

Let's look at some examples:

- Consider the following true implication:
$a > 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad a^2 > 0$
We can label each part of the statement as follows:
$\underbrace{\big(\, a > 0 \, \big)}_{\large\textrm{sufficient}} \: \Rightarrow \: \underbrace{\big(\, a^2 > 0 \, \big)}_{\large\textrm{necessary}}$
Therefore:$a > 0$ is a *sufficient* condition for $a^2 > 0$. In other words, $a > 0$ *guarantees* that $a^2 > 0$.$a^2 > 0$ is a *necessary* condition for $a > 0$. In other words, if $a > 0$, it is necessary that $a^2 > 0$. However, $a^2 > 0$ *does not guarantee* that $a > 0$.
- Suppose your teacher promises that *if* you score at least $90$ on the exam, *then* you will receive an $A$. We can express this promise as the following implication:
$90$
Let's assume the teacher is telling the truth, so the implication is true. We can label each part of our implication as follows:
$90$
Therefore:Scoring at least $90$ on the exam is a *sufficient* condition for getting an $A$. In other words, scoring at least $90$ *guarantees* that you'll get an $A$.Getting an $A$ is a *necessary* condition for scoring at least $90$. In other words, if you score at least $90$, it necessarily follows that you'll receive $A$. However, getting an $A$ *does not guarantee* that you scored at least $90$ on the exam. For example, the teacher may have decided to award an $A$ despite a score smaller than 90 if the exam was particularly tough that year.

## Identifying Necessary or Sufficient Conditions From Implications

**Example:** $\qquad$ $(A=\overline{B}) \Rightarrow (A\cap B = \emptyset)$

Which of the following statements are true regarding the true implication above?

1. $A=\overline{B}$ is a **necessary** condition for $A\cap B = \emptyset$
2. $A=\overline{B}$ is a **sufficient** condition for $A\cap B = \emptyset$
3. $A\cap B = \emptyset$ is a **necessary** condition for $A=\overline{B}$
4. $A\cap B = \emptyset$ is a **sufficient** condition for $A=\overline{B}$

**Explanation**

Given that the implication $S \Rightarrow N$ is true, we have the following:

- $S$ being true *guarantees* that $N$ must also be true. Therefore, $S$ is a *sufficient condition* for $N$.
- For $S$ to be true, it is *necessary* that $N$ is true. Therefore, $N$ is a *necessary condition* for $S$. However, note that $N$ being true does *not* guarantee that $S$ is true.

These ideas can be summarized as follows:

$$
\textbf{S}\textrm{ufficient} \: \Rightarrow \: \textbf{N}\textrm{ecessary}
$$

In our case,

$$
\underbrace{\big(\, A=\overline{B} \, \big)}_{\large\textrm{sufficient}} \: \Rightarrow \: \underbrace{\big(\, A\cap B = \emptyset \, \big)}_{\large\textrm{necessary}}
$$

is a true statement. Therefore:

- Statement II is true. $A=\overline{B}$ is a **sufficient** condition for $A\cap B = \emptyset$.
- Statement III is true. $A\cap B = \emptyset$ is a **necessary** condition for $A=\overline{B}$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."

## Identifying Necessary Conditions

**Example:** Consider the following statement:

$\qquad$ $x$ is divisible by $8$

Which of the following conditions are necessary for this statement to be true?

1. $x$ is divisible by $2$
2. $x$ is divisible by $4$
3. $x$ is divisible by $6$

**Explanation**

Given that the implication $S \Rightarrow N$ is true, we have the following:

- $S$ being true *guarantees* that $N$ must also be true. Therefore, $S$ is a *sufficient condition* for $N$.
- For $S$ to be true, it is *necessary* that $N$ is true. Therefore, $N$ is a *necessary condition* for $S$. However, note that $N$ being true does *not* guarantee that $S$ is true.

These ideas can be summarized as follows:

$$
\textbf{S}\textrm{ufficient} \: \Rightarrow \: \textbf{N}\textrm{ecessary}
$$

In our case, we need to find the conditions that are *necessary* from the given one:

$$
x
$$

With that in mind, let's inspect each of the given conditions.

- Condition I is a necessary condition:
$x$
Since $2$ is a factor of $8$, any number $x$ that is divisible by $8$ is also divisible by $2$.
- Condition II is a necessary condition:
$x$
Since $4$ is a factor of $8$, any number $x$ that is divisible by $8$ is also divisible by $4$.
- Condition III is *not* a necessary condition:
$x$
For example, the number $x=16$ is divisible by $8$, but it is *not* divisible by $6$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

## Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

Suppose that an equivalence $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is true. Then, we have the following terminology:

- $P$ is a **necessary and sufficient condition** for $Q$
- $Q$ is a **necessary and sufficient condition** for $P$

For example, consider the following two predicates:

- $35 \mid n$
- $5 \mid n$ and $7 \mid n$

These are both necessary and sufficient conditions for each other.

Indeed, recall that a number is divisible by $35$ *if and only if* it's divisible by both $5$ and $7$. In other words, we have

$$
5∣n
$$

and

$$
5∣n
$$

Therefore, we can write

$$
5∣n
$$

In words:

- $\big(35 \mid n \big)$ *is necessary and sufficient for* $5∣n$
- $5∣n$ *is necessary and sufficient for* $\big(35 \mid n \big)$.

<a id="identifying-necessary-and-sufficient-conditions"></a>
## Identifying Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

**Example:** Consider the following statement:

$\qquad$ $x^2 - x = 0$

Which of the following conditions are necessary and sufficient for the above statement to be true?

1. $x = 0$ or $x=1$
2. $x = 1$
3. $x$ is an integer.

**Explanation**

Given that the equivalence $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is true, we have that $P$ is a *necessary and sufficient condition* for $Q$.

In our case, we need to find the conditions that are *necessary and sufficient* for the given one. In other words,

$$
\big(x^2 - x = 0 \big) \: \Rightarrow \: \textrm{Condition}
$$

and

$$
\big(x^2 - x = 0 \big) \: \Leftarrow \: \textrm{Condition}
$$

Note that the solution to the given equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} - x &= 0 \\
x(x - 1) &= 0 \\
x &= 0, 1
\end{aligned}
$$

With that in mind, let's inspect each of the given conditions.

- Condition I is both necessary and sufficient: The statement $x^2 - x=0$ is true *if and only if* $x=0$ or $x=1$.
$x = 0$
and
$x = 0$
Therefore, we can write
$x = 0$

- Condition II is *not* a necessary condition:
$\big(x^2 - x = 0 \big) \: \nRightarrow \: \big(x=1 \big) \:\: {\color{red}\times}$
For example, for the number $x=0$, the statement $x^2 - x=0$ is true but $x\neq 1$.
- Condition III is *not* a sufficient condition:
$x$
For example, the number $x=2$ is an integer but it is not a solution to $x^2 - x=0$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only."
