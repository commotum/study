## Introduction

The relationship between two variables $x$ and $y$ is **linear** if there is a **common difference** between successive values of $y$.

For example, consider the relationship between $x$ and $y$ shown below.

| $x$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $2$ | $4$ | $6$ | $8$ |

This is a linear relationship. As $x$ increases by $1$, the respective value of $y$ increases by ${\color{SandyBrown}{2}}$.

| $\phantom{A}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $x$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| $y$ | $2$ | $4$ | $6$ | $8$ |
|  | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{+2}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{+2}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{+2}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |

In this case, the common difference is ${\color{SandyBrown}{2}}$.

We can calculate the common difference for any linear relationship by subtracting any value of $y$ from the one that precedes it. In our case,

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 &= 4 - 2 \\
&= 6 - 4 \\
&= 8 - 6
\end{aligned}
$$

## Exponential Relationships

The relationship between two variables $x$ and $y$ is **exponential** if there is a **common ratio** between successive values of $y$.

For example, consider the relationship between $x$ and $y$ shown below.

| $x$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $1$ | $3$ | $9$ | $27$ |

This is an exponential relationship. As $x$ increases by $\color{SteelBlue}1$, the respective value of $y$ increases by a *factor* of ${\color{SandyBrown}3}$.

|  | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $x$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| $y$ | $1$ | $3$ | $9$ | $27$ |
|  | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 3}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 3}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 3}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |

In this case, the common ratio is ${\color{SandyBrown}{3}}$.

We can calculate the common ratio for any exponential relationship by dividing any $y$ value by the one that precedes it. In our case,

$$
\begin{aligned}
3 &= \frac{3}{1} \\
&= \frac{9}{3} \\
&= \frac{27}{9}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying True Statements Regarding a Relationship Between Two Variables

**Example:** Consider the relationship between $x$ and $y$ shown below.

| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y$ | $\dfrac15$ | $1$ | $5$ | $25$ |

Which of the following statements are true?

1. The relationship between $x$ and $y$ is linear
2. The relationship between $x$ and $y$ is exponential
3. If $x$ is increased by $1$, then $y$ increases by a factor of $10$

**Explanation**

To determine if the relationship is linear or exponential, we need to determine how $y$ changes as $x$ increases at each step:

- For a linear relationship, there should be a common *difference* between successive values of $y$.
- For an exponential relationship, there should be a common *ratio* between successive values of $y$.

With that in mind, let's examine each statement.

- Statement I is false, while statement II is true. Let's check for a common ratio by dividing successive values of $y$. We see that
$1 \div \left(\dfrac15\right) = 5$,
and similarly,
$\frac{5}{1} = \frac{25}{5} = 5$.
Since the *ratio* of any two successive terms is constant, the relationship is *exponential*. In this case, the common ratio is $5$.
- Statement III is false. When $x$ increases by $1$, the respective value of $y$ increases by a factor of $5$, not $10$.

|  | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SteelBlue}\overset{+1}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| $y$ | $\dfrac15$ | $1$ | $5$ | $25$ |
|  | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 5}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 5}{\longrightarrow}$ | $\color{SandyBrown}\underset{\times 5}{\longrightarrow}$ |  |

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

## Classifying the Type of Growth Given a Table of Values

**Example:** The following table shows the relationship between $V$, the value of a painting (in dollars), and $t$, the elapsed time (in years) since it was completed.

| $t$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $V(t)$ | $687$ | $859$ | $1\,074$ | $1\,343$ | $1\,679$ |

What kind of function best models this relationship?

**Explanation**

To determine if the relationship is linear or exponential, we need to determine how $V$ changes as $t$ increases at each step:

- For a linear relationship, there should be a common *difference* between successive values of $V$.
- For an exponential relationship, there should be a common *ratio* between successive values of $V$.

Let's check whether the table shows a linear or exponential relationship with that in mind.

- First, let's check for a common ratio by dividing successive values of $V{:}$
$(V(2))/(V(1))|= \frac{859}{687} \approx 1.25; (V(3))/(V(2))|= \frac{1074}{859} \approx 1.25; (V(4))/(V(3))|= \frac{1343}{1074} \approx 1.25; (V(5))/(V(4))|= \frac{1679}{1343} \approx 1.25$
This list of quotients shows a definite (although approximate) common ratio.
- Now, let's check for a linear pattern by finding the difference between successive values of $V{:}$
$V(2) - V(1)|= 859 - 687 = 172; V(3) - V(2)|= 1074 - 859 = 215; V(4) - V(3)|= 1343 - 1074 = 269; V(5) - V(4)|= 1679 - 1343 = 336$
This list of differences does not show a definite common difference.

Therefore, the function that best models the relationship is an exponential function with a common ratio of approximately $1.25$.

<a id="linear-vs-exponential-growth"></a>
## Linear vs. Exponential Growth

A function that grows exponentially will always, at some point, overtake a function that grows linearly. Functions that grow exponentially grow very rapidly.

For example, suppose that the function

$$
l(x)=10x+25
$$

models the population of lizard species $A$ in a nature reserve, while $e(x)=3^x$ models the population of lizard species $B$, where $x$ is the number of years since the last census.

At some point, the population of species $B$ will overtake the population of species $A$. But when will this happen?

To find how many years it takes for the population of species $B$ to be larger than the population of species $A$, we must find the value of $x$ for which $e(x)$ first exceeds $l(x)$. So, we create a table of values for $l(x)$ and $e(x)$, as follows:

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ | $\ldots$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $l(x) = 10x+25$ | $35$ | $45$ | $55$ | $65$ | $75$ | $\ldots$ |
| $e(x) = 3^x$ | $3$ | $9$ | $27$ | $81$ | $243$ | $\ldots$ |

We see that $e(x)$ first exceeds $l(x)$ when $x=4$. Therefore, the population of species $B$ will be larger than the population of species $A$ after $4$ years.

Plotting $l(x)$ and $e(x)$ on a set of coordinate axes, we get the following:

![](<../Source/14.2.8. Linear vs. Exponential Growth and Decay/Images/91eed32c468ab16257d3c6d7526762fa.png>)

Notice that the points corresponding to $e(x)$ lie above those corresponding to $l(x)$ for

$$
x\geq 4
$$

## Finding the Point When an Exponential Function First Exceeds a Linear Function

**Example:** Consider the linear function $l(x) = 5x+10$ and the exponential function $e(x)=2^x$, where $x$ is a positive integer. At which value of $x$ does the exponential function first exceed the linear function?

**Explanation**

To find the value of $x$ for which $e(x)$ first exceeds $l(x)$, we create a table of values of the two functions for increasing integer values of $x$ until $l(x) < e(x)$, as follows:

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ | $6$ | $\ldots$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $l(x) = 5x+10$ | $15$ | $20$ | $25$ | $30$ | $35$ | $40$ | $\ldots$ |
| $e(x) = 2^x$ | $2$ | $4$ | $8$ | $16$ | $32$ | $64$ | $\ldots$ |

Therefore, we see that $e(x)$ first exceeds $l(x)$ when $x=6$.
