## Introduction

When researchers collect data, they usually take a *sample* from a larger *population*. From this sample, they calculate a number such as an average (mean) or a percentage (proportion). Because the result comes from only part of the population, it is not exact but an *estimate* of the true population value.

That is why statistical reports often include a parameter called the **margin of error**, which tells us how far the sample result might be from the actual population value.

To interpret a margin of error, we form a range:

$$
\text{Sample estimate} \;\pm\; \text{Margin of error}
$$

This is called the **plausible interval**. It means that while we cannot know the exact population parameter, we can say that the true value is *very likely* (but not certain) to fall somewhere inside this interval.

![](<../Source/17.1.5. Sampling and Margin of Error/Images/affaf4300b990936caaf6ff4811b1eb6.png>)

So, the key ideas are the following:

- The sample estimate is our best guess of a population parameter (like the mean or proportion) based on the data.
- The margin of error adds "wiggle room" in both directions from the sample estimate.
- Together, they create a **plausible interval** for the population parameter.

For example, suppose a tech review measured the average battery life of a random sample of laptops and found it to be $9.2$ hours, with a margin of error of $0.8$ hours.
To what interval do the plausible values of the population mean belong?

We are given that the sample mean is $9.2$ hours with a margin of error of $0.8$ hours, so we calculate the plausible bounds as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Lower bound &= 9.2 - 0.8 = 8.4 \\
Upper bound &= 9.2 + 0.8 = 10.0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, plausible values of the population mean are between $8.4$ hours and $10.0$ hours.

**Note:** In this topic you will always be given an appropriate margin of error. You are not expected to calculate it. Calculating margin of error requires using more advanced statistical techniques, which you'll encounter in future courses.

<a id="calculating-plausible-bounds"></a>
## Calculating Plausible Bounds

**Example:** A local library surveyed a random sample of patrons about adding more weekend activities. All selected patrons responded. It was estimated that $31\%$ of patrons supported the addition, with a margin of error of $5\%$.

Find the plausible interval for the percentage of patrons who supported the addition.

**Explanation**

The estimate is $31\%$ with a margin of error of $5\%$, so we compute the plausible bounds as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Lower bound &= 31% - 5% = 26% \\
Upper bound &= 31% + 5% = 36%
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, plausible values of the percent of patrons who support the addition are between $26\%$ and $36\%$.

<a id="calculating-plausible-population-values-given-percent-margin-of-error"></a>
## Calculating Plausible Population Values Given Percent Margin of Error

**Example:** A battery warehouse stores $7{,}200$ batteries. To estimate the percentage that are rechargeable, a random sample was taken. The percent of rechargeable batteries was estimated to be $68\%$, with a margin of error of $5\%$.

Find the plausible interval for the actual number of rechargeable batteries in the warehouse.

**Explanation**

The estimate is $68\%$ with a margin of error of $5\%$, so we compute the plausible bounds as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Lower percent &= 68% - 5% = 63% \\
Upper percent &= 68% + 5% = 73%
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we convert these percentages to totals using $7{,}200$ total batteries:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Lower total &= 63% \cdot 7,200 \\
&= 0.63 \cdot 7,200 \\
&= 4,536 \\
Upper total &= 73% \cdot 7,200 \\
&= 0.73 \cdot 7,200 \\
&= 5,256
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, a plausible range for the actual number of rechargeable batteries is between $4{,}536$ and $5{,}256$.

<a id="interpreting-the-margin-of-error"></a>
## Interpreting the Margin of Error

Recall that the *plausible interval* created by the sample estimate and margin of error gives us a range for the corresponding *population parameter* (e.g., the mean or proportion).

It is important to understand what this interval *does* and *does not* mean.

- What it **DOES** mean. The interval
$\text{Sample estimate} \;\pm\; \text{Margin of error}$
describes plausible values for the *population parameter*. The population value is very *likely* to lie within this interval, but not guaranteed. It is *unlikely* to lie outside the interval, but it is still possible.
- What it **DOES NOT** mean. The interval does *not* describe individual values. Some (or even all) individuals in the population may fall *outside* the bounds of the interval.For example, suppose in a survey about students' study time, the plausible interval for the mean is $13\:\text{–}\:16$ hours per week. But this doesn't mean that all students study between $13$ and $16$ hours. Some might study less than $13$ hours or more than $16$ hours per week.In fact, roughly half of the students could study about $10$ hours and the other half about $20$ hours. Their mean would still be near $15$ hours, which lies between $13$ and $16$.

Let's see some examples.

<a id="identifying-true-statements-concerning-margin-of-error"></a>
## Identifying True Statements Concerning Margin of Error

**Example:** A sports scientist measured the average sprint time for a random sample of soccer players. Based on the data, the estimated mean sprint time for $100$ meters was $11.4$ seconds, with a margin of error of $0.3$ seconds.

Given this information, which of the following statements are true?

1. It is plausible that the mean sprint time for all soccer players is between $11.1$ and $11.7$ seconds.
2. It is possible that some players take less than $11$ seconds to complete $100$ meters.
3. It is **NOT** possible that some players take $12$ seconds or more to complete $100$ meters.

**Explanation**

The estimated mean sprint time is $11.4$ seconds with a margin of error of $0.3$ seconds.
We calculate the plausible bounds as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Lower bound &= 11.4 - 0.3 = 11.1 \\
Upper bound &= 11.4 + 0.3 = 11.7
\end{aligned}
$$

The margin of error bounds the *population mean*, not individual sprint times, so individual values can be below $11.1$ seconds or above $11.7$ seconds.

With that in mind, we have the following:

- Statement I is true.
- Statement II is true.
- Statement III is false.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

## Sample Size and Margin of Error

When you take a sample, the margin of error indicates how far your estimate might be from the true population value. If you only collect a small sample, random chance plays a larger role—one unusual data point can significantly shift your results. But as your sample size grows, those random ups and downs start to balance out. Each new data point adds a little more information and reduces the influence of any single outlier.

With larger samples, your estimate becomes more stable and precise, which means the margin of error decreases. In other words, the more people (or items) you include in your sample, the more confident you can be that your sample’s results are close to the actual truth for the whole population.

The rule of thumb is:

- Smaller samples typically have larger margins of error.
- Larger samples typically have smaller margins of error.

Let's see an example.

---

<a id="the-relationship-between-margin-of-error-and-sample-size"></a>
## The Relationship Between Margin of Error and Sample Size

**Example:** A hospital studied a random sample of patients and found that the average waiting time in the emergency department was $47$ minutes, with a margin of error of $6$ minutes. The study was then repeated with a much larger sample size, and the new sample’s mean and margin of error were calculated in the same way as in the original study.

Which of the following statements is most likely true?

1. The margin of error from the new study is smaller than the margin of error from the original study.
2. The mean from the new study is larger than the mean from the original study.
3. The mean from the new study is $47$ minutes.

**Explanation**

More information is gathered in larger samples, which reduces the amount of random fluctuation in the sample estimate. As a result, the estimate is more stable and precise, with a smaller margin of error.

In our case, the new study has a much *larger sample size* than the original study. So, the margin of error from the new study is *smaller* than that from the original study. Therefore, statement I is likely true.

On the other hand, the new sample mean could be higher, lower, or essentially the same as the original mean; the direction is not determined by sample size alone. Therefore, statements II and III might not be true.

In conclusion, the correct answer is "I only."
