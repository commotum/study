## Introduction

Whenever we have an expression that's written as a difference of squares, we can factor the expression by applying the difference of squares formula:

$$
a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)
$$

Factoring a difference of squares works even when the numbers involved are not perfect squares! The key is to recognize that *any* positive number can be written as the square of its square root, even if that square root is irrational.

Let’s illustrate this idea by factoring the following expression:
$x^2 - 2$

To factor this expression, we first note that the $2$ can be expressed as a square:

$$
2 = \big(\sqrt{2}\big)^2
$$

This means we can write our expression $x^2-2$ as a difference of squares:
$x^{2} - (\sqrt{2})^{2}$

Finally, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula with $a=x$ and

$$
b=\sqrt{2}{:}
$$

$$
x^{2} - (\sqrt{2})^{2} = (x - \sqrt{2})(x + \sqrt{2})
$$

Let's see some more examples.

## Factoring a Difference of Squares

**Example:** Factor the expression $y^2-\dfrac{1}{10}$.

**Explanation**

Notice that

$$
10 = \big(\sqrt{10}\big)^2
$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^{2} - \frac{1}{10} &=  \\
y^{2} - (1)/((\sqrt{10})^{2}) &=  \\
&= y^{2} - (\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}})^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$
y^{2} - (\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}})^{2} = (y - \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}})(y + \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}})
$$

## Factoring a Difference of Squares with Leading Coefficients

**Example:** Factor the expression $17t^2-13$.

**Explanation**

Notice that

$$
17 = \big(\sqrt{17}\big)^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 13 = \big(\sqrt{13}\big)^2
$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$
\begin{aligned}
17t^{2} - 13 &= (\sqrt{17}t)^{2} - (\sqrt{13})^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$
(\sqrt{17}t)^{2} - (\sqrt{13})^{2} = (\sqrt{17}t - \sqrt{13})(\sqrt{17}t + \sqrt{13})
$$

## Factoring a Difference of Squares Whose Terms Have a Common Factor

**Example:** Factor the expression $\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36$.

**Explanation**

The terms of our expression have a common factor of $4$. So, we simplify the expression by factoring it out:

$$
\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36 = 4\left(\dfrac{m^2}{5}-9\right)
$$

Now, let's factor the expression inside parentheses using the difference of squares formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{m^{2}}{5} - 9 &=  \\
(\frac{m}{\sqrt{5}})^{2} - 3^{2} &=  \\
&= (\frac{m}{\sqrt{5}} - 3)(\frac{m}{\sqrt{5}} + 3)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, our original expression factors as follows:

$$
\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36 = 4\left(\dfrac{m}{\sqrt5} - 3 \right)\left(\dfrac{m}{\sqrt5} + 3 \right)
$$

## Factoring a Difference of Squares Containing More Than One Variable

**Example:** Factor the expression $-5r^2+ 36s^2$.

**Explanation**

Notice that

$$
5 = \big(\sqrt{5}\big)^2
$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-5r^{2} + 36s^{2} &=  \\
36s^{2} - 5r^{2} &=  \\
&= (6s)^{2} - (\sqrt{5}r)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$
(6s)^{2} - (\sqrt{5}r)^{2} = (6s - \sqrt{5}r)(6s + \sqrt{5}r)
$$
