## Introduction

The **midpoint theorem** states the following:

> *The line segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side, and its length is equal to half the length of the third side.*

To illustrate, consider the triangle $\triangle ABC$ shown below, where $M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$ and $L$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AC}$. How can we find the length of $\color{SteelBlue}{\overline{BC}}$ if

$$
{\color{DarkGoldenrod}{ML}} = 4
$$

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/197641f478abe12da442d31f8009dc24.png>)

According to the midpoint theorem, since $\overline{LM}$ joins the midpoints of the two sides $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, the length of $\overline{LM}$ is half the length of the third side $\overline{BC}$:

$$
{\color{DarkGoldenrod}{ML}} =\dfrac{1}{2}{\color{SteelBlue}{{BC}}}
$$

Therefore,

$$
{\color{SteelBlue}{BC}} = 2 {\color{DarkGoldenrod}{ML}} =2\cdot 4 = 8
$$

Note that the midpoint theorem also guarantees that $\overline{ML} \parallel \overline{BC}$, even though we didn't need to use that fact to solve this particular problem.

## Calculating Lengths of Segments Using the Midpoint Theorem

**Example:** In the triangle $\triangle ABC$, $F$ is the midpoint of the side $\overline{AC}$ and $E$ is the midpoint of the side $\overline{BC}$. Find the measure of $\overline{FE}$ if $AB = 26$.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/54e34e26b6a6e15b330c64dcafc03b89.png>)

**Explanation**

According to the midpoint theorem, the line segment joining the midpoints of two sides is equal to half the length of the third side. Therefore, we obtain

$$
FE = \dfrac{AB}{2} = 13
$$

## Determining Measures of Angles Using the Midpoint Theorem

**Example:** Given the triangle above, find the measure of $\angle{K}$.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/bc0ae8d96e14c95fee65e199fba17033.png>)

**Explanation**

According to the midpoint theorem, the line segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side.

Now, since $\overline{CE} \parallel \overline{LM}$, we have a pair of corresponding angles, $\angle{KCE}$ and $\angle{L}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/99b4dfd836df93157474e05858eab6e8.png>)

Therefore, we obtain

$$
m\angle{L} = m\angle{KCE} = 100^{\circ}
$$

We now find the measure of $\angle{K}$ using the sum of the interior angles of a triangle. We get

$$
\begin{aligned}
m∠K &= 180^{∘} - m∠L - m∠M \\
&= 180^{∘} - 100^{∘} - 45^{∘} \\
&= 35^{∘}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating Perimeters Using the Midpoint Theorem

**Example:** Given the diagram below, find the perimeter of the quadrilateral $LKBA$ if the perimeter of $\triangle CKL$ is $25$.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/219f35f9e4d06e87cae9f65b89df20b6.png>)

**Explanation**

According to the midpoint theorem, the line segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is equal to half the length of the third side. Therefore,

$$
AB = 2KL
$$

Now, the perimeter of the quadrilateral $LKBA$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= KL + KB + AB + AL \\
&= KL + CK + 2KL + CL \\
&= 2KL + (KL + CK + CL) \\
&= 2KL + p_{△CKL} \\
&= 2 \cdot 10 + 25 \\
&= 20 + 25 \\
&= 45
\end{aligned}
$$

## The Converse of the Midpoint Theorem

The converse of the midpoint theorem is also true. This states the following:

> *A line drawn through the midpoint of one side of a triangle and parallel to another side bisects the third side.*

To illustrate, consider the triangle $\triangle{ABC}$ below.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/eb8e2871991a5688188a4ba587e2ea2f.png>)

Here, ${\color{DarkGreen}{\overline{DE}}}$ passes through $D$, the midpoint of $\overline{AC}$, and is parallel to the side ${\color{SteelBlue}\overline{AB}}$.

So, by the converse of the midpoint theorem, we have that ${\color{DarkGreen}{\overline{DE}}}$ bisects the third side, $\overline{CB}$.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/5a7bd205c0717ac9ab96e3422d405ae2.png>)

## Finding Lengths of Line Segments Using the Converse of the Midpoint Theorem

**Example:** Given the diagram below, where $\overline{AB} \parallel \overline{DE}$, determine the value of $BD$.

![](<../Source/7.2.13. The Midpoint Theorem/Images/84a5a7a0ec7354c1c4401d32a1750247.png>)

**Explanation**

Since $AE = CE$ and $\overline{AB} \parallel \overline{DE}$, by the converse of the midpoint theorem, $D$ is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$. Therefore,

$$
BD = CD = 5
$$
