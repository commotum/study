## Determining Possible Non-Interior Points of a Circle Given Its Canonical Equation

**Example:** A circle in the $xy$-plane has equation $(x - 7)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 16$. Which of the following points does **NOT** lie in the interior of the circle?

1. $(7,-4)$
2. $(12,-4)$
3. $(9,-4)$

**Explanation**

The general equation of a circle with radius $r$ centered at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$
(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2
$$

We are given the equation

$$
(x - 7)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 16
$$

which can be rewritten as

$$
(x - 7)^2 + (y - (-4))^2 = 4^2
$$

Comparing the last equation with the general equation of a circle, we obtain that the center of our circle has coordinates $(7,-4)$, and its radius is $4$.

Now, for a point to be inside a circle, the distance from that point to the center of the circle must be less than the radius.

With this in mind, we proceed to find which of the given points is such that its distance to $(7,-4)$ is less than $4$ (the radius of the circle).

- For $(7,-4)$, this is the center of the circle, and therefore must lie in its interior.
- For $(12,-4)$, we get
$\sqrt{(12 - 7)^{2} + (-4 - (-4))^{2}}|= \sqrt{5^{2} + 0}; = \sqrt{25}; = 5; ≮4$.
- For $(9,-4)$, we obtain
$\sqrt{(9 - 7)^{2} + (-4 - (-4))^{2}}|= \sqrt{2^{2} + 0}; = \sqrt{4}; = 2; < 4$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

## Determining Constraints of a Circle Parameter Given Its Canonical Equation

**Example:** The graph of the equation $(x + 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 100$ is a circle in the $xy$-plane. The point $(h, k)$ lies on the circle. Which of the following is a possible value for $k$?

1. $10$
2. $13$
3. $6$

**Explanation**

The general equation of a circle with radius $r$ centered at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$
(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2
$$

We are given the equation

$$
(x + 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 100
$$

which can be rewritten as

$$
(x - (-4))^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 10^2
$$

So, the center of the circle is $(-4, 3)$ and its radius is $10$.

Next, we draw our circle in the $xy$-plane.

![](<../Source/3.5.4. Solving Problems Involving the Canonical Equation of a Circle/Images/27d60455fab9f1d3fb5b35fb3e61bdd1.png>)

Since the $y$-coordinate of the center is $3$ and the radius is $10$, the least possible $y$-coordinate for any point on the circle is

$$
3 - 10 = -7
$$

Similarly, the greatest possible $y$-coordinate for any point on the circle is

$$
3 + 10 = 13
$$

Since $(h, k)$ lies on the circle, we must have

$$
-7 \leq k \leq 13
$$

Therefore, among the given options, the only value that satisfies the above inequality is $13$. So, the correct answer is "II" only.

## Identifying a Canonical Equation That Touches an Axis

**Example:** Which of the following equations represents a circle in the $xy$-plane that intersects the $x$-axis at exactly one point?

1. $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 16$
2. $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 16$
3. $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 25$
4. $(x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 9$

**Explanation**

The general equation of a circle with radius $r$ centered at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$
(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2
$$

The radius of a circle is the distance from the center of the circle to any point on the circle. If a circle in the $xy$-plane intersects the $x$-axis at exactly one point, then the distance from the center of the circle to this point on the $x$-axis must be equal to the radius of the circle.

In our case, if

$$
(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2
$$

represents a circle that intersects the $x$-axis at exactly one point, then

$$
\mid b \mid = r
$$

With this in mind, we proceed to analyze each of the equations given.

- For the equation $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 16$, we have $b = -4$ and $r = 4$. Then $\mid b \mid = r$.
- For the equation $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 16$, we have $b = -5$ and $r = 4$. Then $\mid b \mid \neq r$.
- For the equation $(x - 6)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 25$, we have $b = -4$ and $r = 5$. Then $\mid b \mid \neq r$.
- For the equation $(x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 9$, we have $b = 4$ and $r = 3$. Then $\mid b \mid \neq r$.

Therefore, the circle that touches the $x$-axis at exactly one point is

$$
(x - 6)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 16
$$

So, the correct answer is "IV" only.

## Finding the Coordinates of a Diameter's Endpoint Given Another Endpoint and the Canonical Equation

**Example:** In the $xy$-plane, the graph of the equation
$(x + 3)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 36$
is a circle. Point $M$ lies on the circle and has coordinates $(-3, 7)$. If $\overline{MN}$ is a diameter of the circle, what are the coordinates of point $N$?

**Explanation**

The general equation of a circle with radius $r$ centered at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$
(x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2
$$

We are given the equation

$$
(x + 3)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 36
$$

which can be rewritten as

$$
\big(x - (-3)\big)^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 6^2
$$

Comparing the last equation with the general equation of a circle, we obtain that the coordinates of the center of our circle are $(-3,1)$.

We are told that $\overline{MN}$ is a diameter of the circle. Therefore, the midpoint of $\overline{MN}$ coincides with the center of the circle; that is, the midpoint of $\overline{MN}$ has coordinates $(-3,1)$.

Now, let $(x_1,y_1)$ be the coordinates of point $N$. Since $(-3,1)$ is the midpoint of the segment with endpoints $M(-3,7)$ and $N(x_1,y_1)$, we have

$$
(-3,1) = \left(\dfrac{-3 + x_1}{2}, \dfrac{7 + y_1}{2}\right)
$$

Solving for $x_1$ in the equation

$$
-3 = \dfrac{-3 + x_1}{2}
$$

and solving for $y_1$ in the equation

$$
1 = \dfrac{7 + y_1}{2}
$$

we get $x_1 = -3$ and $y_1 = -5$.

Therefore, the coordinates of point $N$ are $(-3,-5)$.
