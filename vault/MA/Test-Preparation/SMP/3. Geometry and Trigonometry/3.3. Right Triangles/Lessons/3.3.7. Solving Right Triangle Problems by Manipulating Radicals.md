## Introduction

On the math section of the SAT exam, we may see right-triangle problems where side lengths are written with radicals, and we’re expected to simplify and compare the roots.

With focused practice on factoring radicands and extracting perfect squares, we can avoid common mistakes with radical arithmetic.

## Using the Pythagorean Theorem Given Radical Side Lengths

**Example:** A right triangle has legs with lengths of $4\,\textrm{cm}$ and $6\sqrt{3}\,\textrm{cm}$. If the length of this triangle's hypotenuse can be written in the form $2\sqrt{d}\,\textrm{cm}$, where $d$ is an integer, what is the value of $d$?

**Explanation**

We begin by sketching a right triangle with legs whose lengths are $4\,\textrm{cm}$ and $6\sqrt{3}\,\textrm{cm}$, and a hypotenuse with length $2\sqrt{d}\,\textrm{cm}$.

![](<../Source/3.3.7. Solving Right Triangle Problems by Manipulating Radicals/Images/e7dfed05f399be5bf637435a3b916d35.png>)

The Pythagorean theorem states that for a right triangle,

$$
c^2 = a^2 + b^2
$$

where $a$ and $b$ represent the lengths of the legs of the triangle, and $c$ represents the length of its hypotenuse.

In our case, we are given the lengths of the legs

$$
a = 4\,\textrm{cm}
$$

and

$$
b = 6\sqrt{3}\,\textrm{cm}
$$

and the length of the hypotenuse

$$
c = 2\sqrt{d}\,\textrm{cm}
$$

Substituting these values into the formula above and solving for $d$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\sqrt{d})^{2} &= 4^{2} + (6\sqrt{3})^{2} \\
4d &= 16 + 108 \\
4d &= 124 \\
d &= \frac{124}{4} \\
d &= 31
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding Areas of Isosceles Right Triangles by Comparing Radicals

**Example:** An isosceles right triangle has a side of length $\sqrt{648}\,\textrm{cm}$ and another side of length $9\sqrt{4}\,\textrm{cm}$. In square centimeters, what is the area of the triangle?

**Explanation**

To determine which side is the hypotenuse (i.e., the longest side), we rewrite the radicals as single square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
9\sqrt{4} &= \sqrt{81} \cdot \sqrt{4} \\
&= \sqrt{81 \cdot 4} \\
&= \sqrt{324}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we compare the square roots directly:
$\sqrt{648} > \sqrt{324}$

So, the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{648}$, and the legs are $9\sqrt{4}$ and $9\sqrt{4}$.

Next, we apply the formula for the area of a $45^\circ$-$45^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle

$$
\mathcal A = \dfrac{1}{2} s^2
$$

where $s$ is a leg. Substituting the values, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot (9\sqrt{4})^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 9^{2} \cdot (\sqrt{4})^{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 81 \cdot 4 \\
&= 81 \cdot \frac{4}{2} \\
&= 81 \cdot 2 \\
&= 162
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the area of the triangle is $162 \,\textrm{cm}^2$.

## Finding Areas of Right Triangles by Comparing Radicals

**Example:** A right triangle has side lengths $2\sqrt{2}$, $5\sqrt{2}$, and $\sqrt{58}$ units. What is the area of the triangle, in square units?

**Explanation**

We are given that the triangle has side lengths:
$2\sqrt{2}, \quad 5\sqrt{2}, \quad \sqrt{58}$

To determine which side is the hypotenuse (i.e., the longest side), we rewrite the radicals as single square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\sqrt{2} &= \sqrt{2^{2}} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= \sqrt{4} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= \sqrt{4 \cdot 2} \\
&= \sqrt{8} \\
5\sqrt{2} &= \sqrt{5^{2}} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= \sqrt{25} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} \\
&= \sqrt{50}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we compare the square roots directly:
$\sqrt{58} > \sqrt{50} > \sqrt{8}$

So, the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{58}$, and the legs are $2\sqrt{2}$ and $5\sqrt{2}$.

Next, we apply the formula for the area of a right triangle

$$
\mathcal A = \dfrac{1}{2} ab
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the legs. Substituting the values, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2\sqrt{2} \cdot 5\sqrt{2} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 10 \cdot (\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2}) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot 10 \cdot 2 \\
&= \frac{20}{2} \\
&= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the area of the triangle is $10$ square units.
