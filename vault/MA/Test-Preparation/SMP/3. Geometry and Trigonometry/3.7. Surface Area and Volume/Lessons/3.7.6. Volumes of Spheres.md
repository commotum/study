## Introduction

Questions involving spheres usually test whether we can apply and manipulate the volume formula efficiently, including working backward to find the radius or diameter from a given volume.

In this lesson, we’ll practice using the sphere volume formula in both direct and inverse problem setups.

<a id="finding-the-volume-of-a-sphere"></a>
## Finding the Volume of a Sphere

**Example:** What is the volume, in cubic meters, of a sphere with a radius of $\dfrac{9}{2}$ meters?

**Explanation**

We are given the radius of a sphere and asked to find its volume.

The formula for the volume of a sphere is

$$
V = \dfrac{4}{3} \pi r^3
$$

where $r$ is the radius.

Let's substitute

$$
r = \dfrac{9}{2}
$$

into the formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{4}{3}π(\frac{9}{2})^{3} \\
&= \frac{4}{3}π \cdot \frac{729}{8} \\
&= \frac{243π}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the volume is

$$
\dfrac{243\pi}{2}
$$

cubic meters.

## Finding the Radius of a Sphere Given Its Volume

**Example:** What is the radius of a sphere whose volume is $\dfrac{32}{3}\pi\:\textrm{m}^3$?

**Explanation**

We can find the volume of a sphere using the formula

$$
V=\dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3
$$

where $r$ is the radius of the sphere.

Substituting

$$
V=\dfrac{32}{3}\pi\:\textrm{m}^3
$$

into the formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= \frac{4}{3}πr^{3} \\
\frac{32}{3}π &= \frac{4}{3}πr^{3} \\
\frac{32π \cdot 3}{3 \cdot 4π} &= r^{3} \\
r^{3} &= 8 \\
&= 2^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
r = \sqrt[3]{2^3} = 2\:\textrm{m}
$$
