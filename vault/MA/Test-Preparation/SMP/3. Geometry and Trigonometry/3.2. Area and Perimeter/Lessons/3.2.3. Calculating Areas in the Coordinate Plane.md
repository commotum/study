## Introduction

In the math section of the SAT exam, we’ll often encounter geometry problems that ask you to calculate areas of figures drawn in the coordinate plane using points, distances, or diagonals.

Consistent practice with these problems will help us quickly translate between coordinates and geometry formulas.

## Finding the Area of a Square Centered at the Origin

**Example:** In the $xy$-plane shown, rhombus $EFGH$ has its diagonals aligned along the $x$- and $y$-axes. What is the area, in square units, of the rhombus?

![](<../Source/3.2.3. Calculating Areas in the Coordinate Plane/Images/0528bbd9167b17228b702a5c4b9c3c62.png>)

**Explanation**

We are given that $EFGH$ is a rhombus, which means all of its sides are congruent and its diagonals intersect at right angles. So, $\overline{EG}$ divides this rhombus into two congruent triangles, $\triangle{EFG}$ and $\triangle{EHG}$.

The formula for the area of a triangle is given by

$$
A_t = \dfrac{b \cdot h}{2}
$$

where $b$ is the base length of the triangle and $h$ is the height of the triangle.

In our case, each triangle has a base length of

$$
b = 7 - (-7) = 14
$$

units, while its height is $h = 5$. Therefore, the area of each triangle, in square units, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{t} &= \frac{b \cdot h}{2} \\
&= \frac{14 \cdot 5}{2} \\
&= \frac{70}{2} \\
&= 35
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, since $\triangle{EFG}$ and $\triangle{EHG}$ are congruent, the area of each is

$$
\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

of the area of rhombus $EFGH$. Therefore, the area of rhombus $EFGH$, in square units, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{r} &= 2 \cdot A_{t} \\
&= 2 \cdot 35 \\
&= 70
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding a Leg of a Right Triangle Given Its Area and Another Leg

**Example:** In the coordinate plane, the segment shown represents one of the legs of a right triangle. The area of this triangle is $54\sqrt{5}$ square units. What is the length, in units, of the other leg of this triangle?

![](<../Source/3.2.3. Calculating Areas in the Coordinate Plane/Images/187f9a54052b35783351bac5702aa236.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's remember that if $P$ and $Q$ have coordinates $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$, respectively, the distance between $P$ and $Q$ is given by

$$
d(P,Q) = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}
$$

We proceed to find the length $x$ of the first leg of the right triangle, whose endpoints are $(1,3)$ and $(4,9)$. To do this, we use the formula for the distance between points given above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= \sqrt{(4 - 1)^{2} + (9 - 3)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{3^{2} + 6^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{9 + 36} \\
&= \sqrt{45} \\
&= \sqrt{9 \cdot 5} \\
&= \sqrt{9} \cdot \sqrt{5} \\
&= 3\sqrt{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $y$ be the length of the second leg. We're told the area $A$ of the triangle is $54\sqrt{5}$ square units. Since the area of a right triangle is found by multiplying the lengths of its two legs and then dividing that product by two, we can find $y$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= 54\sqrt{5} \\
\frac{x \cdot y}{2} &= 54\sqrt{5} \\
\frac{3\sqrt{5} \cdot y}{2} &= 54\sqrt{5} \\
y &= \frac{2 \cdot 54\sqrt{5}}{3\sqrt{5}} \\
y &= 2 \cdot 18 \\
y &= 36
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the length of the second leg of the triangle is $36$ units.

## Finding the Area of a Triangle

**Example:** The vertices of $\triangle ABC$ have coordinates $A(1,1)$, $B(2,5)$, and $C(6,2)$. Find the area of $\triangle ABC$.

![](<../Source/3.2.3. Calculating Areas in the Coordinate Plane/Images/94eabfeb47ad89de154fcca136307106.png>)

**Explanation**

First, note that we can inscribe our triangle within the rectangle shown in the diagram below.

![](<../Source/3.2.3. Calculating Areas in the Coordinate Plane/Images/71c04a9cc861d1af2da92e961efb1512.png>)

We have the rectangle, whose area we denote by $A$, and four triangles:

- $\triangle ABC$, whose area $A_{\triangle}$ we're required to determine,
- the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{AB}$ and area $A_1$,
- the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{BC}$ and area $A_2$, and
- the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{AC}$ and area $A_3$.

Recall that the area of a figure consisting of several smaller non-overlapping parts is equal to the sum of the areas of those parts. So, we have

$$
A = A_{\triangle} + A_1 + A_2 + A_3
$$

which means that

$$
A_{\triangle} = A - A_1 - A_2 - A_3
$$

Let's now compute all the areas on the right-hand side of this equation.

- For the area of the rectangle, we have
$A|= width \cdot height; = 5 \cdot 4; = 20$.
- For the area of the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{AB}$, we have
$A_{1}|= \frac{1}{2} \cdot leg_{1} \cdot leg_{2}; = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 4; = 2$.
- For the area of the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{BC}$, we have
$A_{2}|= \frac{1}{2} \cdot leg_{1} \cdot leg_{2}; = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \cdot 3; = 6$.
- For the area of the right triangle with hypotenuse $\overline{AC}$, we have
$A_{3}|= \frac{1}{2} \cdot leg_{1} \cdot leg_{2}; = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 5 \cdot 1; = 2.5$.

Therefore, the area of $\triangle ABC$ is

$$
A_{\triangle} = 20 - 2 - 6 - 2.5 = 9.5
$$

square units.
