## Introduction

Questions about the number of solutions to a quadratic equation are common on the math section of the SAT exam. Usually, we’ll need to decide whether an equation has $0$, $1$, or $2$ real roots using the discriminant or by analyzing the equation's structure.

---

<a id="counting-solutions-to-perfect-square-quadratic-equations"></a>
## Counting Solutions to Perfect Square Quadratic Equations

**Example:** Determine the number of distinct real solutions for the equation $(t - 2)^{2} = 4$.

**Explanation**

We start by analyzing the equation:

$$
(t - 2)^{2} = 4
$$

Taking the square root of both sides, we get

$$
t - 2 = \pm 2
$$

Now, we solve for $t{:}$

$$
t = 2 + 2 = 4ort = 2 - 2 = 0
$$

Therefore, there are exactly two real solutions.

## Counting Solutions to Quadratic Equations in Standard Form

**Example:** Determine the number of distinct real solutions for the equation $2t^{2} + 5t + 1 = 0$.

**Explanation**

For a quadratic equation

$$
at^2 + bt + c = 0
$$

the discriminant is

$$
\mathcal{D} = b^2 - 4ac
$$

- If $\mathcal{D} > 0$, there are two distinct real solutions.
- If $\mathcal{D} = 0$, there is exactly one real solution.
- If $\mathcal{D} < 0$, there are no real solutions (the solutions are complex).

The coefficients of our quadratic equation are the following:

$$
a = 2, \qquad b = 5, \qquad c = 1
$$

So, the discriminant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
D &= b^{2} - 4ac \\
&= 5^{2} - 4(2)(1) \\
&= 25 - 8 \\
&= 17
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the discriminant is positive ($\mathcal{D} > 0$), the quadratic equation has exactly two real solutions.

## Counting the Number of Solutions to a Constant-Quadratic System

**Example:** ${y = x^{2} + 2x + 7; y =-3x + 5$

How many real solutions does the given system of equations have?

**Explanation**

To solve the given system, we set the two equations equal to each other:

$$
x^{2} + 2x + 7 = -3x + 5
$$

Now, we rearrange and write in standard form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + 2x + 7 + 3x - 5 &= 0 \\
x^{2} + 5x + 2 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

For a quadratic equation

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = 0
$$

the discriminant is

$$
\mathcal{D} = b^2 - 4ac
$$

- If $\mathcal{D} > 0$, there are two distinct real solutions.
- If $\mathcal{D} = 0$, there is exactly one real solution.
- If $\mathcal{D} < 0$, there are no real solutions (the solutions are complex).

The coefficients of our quadratic equation are the following:

$$
a = 1, \qquad b = 5, \qquad c = 2
$$

So, we compute the discriminant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D &= b^{2} - 4ac \\
&= 5^{2} - 4(1)(2) \\
&= 25 - 8 \\
&= 17
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\mathcal{D} > 0$, there are exactly $2$ real solutions.

## Counting the Number of Intersection Points Between a Parabola and a Line

**Example:** In the equation $y = a x^{2} - b$, where $a$ and $b$ are positive constants, how many times does its graph intersect the graph of $y = a + 2b$ in the $xy$-plane?

**Explanation**

To determine how many times the graph of $y=ax^2-b$ intersects the graph of

$$
y=a+2b
$$

we must find the $x$-coordinates of such points (if any). To do this, we equate the $y$-values ​​of both equations, as follows:

$$
a x^2 - b = a + 2b
$$

Next, we isolate the $x^2$ on one side of the equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
ax^{2} - b &= a + 2b \\
ax^{2} &= a + 3b \\
x^{2} &= \frac{a + 3b}{a}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that the last operation is possible since $a$ is positive. Also note that

$$
\dfrac{a + 3b}{a}
$$

is positive. Thus, we can take the square root of both sides of the last equation and solve the resulting absolute value equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} &= \frac{a + 3b}{a} \\
\sqrt{x^{2}} &= \sqrt{\frac{a + 3b}{a}} \\
\mid x \mid &= \sqrt{\frac{a + 3b}{a}} \\
x &= ± \sqrt{\frac{a + 3b}{a}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we can conclude that the graph of

$$
y = a x^{2} - b
$$

intersects the graph of

$$
y = a + 2b
$$

exactly twice.
