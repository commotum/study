## Introduction

The difference of squares formula often appears in questions that require quick recognition of algebraic patterns.

In this lesson, we’ll practice identifying and factoring differences of squares, including cases with coefficients, fractions, and multiple variables.

## Factoring Differences of Squares

**Example:** Find an equivalent factored form of the expression $x^2 - 64$.

**Explanation**

Find an equivalent factored form of the expression $x^2 - 64$.

The expression $x^2 - 64$ is a difference of squares. We recognize this pattern as

$$
x^2 - a^2 = (x + a)(x - a)
$$

Here, $a^2 = 64$, so $a = 8$.

Using this identity, we factor our expression as follows:

$$
x^2 - 64 = (x + 8)(x - 8)
$$

So, the expression $x^2 - 64$ is equivalent to $(x + 8)(x - 8)$.

## Factoring Differences of Squares With Leading Coefficients

**Example:** Find an equivalent factored form of the expression $121p^2 - 36$.

**Explanation**

When faced with simplifying expanded polynomial expressions, our strategy should consist of first checking for like terms and factorability.

Here, since we have no like terms, we must consider factoring. We have that $121 = 11^2$ and $36 = 6^2$, and so we may factor as a difference of squares:

$$
\begin{aligned}
121p^{2} - 36 &= 11^{2}p^{2} - 6^{2} \\
&= (11p)^{2} - (6)^{2} \\
&= (11p + 6)(11p - 6)
\end{aligned}
$$

## Factoring Differences of Squares After Factoring a Leading Coefficient

**Example:** The expression
$\dfrac{3}{4} z^{2} - 12$
can be rewritten in the form
$\dfrac{3}{4} (z - k)(z + k)$,
where $k$ is a positive constant. What is the value of $k$?

**Explanation**

We are asked to rewrite

$$
\dfrac{3}{4} z^{2} - 12
$$

in the form

$$
\dfrac{3}{4} (z - k)(z + k)
$$

First, we factor out

$$
\dfrac{3}{4}
$$

from the entire expression:

$$
\dfrac{3}{4} z^{2} - 12 = \dfrac{3}{4} \left(z^{2} - 16\right)
$$

Next, we can factor $z^2 - 16$ as follows:

$$
z^{2} - 16 = z^{2} - 4^{2} = (z - 4)(z + 4)
$$

Then, the entire expression can be written as

$$
\dfrac{3}{4} (z - 4)(z + 4)
$$

Finally, we compare the above expression with the given expression of $k$. Therefore, the value of $k$ is $4$.

## Factoring Differences of Squares for Two-Variable Binomials

**Example:** The expression $5a^2 - \dfrac{4}{9}b^2$ is equivalent to $5(a - cb)(a + cb)$, where $c$ is a positive constant. What is the value of $c$?

**Explanation**

Since our equivalent expression has a constant factor $5$, we start by factoring $5$ out of our original expression:

$$
5a^2 - \dfrac{4}{9}b^2 = 5\left(a^2 - \dfrac{4}{45}b^2\right)
$$

To write our bracketed expression as a difference of squares, we must write

$$
\dfrac{4}{45}
$$

as a square. Applying the exponent rules, we have

$$
\dfrac{4}{45} = \left(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{45}}\right)^2 = \left(\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt{5}}\right)^2
$$

Hence, we apply our formula for factoring a difference of squares:

$$
a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)
$$

With $a=a$ and

$$
b=\dfrac{2b}{3\sqrt{5}}
$$

this yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
5(a^{2} - \frac{4}{45}b^{2}) &= 5(a^{2} - (\frac{2}{3\sqrt{5}})^{2}b^{2}) \\
&= 5(a^{2} - (\frac{2b}{3\sqrt{5}})^{2}) \\
&= 5(a - \frac{2b}{3\sqrt{5}})(a + \frac{2b}{3\sqrt{5}}) \\
&= 5(a - \frac{2}{3\sqrt{5}}b)(a + \frac{2}{3\sqrt{5}}b)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
c = \dfrac{2}{3\sqrt{5}}
$$
