## Introduction

Factoring higher-order polynomials tests our ability to recognize patterns beyond simple quadratics.

In this topic, we’ll practice identifying and factoring polynomial patterns, including higher powers and nested differences of squares.

## Factoring Biquadratic Trinomials

**Example:** Which of the following is equivalent to the expression $x^4 + 13x^2 + 36$?

1. $(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 12)$
2. $(x^2 + 6)(x^2 + 3)$
3. $(x^2 + 3)(x^2 + 12)$
4. $(x^2 + 4)(x^2 + 9)$

**Explanation**

We want to factor the expression $x^4 + 13x^2 + 36$. First, we write this as

$$
\left(x^2\right)^2 + 13x^2 + 36
$$

Let $u = x^2$. Then, the expression becomes

$$
u^2 + 13u + 36
$$

Now, we factor this quadratic. We look for two numbers that multiply to $36$ and add up to $13$. Since

$$
4 \cdot 9 = 36
$$

and

$$
4 + 9 = 13
$$

, we can factor the expression as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u^{2} + 13u + 36 &= u^{2} + 9u + 4u + 36 \\
&= (u^{2} + 9u) + (4u + 36) \\
&= u(u + 9) + 4(u + 9) \\
&= (u + 4)(u + 9)
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we substitute $x^2$ back in for $u$:

$$
(x^2 + 4)(x^2 + 9)
$$

So, the factored form of $x^4 + 13x^2 + 36$ is $(x^2 + 4)(x^2 + 9)$ which is expression "IV."

## Factoring Higher-Order Difference of Squares

**Example:** Factor the expression $8t^4 - 72$.

**Explanation**

When faced with simplifying expanded polynomial expressions, our strategy should consist of first checking for like terms and factorability.

Here, since we have no like terms, we must consider factoring. First, notice that

$$
\operatorname{GCF}(8t^4, 72)=8
$$

and so we factor out an $8$ from both terms to obtain

$$
8t^4 - 72 = 8(t^4 - 9)
$$

Now, we observe that $t^4 - 9$ is a difference of squares:

$$
t^4 - 9 = (t^2)^2 - 3^2 = (t^2 + 3)(t^2 - 3)
$$

Next, we factor $t^2 - 3$ as another difference of squares:

$$
t^2 - 3 = (t + \sqrt{3})(t - \sqrt{3})
$$

So the complete factorization is
$8(t^2 + 3)(t + \sqrt{3})(t - \sqrt{3})$.

Since none of these factors can be reduced further, this is our fully factored equivalent expression.

## Factoring Biquadratic Trinomials as Perfect Squares

**Example:** Which expression is equivalent to $\big(x^2-2\sqrt{3}x+3\big)\big(x^2+2\sqrt{3}x+3\big)$?

1. $(x^2-3)^2$
2. $(x^2+3)^2$
3. $(x-1)^2(x+3)^2$
4. $\big(x-\sqrt{3}\big)^4$

**Explanation**

Notice that we have no like terms to collect, and so we begin by expanding the product and then collecting like terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x^{2} - 2\sqrt{3}x + 3)(x^{2} + 2\sqrt{3}x + 3) &= x^{2}(x^{2} + 2\sqrt{3}x + 3) - 2\sqrt{3}x(x^{2} + 2\sqrt{3}x + 3) + 3(x^{2} + 2\sqrt{3}x + 3) \\
&= x^{4} + 2\sqrt{3}x^{3} + 3x^{2} - 2\sqrt{3}x^{3} - 12x^{2} - 6\sqrt{3}x + 3x^{2} + 6\sqrt{3}x + 9 \\
&= x^{4} - 6x^{2} + 9
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, by making the substitution $y=x^2$, we have a factorable quadratic
$y^2-6y+9$.

The "square of a difference" formula states that

$$
y^2-{\color{red}{2}}{\color{blue}{a}}y+a^2=(y-{\color{blue}{a}})^2
$$

We will use this formula to factor our trinomial
$y^2-6y+9$.

First, we write the constant term as a perfect square:

$$
y^{2} - 6y + 3^{2}
$$

Then, we factor a $2$ out of the linear term:

$$
y^{2} - 2 \cdot 3y + 3^{2}
$$

Now, we factor the trinomial using the square of difference formula with

$$
{\color{blue}{a}} = {\color{blue}{3}}
$$

This gives

$$
(y - 3)^{2}
$$

Since $y=x^2$, we have

$$
(x^2-3)^2
$$

Therefore, the correct answer is expression "I."
