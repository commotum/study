## Introduction

This topic helps us build proficiency in solving equations containing multiple radicals. The key idea is to write the equation in such a way that squaring both sides eliminates the radicals. In some cases, we will find a solution in terms of an unknown parameter.

---

<a id="solving-equations-with-radicals-on-both-sides"></a>
## Solving Equations With Radicals on Both Sides

**Example:** What are the solutions to $\sqrt{x-x^2}=\sqrt{-3x-5}$?

**Explanation**

To solve for $x$, we first square both sides to remove the square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{x - x^{2}})^{2} &= (\sqrt{-3x - 5})^{2} \\
x - x^{2} &= -3x - 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we move all terms to one side and factor the result to obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
-3x - 5 &= x - x^{2} \\
x^{2} - 4x - 5 &= 0 \\
(x + 1)(x - 5) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

By the zero-product property, we have two candidates for solutions: $x=-1$ and $x=5$. Now, we must verify if they are indeed solutions by substituting them back into our original equation.

- For $x=-1$, we have
$\sqrt{-1 - (-1)^{2}}|= ^(?)\sqrt{-3(-1) - 5}; \sqrt{-1 - 1}|= ^(?)\sqrt{3 - 5}; \sqrt{-2}|= ^(?)\sqrt{-2}, \cdot$
which is undefined on both sides. Hence, $x=-1$ is not a valid solution.
- For $x=5$, we have
$\sqrt{5 - 5^{2}}|= ^(?)\sqrt{-3(5) - 5}; \sqrt{5 - 25}|= ^(?)\sqrt{-15 - 5}; \sqrt{-20}|= ^(?)\sqrt{-20}, \cdot$
which is undefined on both sides. Hence, $x=5$ is not a valid solution.

Therefore, the equation

$$
\sqrt{x-x^2}=\sqrt{-3x-5}
$$

has no solutions.

## Solving Equations With Radicals on Both Sides by Factoring

**Example:** $\sqrt{4q + 28} = \sqrt{(q - 1)^{2}}$

Find the smallest real solution of the equation given above.

**Explanation**

Squaring both sides of the given equation, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{4q + 28})^{2} &= (\sqrt{(q - 1)^{2}})^{2} \\
4q + 28 &= (q - 1)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, expanding the square, moving all terms to one side, and factoring, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
4q + 28 &= q^{2} - 2q + 1 \\
0 &= q^{2} - 6q - 27 \\
0 &= (q + 3)(q - 9)
\end{aligned}
$$

By the zero-product property, we have two possible solutions: $q=-3$ or $q=9$. Now, we must verify if they are indeed solutions, by substituting them back into our original equation.

- For $q=-3$, we have
$\sqrt{4(-3) + 28}|= ^(?)\sqrt{((-3) - 1)^{2}}; \sqrt{-12 + 28}|= ^(?)\sqrt{(-4)^{2}}; \sqrt{16}|= ^(?)\sqrt{16}.✓$
Hence, $q=-3$ *is* a valid solution.
- For $q=9$, we have
$\sqrt{4(9) + 28}|= ^(?)\sqrt{(9 - 1)^{2}}; \sqrt{36 + 28}|= ^(?)\sqrt{8^{2}}; \sqrt{64}|= ^(?)\sqrt{64}.✓$
Hence, $q=9$ *is* a valid solution.

So, the valid solutions are $q=-3$ and $q=9$.
Therefore, the smallest real solution is $q=-3$.

## Solving Equations With Three Radicals

**Example:** What are the solutions to $\sqrt{3x}+1-\sqrt{x-2}=\sqrt{x-2}$?

**Explanation**

To solve for $x$, we first combine like terms and square both sides to remove the square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{3x} + 1 - \sqrt{x - 2} &= \sqrt{x - 2} \\
\sqrt{3x} + 1 &= 2\sqrt{x - 2} \\
(\sqrt{3x} + 1)^{2} &= (2\sqrt{x - 2})^{2} \\
3x + 2\sqrt{3x} + 1 &= 4(x - 2)
\end{aligned}
$$

The resulting equation still contains a radical term. To solve, we isolate this term and square both sides again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
3x + 2\sqrt{3x} + 1 &= 4x - 8 \\
2\sqrt{3x} &= x - 9 \\
(2\sqrt{3x})^{2} &= (x - 9)^{2} \\
12x &= x^{2} - 18x + 81 \\
0 &= x^{2} - 30x + 81 \\
x^{2} - 30x + 81 &= 0 \\
(x - 27)(x - 3) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have two candidates for solutions: $x=27$ and $x=3$. Now, we must verify if they are indeed solutions by substituting them back into our original equation.

- For $x=27$, we have
$\sqrt{3 \cdot 27} + 1 - \sqrt{27 - 2}|= ^(?)\sqrt{27 - 2}; \sqrt{81} + 1 - \sqrt{25}|= ^(?)\sqrt{25}; 9 + 1 - 5|= ^(?)5; 5|= 5.✓$
Hence, $x=27$ is a valid solution.
- For $x=3$, we have
$\sqrt{3 \cdot 3} + 1 - \sqrt{3 - 2}|= ^(?)\sqrt{3 - 2}; \sqrt{9} + 1 - \sqrt{1}|= ^(?)\sqrt{1}; 3 + 1 - 1|= ^(?)1; 3 \mid \ne 1.\cdot$
Hence, $x=3$ is not a valid solution.

Therefore, the solution to

$$
\sqrt{3x}+1-\sqrt{x-2}=\sqrt{x-2}
$$

is $x=27$ only.

<a id="solving-radical-equations-containing-unknown-parameters"></a>
## Solving Radical Equations Containing Unknown Parameters

**Example:** Consider the equation
$\dfrac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} = \dfrac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} + 4$,
where $k$ is a positive constant. What are the solutions to this equation?

**Explanation**

All terms have the same square root in the denominator. So, multiplying through by $\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} \cdot \sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}} &= (\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} + 4) \cdot \sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}} \\
\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} \cdot \sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}} &= \frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}} \cdot \sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}} + 4 \cdot \sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}} \\
r^{2} &= k^{2} + 4\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we subtract $k^2$ from both sides:

$$
r^2 - k^2 = 4 \sqrt{r^2 - k^2}
$$

Now, let

$$
u = \sqrt{r^2 - k^2}
$$

Then, substituting

$$
u^2= r^2 - k^2
$$

into the equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
u^{2} &= 4u \\
u^{2} - 4u &= 0 \\
u(u - 4) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

By the zero-product property, the possible values of $u$ are

$$
u = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad u = 4
$$

Now, we find the corresponding potential solutions for $r$ and verify if they are valid solutions.

- If $u=0$, then we obtain
$\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}|= 0; r^{2}|= k^{2}; r|= ± k$.
However, plugging $r = k$ or $r = -k$ into the original equation makes the denominator zero. Hence, $u=0$, or equivalently, $r=\pm k$, is *not* a valid solution.
- If $u=4$, then we obtain
$\sqrt{r^{2} - k^{2}}|= 4; r^{2} - k^{2}|= 16; r^{2}|= k^{2} + 16; r|= ± \sqrt{k^{2} + 16}$.
Plugging this into the original equation, we get
$\frac{k^{2} + 16}{\sqrt{k^{2} + 16 - k^{2}}}|= ^(?)\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{k^{2} + 16 - k^{2}}} + 4; \frac{k^{2} + 16}{\sqrt{16}}|= ^(?)\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{16}} + 4; \frac{k^{2} + 16}{4}|= ^(?)\frac{k^{2}}{4} + 4; \frac{k^{2}}{4} + 4|= ^(?)\frac{k^{2}}{4} + 4.✓$
Hence, $u=4$, or equivalently, $r=\pm \sqrt{k^2 + 16}$, *is* a valid solution.
