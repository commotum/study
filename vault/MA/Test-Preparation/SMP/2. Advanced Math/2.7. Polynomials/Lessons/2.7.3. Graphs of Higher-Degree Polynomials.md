## Introduction

In this topic, we'll use our knowledge of polynomial graphs to determine their properties, such as the number of roots and valid outputs of polynomials over particular intervals. We'll also practice identifying the factored form of a polynomial from its graph.

## Counting Distinct Roots of Polynomials Using Graphs

**Example:** The graph of $y = f(x)$ is shown, where the function $f$ is defined by $f(x) = ax^{3} + bx^{2} + cx + d$ and $a, b, c, d$ are constants. For how many distinct values of $x$ does $f(x) = 0$?

![](<../Source/2.7.3. Graphs of Higher-Degree Polynomials/Images/8d0906b8bc1b7fdef3a3ee054638f939.png>)

*(Note: Figures not drawn to scale)*

**Explanation**

We are given the graph of a cubic function and asked how many values of $x$ are such that

$$
f(x) = 0
$$

Note that

$$
f(x) = 0
$$

if and only if the graph of $f$ passes through the point $(x,0)$. Now, by observing the graph, we can see that the curve passes through the points $(-2,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(4,0)$.

Given that the sum of the multiplicities of the roots for a third-degree polynomial is $3$, then all roots have multiplicity $1$ and there are no other roots.

Therefore, there are **three** values where

$$
f(x) = 0
$$

These values are $x = -2$, $x = 1$, and $x = 4$.

## Identifying Valid Outputs of Factored Polynomials

**Example:** $y=(x-3)(x^2+x+2)$
In the $xy$-plane, the graph of the equation above contains the point $(a,b)$. If $1 \leq a \leq 3$, which of the following is **NOT** a possible value of $b$?

1. $-1$
2. $0$
3. $2$

**Explanation**

First, notice that the discriminant of the factor $x^2+x+2$ is

$$
\mathcal D = 1^2-4(1)(2) = -7 < 0
$$

so our cubic cannot be factored any further over the real numbers.

Now, we sketch the graph of the cubic. From the equation, we conclude the following:

- The graph has an $x$-intercept at $x=3$.
- $y \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$ and $y \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.
- The $y$-intercept is
$y|= (0 - 3)(0^{2} + 0 + 2); = (-3) \cdot (2); =-6$.

The corresponding partial graph is shown below.

![](<../Source/2.7.3. Graphs of Higher-Degree Polynomials/Images/5e36e4c2ffbf69a119384257c478198a.png>)

We are interested in the points $(a,b)$ whose $x$-coordinate $a$ satisfies

$$
1 \leq a \leq 3
$$

The corresponding interval on the $x$-axis is highlighted on the graph.
Notice that in this interval, the graph of the cubic lies below the $x$-axis. So, we must have

$$
b \leq 0
$$

Among the given options, only option III does **NOT** satisfy this condition.

## Graphs of General Polynomials

**Example:** Write the equation that could represent the graph shown in the $xy$-plane?

![](<../Source/2.7.3. Graphs of Higher-Degree Polynomials/Images/6bd413ca66d5c94dc817fd0d0e67ed1a.png>)

*(Note: Figures not drawn to scale)*

**Explanation**

First, we notice that the function has three distinct roots, $x = -4$, $x = 0$, and $x = 3$.

Based on the graph, we can make the following conclusions about the multiplicities of the roots:

- The graph is *not* tangent to the $x$-axis at $x = -4$. Therefore, this root is simple (the multiplicity equals $1$).
- The graph touches the $x$-axis and reverses direction at $x = 0$. Therefore, this root has even multiplicity.
- The graph is *not* tangent to the $x$-axis at $x = 3$. Therefore, this root is simple (the multiplicity equals $1$).

Therefore, the equation we need to find has the form

$$
f(x) = a(x + 4)x^n(x - 3)
$$

where $n$ is an even natural number.

Of the given options, the following are similar to the form described above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \frac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^{2}(x - 3), \\
f(x) &= -\frac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^{2}(x - 3)
\end{aligned}
$$

From the diagram, we can notice that the value of the function at $x=1$ is negative, that is, $f(1) \lt 0$.

- For $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^2(x - 3)$, we have
$f(1)|= \frac{1}{3}(1 + 4)(1)^{2}(1 - 3); =-\frac{10}{3}; < 0$.
- For $f(x) = -\dfrac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^2(x - 3)$, we have
$f(1)|=-\frac{1}{3}(1 + 4)(1)^{2}(1 - 3); = \frac{10}{3}; ≮0$.

Therefore, we discard

$$
f(x) = -\dfrac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^2(x - 3)
$$

Consequently, the equation that could represent the graph shown is

$$
f(x) = \dfrac{1}{3}(x + 4)x^2(x - 3)
$$
