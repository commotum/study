## Introduction

In this topic, we'll practice applying the zero-product rule to higher-order polynomials such as cubics and quartics and using the information gained to determine properties such as the number of roots and intercepts of a polynomial.

## Solving Factored Polynomial Equations Using the Zero Product Rule

**Example:** Consider the equation
$(y + 3)(y + 1)(y - 5) = 0$.
What is the product of all solutions to the equation above?

**Explanation**

To find the solutions to the given equation, we use the zero-product property. This property states that if a product of factors equals zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero.

Setting each factor equal to zero, we get

- $y + 3 = 0$ gives $y = -3$,
- $y + 1 = 0$ gives $y = -1$, and
- $y - 5 = 0$ gives $y = 5$.

Now, the product of these solutions is

$$
(-3)(-1)(5) = 15
$$

Therefore, the product of the solutions is $15$.

## Finding the X-Intercepts of a Factored Polynomial

**Example:** What is an $x$-coordinate of an $x$-intercept of the graph of $y = -6(x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 5)$ in the $xy$-plane?

**Explanation**

Remember that the $x$-intercepts of the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ are the points at which the graph intersects the $x$-axis. The $x$-coordinates of these points are obtained by setting $y = 0$ and solving for $x$.

Substituting $y = 0$ into the given equation, we obtain

$$
-6(x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 5) = 0
$$

or equivalently,

$$
(x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 5) = 0
$$

To find the solutions to the last equation, we apply the zero-product property. This property states that if a product of factors equals zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero.

Setting each factor equal to zero, we get

- $x + 2 = 0$ gives $x = -2$,
- $x - 3 = 0$ gives $x = 3$, and
- $x + 5 = 0$ gives $x = -5$.

Therefore, among the choices, the only possible $x$-coordinate of an $x$-intercept is $-2$.

## Counting the Number of X-Intercepts of a Polynomial Function

**Example:** In the equation
$y = 8(x + a)(x - a)(x - b)(x+a)$,
$a$ and $b$ are distinct positive constants. When the equation is graphed in the $xy$-plane, how many **distinct** $x$-intercepts does the graph have?

**Explanation**

Remember that the $x$-intercepts of the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ are the points at which the graph intersects the $x$-axis. The $x$-coordinates of these points are obtained by setting $y = 0$ and solving for $x$.

Substituting $y = 0$ into the given equation, we obtain

$$
8(x + a)(x - a)(x - b)(x+a) = 0
$$

or equivalently,

$$
(x + a)^2(x - a)(x - b) = 0
$$

To find the solutions to the last equation, we apply the zero-product property. This property states that if a product of factors equals zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero.

Setting each factor equal to zero, we get

- $(x + a)^2 = 0$ gives $x = -a$,
- $x - a = 0$ gives $x = a$, and
- $x - b = 0$ gives $x = b$.

Now, we are given that $a$ and $b$ are distinct positive constants. This implies that

$$
-a \neq a
$$

$$
-a \neq b
$$

and

$$
a \neq b
$$

Therefore, the equation

$$
8(x + a)^2(x - a)(x - b) = 0
$$

has $3$ unique solutions: $x = -a$, $x = a$, and $x = b$.

Thus, the graph of the given equation has $3$ distinct $x$-intercepts.

## Finding a Cubic Function in Expanded Form Given Its Roots

**Example:** Consider the function
$h(x) = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$,
where $a, b$, and $c$ are integer constants. If the zeros of the function are $-5, -2$, and $-1$, what is the value of $c$?

**Explanation**

First, note that the cubic polynomial defining the function $h$ has a leading coefficient equal to $1$. Taking this into account and the fact that the zeros of the function $h$ are $-5, -2$, and $-1$, we can write $h$ as

$$
h(x) = (x + 5)(x + 2)(x + 1)
$$

Expanding the expression on the right-hand side of the last equality, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x) &= (x + 5)(x + 2)(x + 1) \\
&= (x + 5)(x^{2} + 3x + 2) \\
&= x^{3} + 5x^{2} + 3x^{2} + 15x + 2x + 10 \\
&= x^{3} + 8x^{2} + 17x + 10
\end{aligned}
$$

Comparing this to the expression

$$
h(x) = x^{3} + ax^{2} + bx + c
$$

we conclude that $c = 10$.
