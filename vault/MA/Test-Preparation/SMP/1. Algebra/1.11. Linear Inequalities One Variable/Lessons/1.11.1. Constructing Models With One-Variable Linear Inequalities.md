## Introduction

A common theme on the math section of the SAT is modeling real-world situations with inequalities.

To prepare for this, we'll go over constructing one-variable linear inequalities to represent situations involving quantities and averages. In some questions, the linear inequality may have variables on both sides.

---

<a id="modeling-situations-using-linear-inequalities"></a>
## Modeling Situations Using Linear Inequalities

**Example:** A rectangular rug has a fixed width of $28$ inches and a variable length of $\ell$ inches. If the perimeter of the rug must be no more than $210$ inches, then find the inequality that represents this condition.

**Explanation**

The perimeter $P$ of a rectangle is given by the formula

$$
P = 2 \cdot \text{length} + 2 \cdot \text{width}
$$

We are told the width is $28$ inches and the length is $\ell$ inches, so we substitute this into our expression as follows:

$$
P = 2(\ell) + 2(28)
$$

Next, we simplify the expression:

$$
P = 2\ell + 56
$$

We are also told that the perimeter must be *no more than* $210$ inches. This translates to the following inequality:

$$
2\ell + 56 \leq 210
$$

So, the correct inequality is

$$
2\ell + 56 \leq 210
$$

## Modeling Averages Using Linear Inequalities

**Example:** A small factory produced $\[math]72$, $\[/math]89$, $\[math]96$, $\[/math]81$, and $\[math]74$ worth of parts over five of the last six days. Find the inequality that represents the condition that the factory’s average daily production must be greater than $\[/math]85$.

**Explanation**

To find the mean of all six production values, we first compute the sum of the five known values and the unknown one:

$$
\text{Total amount} = 72 + 89 + 96 + 81 + 74 + S
$$

Next, we divide this total by $6$ to calculate the average:

$$
\frac{72 + 89 + 96 + 81 + 74 + S}{6}
$$

Since the average must be greater than $\[math]85$, we write the inequality

$$
\frac{72 + 89 + 96 + 81 + 74 + S}{6} > 85
$$

This inequality represents the condition that the factory’s average daily production must be greater than $\[/math]85$.

## Modeling With Linear Inequalities Containing Variables on Both Sides

**Example:** A hospital spends an average of $\[math]9,600$ per year on outsourced medical transcription. It plans to invest $\[/math]30,000$ in voice recognition software. After the upgrade, the annual transcription cost is expected to decrease to $\[math]4,800$. Find the inequality that can be solved to find $t$, the number of years after installation when the total savings will exceed the cost of the upgrade?

**Explanation**

We are told that the hospital currently spends $\[math]9,600$ per year and expects to reduce this cost to $\[/math]4,800$ per year after the upgrade.

So, the annual savings is

$$
9,600 - 4,800 = 4,800 \text{dollars}
$$

Let $t$ represent the number of years after the upgrade.

Then, the total savings after $t$ years is

$$
4,800t
$$

We are told the upgrade costs $\[math]30,000$, and we want to find when the *total savings exceeds the upgrade cost*. So, we write

$$
4,800t > 30,000
$$

This is the inequality that can be solved to find $t$. Rearranging this, we could also write

$$
30,000 < 4,800t
$$

Finally, since

$$
9,600 - 4,800 = 4,800
$$

we can write this inequality as

$$
30,000 < (9,600 - 4,800)t
$$

## Modeling Situations With Distractors Using Linear Inequalities

**Example:** Dev can either paddle across a lake, which takes $27$ minutes, or take a ferry that takes $9$ minutes. The ferry comes every $32$ minutes, and the number of minutes $f$, that Dev waits for the next ferry can be any value from $0$ to $32$.

Find the inequality that represents the values of $f$ for which it is faster for Dev to take the ferry than to paddle?

**Explanation**

We are told that Dev has two travel options:

- Paddling takes a fixed time of $27$ minutes.
- Taking the ferry involves waiting $f$ minutes and riding for $9$ minutes.

So, the total time for the ferry trip is $f + 9$ minutes.

We want to find when this ferry time is *less than* the paddling time:

$$
f + 9 < 27
$$

This inequality represents the values of $f$ for which the total ferry trip is faster than paddling.
