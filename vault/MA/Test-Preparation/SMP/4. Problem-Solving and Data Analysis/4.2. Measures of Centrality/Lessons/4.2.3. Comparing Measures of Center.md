## Introduction

An important concept on the math section of the SAT exam is understanding how the mean and median relate to each other.

Here, we will determine which measures can be calculated, find means and medians of consecutive integers, and use relationships between them to find missing values.

<a id="determining-calculable-measures"></a>
## Determining Calculable Measures

**Example:** Five salespeople made a total of $\[math]1,000$ in sales. It is known that one of the salespeople made $\[/math]400$ in sales. Which of the following can be determined from this information?

1. The average sales per person.
2. The greatest sales made by any one salesperson.
3. The least sales made by any one salesperson.

**Explanation**

The average (or mean) is calculated by dividing the sum of the data points by the number of data points.

Now, let's consider each statement in turn:

- Consider statement I. We are given the total sales amount $(\[math]1,000)$ and the number of salespeople $(5)$. So, we can compute the average, given by
$\text{Average} = \dfrac{1,000}{5} = \[/math]200$.
Therefore, the average sales per person *can* be determined.
- Consider statement II. We know one salesperson made $\[math]400$, but we do not know the sales amounts of the others. It is possible that another salesperson made more. Therefore, the greatest sales *cannot* be determined from the given information.
- Consider statement III. Similarly, we cannot determine the least sales without knowing the sales for each person. Therefore, the least sales *cannot* be determined from the given information.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I only".

## Finding the Mean and Median of Consecutive Integers

**Example:** If $c$ is the mean and $d$ is the median of six consecutive negative integers, what is the value of $\dfrac{d}{c}$?

**Explanation**

Let $x$ be the largest of the numbers. Then, the six consecutive negative integers are

$$
x - 5,\quad x - 4,\quad x - 3,\quad x - 2,\quad x - 1,\quad x
$$

So, the mean is

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= ((x - 5) + (x - 4) + (x - 3) + (x - 2) + (x - 1) + x)/(6) \\
&= \frac{6x - 15}{6} \\
&= x - 2.5
\end{aligned}
$$

Since there are $6$ values, the median is the average of the third and fourth values:

$$
x - 5,\quad x - 4,\quad \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{x - 3},\quad \bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{x - 2},\quad x - 1,\quad x
$$

Thus, the median is

$$
\begin{aligned}
d &= ((x - 3) + (x - 2))/(2) \\
&= \frac{2x - 5}{2} \\
&= x - 2.5
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{d}{c} = \dfrac{x - 2.5}{x - 2.5} ={1}
$$

## Finding an Unknown Element Given a Relation Between the Mean and Median

**Example:** $11,\: w,\: 14,\: 16,\: 20$
The mean and the median of the numbers above are not equal. Which of the following could be the value of $w$?

1. $13$
2. $14$
3. $19$

**Explanation**

We are told that the mean and median of the five numbers are not equal.

Let’s begin by writing an expression for the mean. The mean of the five values is

$$
\dfrac{11 + w + 14 + 16 + 20}{5} = \dfrac{61+w}{5}
$$

Also, since there are five numbers, the median will be the $3$rd value when the values are arranged in order from smallest to greatest.

With this in mind, let’s test each value of $w$ in turn.

- If $w = 13$, then the list arranged in order from smallest to greatest is
$11,\: 13,\: 14,\: 16,\: 20$.
In this case, the median is $14$, and the mean is
$\frac{61 + 13}{5} = \frac{74}{5} = 14.8$.
Since the mean and median are not equal, $13$ *is* a possible value of $w$.
- If $w = 14$, then the list arranged in order from smallest to greatest is
$11,\: 14,\: 14,\: 16,\: 20$.
In this case, the median is $14$, and the mean is
$\frac{61 + 14}{5} = \frac{75}{5} = 15$.
Since the mean and median are not equal, $14$ *is* a possible value of $w$.
- If $w = 19$, then the list arranged in order from smallest to greatest is
$11,\: 14,\: 16,\: 19,\: 20$.
In this case, the median is $16$, and the mean is
$\frac{61 + 19}{5} = \frac{80}{5} = 16$.
Since the mean and median are equal, $19$ *is not* a possible value of $w$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only".
