## Introduction

In this topic, we'll focus on interpreting survey results. We will determine how broadly results can be generalized, identify overgeneralizations or unsupported claims, and decide which conclusions are logically supported by the data.

<a id="finding-best-estimates-from-sample-data"></a>
## Finding Best Estimates From Sample Data

**Example:** At a beach event, $2{,}900$ people attended. The organizer randomly surveyed $145$ of them and found that $29$ bought sunglasses. Which of the following is the most appropriate conclusion?

1. Exactly $580$ attendees must have bought sunglasses.
2. Exactly $290$ attendees must have bought sunglasses.
3. The best estimate for the number of attendees who bought sunglasses is $580$.
4. The best estimate for the number of attendees who bought sunglasses is $290$.

**Explanation**

From the random sample, the proportion who bought sunglasses is

$$
\frac{29}{145} = 0.20 = 20\%
$$

So, $20\%$ of those sampled bought sunglasses.

To estimate how many of the $2{,}900$ attendees bought sunglasses, we scale by this proportion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2,900 \cdot 20% &= 2,900 \cdot 0.20 \\
&= 580
\end{aligned}
$$

Because this is based on a sample, we report it as a *best estimate,* not an exact count.

Therefore, the most appropriate conclusion is, "The best estimate for the number of attendees who bought sunglasses is $580$."

## Identifying Appropriate Conclusions From Survey Data

**Example:** A psychologist studied the social media habits of teenagers in a city. A random sample of 300 teens was taken, and 40% of the teens in the sample reported spending more than 3 hours per day on social media.

Based on this information, what conclusion can reasonably be made about teenagers in this city?

**Explanation**

The statistics obtained from a random sample can be used to make inferences about the population *from which the sample is made*. We cannot infer anything about teenagers in other cities or about different age groups.

Because the sample was chosen randomly, it is reasonable to use the results to make an inference about all teenagers in this city.

The data show that $40\%$ of the teens in the sample reported spending more than $3$ hours per day on social media. This does not imply anything about the average time, nor does it imply anything about adults in general.

The valid conclusion is that approximately $40\%$ of all teenagers in this city spend more than $3$ hours per day on social media.

## Identifying True Statements Given Survey Data

**Example:** A city government surveyed $240$ residents chosen at random and asked, "Are you satisfied with the garbage collection schedule?" Of those surveyed, $58\%$ responded that they were satisfied. Based on the results of the survey, which of the following statements **must** be true?

1. Of all residents in the city, $58\%$ are satisfied with the garbage collection schedule.
2. If another $240$ residents were randomly surveyed in the city, exactly $58\%$ of them would report satisfaction with the garbage collection schedule.
3. If $240$ residents in a different city were surveyed, $58\%$ would report satisfaction with their garbage collection schedule.

**Explanation**

When generalizing survey results, the population cannot extend beyond the group from which the sample was drawn.

The survey tells us that in one sample of $240$ residents, $58\%$ reported satisfaction. This does not mean we know the exact opinion of the entire city or that another sample would give the same percentage.

When interpreting survey results, it is essential to distinguish between what the sample tells us and what we can conclude about the population.

- Statement I extends the sample percentage to the entire resident population. This is not guaranteed, since sampling cannot prove the exact population proportion.
- Statement II assumes that a new random sample of the same size would always yield exactly $58\%$. In practice, sampling variability makes this impossible to guarantee.
- Statement III applies the result to a completely different city, which is not supported by the data.

Therefore, none of the three statements must be true.

## Identifying the Largest Population to Which Inferences Can Be Made

**Example:** A technology researcher designed a study to test whether a new customer service chatbot improves satisfaction ratings. For the study, the researcher randomly selected $70$ users from one company’s website. The researcher found that users with access to the chatbot gave higher ratings. What is the largest group to which the results of the study can be generalized?

**Explanation**

When generalizing study results, the group to which the results apply cannot extend beyond the population from which the sample was drawn.

The study sample was specifically drawn from *users of one company’s website*:

- While the study results apply to the $70$ users who were in the sample, this is not the largest group to which the findings can be generalized.
- We cannot generalize to all website users in the city, because the sample came from just one company’s site.
- We cannot generalize to all website users in the state, because the sample was limited to one company.

Therefore, the largest group to which the results of the study can be generalized is

$$
\text{All users of the company’s website.}
$$

## Identifying True Statements About Resampling

**Example:** A nonprofit surveyed $600$ volunteers who were selected at random from its membership list and asked, “Do you plan to volunteer at least once in the next three months?” Of those surveyed, $35\%$ responded “no”.

Based on this result, which of the following conclusions are appropriate?

1. Of all volunteers in the nonprofit, exactly $35\%$ do not plan to volunteer at least once in the next three months.
2. If another $600$ volunteers selected at random from the nonprofit were surveyed, the proportion who say “no” is likely to be close to $35\%$.
3. If $600$ volunteers selected at random from a different nonprofit were surveyed, the proportion who say “no” is likely to be close to $35\%$.

**Explanation**

Because the $600$ volunteers were chosen *at random* from the nonprofit, the $35\%$ is a *sample proportion* that serves as a *best estimate* for the population proportion in this nonprofit. The exact value for the whole population is unknown, but another random sample from the *same nonprofit* would be expected to give a result close to $35\%$ (though not necessarily exactly $35\%$).

- Statement I is not an appropriate conclusion. The true proportion in the nonprofit may differ slightly from $35\%$ due to sampling variability.
- Statement II is an appropriate conclusion. Repeating the sampling process with the same method and sample size from the same nonprofit would likely produce a proportion close to $35\%$.
- Statement III is not an appropriate conclusion. The inference applies only to this nonprofit, not to a different nonprofit that could have a different population proportion.

Therefore, the correct answer is “II only.”
