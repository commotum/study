## Introduction

Sometimes, questions about data plots may test our ability to read and interpret information from graphs such as scatterplots and line graphs.

In this topic, we’ll practice extracting information from visual data, including estimating values, identifying trends, and finding maximum and minimum points.

---

<a id="identifying-a-dependent-variable-value-from-a-scatter-plot"></a>
## Identifying a Dependent Variable Value From a Scatter Plot

**Example:** A household recorded the total water used, in gallons, during each day after a new meter was installed. The scatterplot shows this amount, in gallons, for each day after installation.

What was the amount, rounded to the nearest gallon, of all water used during the $5$th day after installation?

![](<../Source/4.7.1. Interpreting Data Plots/Images/1b0d9017b1a49ef1add3bc655f09f9e8.png>)

**Explanation**

To predict the amount at the $5$th day after installation, we must find the $y$-coordinate of the point whose $x$-coordinate is $5$.

From the graph, we can deduce that the amount, rounded to the nearest gallon, of all water used during the $5$th day after installation was $100$ gallons.

## Identifying an Independent Variable Value From a Data Plot

**Example:** The line graph shows the number of website visitors for each day during a six-day period. According to the line graph, for which day during this six-day period is the number of visitors $1{,}200$?

![](<../Source/4.7.1. Interpreting Data Plots/Images/e4732793e6daa0d6207d2b3c045e748a.png>)

**Explanation**

To find the requested day, we locate the plotted point with a $y$-value $1{,}200$ and read its corresponding day on the $x$-axis.

Therefore, from the graph, the number of visitors $1{,}200$ occurs on Thursday.

## Making Predictions From Scatter Plots

**Example:** The crop yields for different fertilizer concentrations are shown in the scatterplot below. According to the trend shown by the data, which of the following is closest to the predicted yield, in tons per hectare $(\text{t/ha})$, for fertilizer at $45\%$?

![](<../Source/4.7.1. Interpreting Data Plots/Images/e5134b523a23d9aad587f074a95bf15e.png>)

1. $3.3\text{t/ha}$
2. $3.9\text{t/ha}$
3. $3.6\text{t/ha}$
4. $4.1\text{t/ha}$

**Explanation**

The data in the scatterplot show an increasing, almost linear trend.

From the graph, the yield at $45\%$ concentration will be between the yields shown at about $40\%$ and $50\%$, or between about $3.4$ and $3.8\text{t/ha}$.

Therefore, of the choices given, only $3.6\text{t/ha}$ falls within this range.

<a id="interpolating-data-by-calculating-an-average-y-value"></a>
## Interpolating Data by Calculating an Average Y-Value

**Example:** A safety test was conducted to determine how far a motorcycle takes to stop when traveling at different speeds. The test was conducted in winter, and the results are shown in the quadratic model plotted above.

![](<../Source/4.7.1. Interpreting Data Plots/Images/182ca7910f88613f7dc6ff632d52acde.png>)

According to the model, which of the following is the best estimate for the stopping distance, in feet, if the motorcycle was traveling $35\,\textrm{mph}$?

1. $95$
2. $120$
3. $130$
4. $105$

**Explanation**

From the graph, we note the following:

- At $30 \, \text{mph}$, the stopping distance is about $85 \, \text{ft}$, and
- At $40 \, \text{mph}$, the stopping distance is about $130 \, \text{ft}$.

Since $35\,\text{mph}$ is a value between $30\,\text{mph}$ and $40 \, \text{mph}$, we can estimate the stopping distance by finding the mean of the corresponding distances for these two speeds:

$$
\frac{85\, \text{ft} + 130\, \text{ft}}{2} = 107.5\, \text{ft}
$$

Therefore, among the choices, the closest estimate for the stopping distance at $35 \text{mph}$ is about $105 \,\text{ft}$.
