## Introduction

On the math section of the SAT exam, questions that describe physical relationships usually test our ability to translate written definitions into mathematical formulas.

In this lesson, we’ll practice turning descriptions of physical phenomena into equations, using the given definitions to solve for unknown variables, and reinforcing formula reasoning through applied examples.

<a id="interpreting-a-physical-formula-from-a-description"></a>
## Interpreting a Physical Formula From a Description

**Example:** The current density of a wire is defined as the amount of electric current, in microamperes, that passes perpendicularly through the wire’s cross-sectional surface, divided by the area of that surface, measured in square meters. What is the current density, in microamperes per square meter, of a wire with a cross-sectional area of $0.004$ square meters carrying a current of $12$ microamperes?

**Explanation**

First, let's examine the definition that's given to us:

> The current density of a wire is defined as the **amount of electric current,** in microamperes, that passes perpendicularly through the wire’s cross-sectional surface, **divided by** the **area of that surface**, measured in square meters.

So, let's define the following variables:

- $J$ is the current density, in microamperes per square meter.
- $I$ is the current, in microamperes
- $A$ is the cross-sectional area, in square meters.

From the definition that's given, we have that the current density is the current divided by the area:

$$
J = \frac{I}{A}
$$

We can check that the formula looks correct by comparing the units on each side of this formula:

$$
\underbrace{\textrm{microamperes per square meter}}_{\textrm{units of} J} = \underbrace{\dfrac{\textrm{microamperes}}{\textrm{square meters}}}_{\textrm{units of} I/A}
$$

The units on both sides are the same, so our formula looks correct.

Now, we are given the following data:

- $I = 12$ microamperes
- $A = 0.004$ square meters

Substituting these values into our formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
J &= \frac{12microamperes}{0.004square meters} \\
&= 3000microamperes per square meter
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the current density is $\bbox[4pt, border: 1px solid gainsboro]{3000}$ microamperes per square meter.

## Interpreting a Physical Formula From a Description and Solving for a Variable

**Example:** The magnetic flux density on a surface is defined as the magnetic flux, in webers, passing perpendicularly through the surface, divided by the area of the surface, measured in square meters. If the magnetic flux density on a surface is $10$ webers per square meter and the area of the surface is $7$ square meters, what is the magnetic flux through the surface, in webers?

**Explanation**

First, let's examine the definition that's given to us:

> The magnetic flux density on a surface is defined as the **magnetic flux**, in webers, passing perpendicularly through the surface, **divided by** the **area of the surface**, measured in square meters.

So, let's define the following variables:

- $B$ is the magnetic flux density, in webers per square meter.
- $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux, in webers.
- $A$ is the area of the surface, in square meters.

From the definition that's given, we have that

$$
B = \frac{\Phi}{A}
$$

We can check that the formula looks correct by comparing the units on each side of this formula:

$$
\underbrace{\textrm{webers per square meter}}_{\textrm{units of} B} = \underbrace{\dfrac{\textrm{webers}}{\textrm{square meters}}}_{\textrm{units of} \Phi/A}
$$

The units on both sides are the same, so our formula looks correct.

Now, we are given the following data:

- $B = 10$ webers per square meter
- $A = 7$ square meters

Substituting these values into our formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \frac{Φ}{A} \\
10 &= \frac{Φ}{7}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying both sides by $7$, we find

$$
\Phi = 10 \cdot 7 = 70\,\,\textrm{webers}
$$

So, the magnetic flux is $70$ webers.

## Calculating a Physical Property From a Description

**Example:** The magnetic flux of a magnetic field passing perpendicularly through a surface is defined as the product of the magnetic field strength, in webers per square meter, and the area of the surface, measured in square meters. What is the magnetic flux, in webers, through a rectangular surface whose width is four times its length, if the length is $1.5$ meters and the magnetic field strength is $4$ weber per square meter?

**Explanation**

First, let's examine the definition that's given to us:

> The magnetic flux of a magnetic field passing perpendicularly through a surface is defined as the **product** of the **magnetic field strength**, in webers per square meter, and the **area of the surface,** measured in square meters.

So, let's define the following variables:

- $\Phi$ is the magnetic flux, in webers.
- $B$ is the magnetic field strength, in webers per square meter.
- $A$ is the area of the surface, in square meters.
- $\ell$ is the length of the rectangle, in meters.
- $w$ is the width of the rectangle, in meters.

From the definition that's given, we have that

$$
\Phi = B \cdot A
$$

We can check that the formula looks correct by comparing the units:

$$
\underbrace{\textrm{webers}}_{\textrm{units of} \Phi} = \underbrace{(\textrm{webers per square meter})(\textrm{square meters})}_{\textrm{units of} B \cdot A}
$$

The units on both sides are the same, so our formula looks correct.

Since the width is four times the length, we have $w = 4\ell$. Therefore,

$$
A = \ell \cdot w = \ell \cdot (4\ell) = 4\ell^2
$$

Now, we are given the following data:

- $\ell = 1.5$ meters
- $B = 4$ weber per square meter

Therefore,

$$
A = 4 \cdot (1.5)^2 = 9\ \text{square meters}
$$

Substituting these values into our formula for $\Phi$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
Φ &= 4 \cdot 9 = 36webers
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the magnetic flux is $36$ webers.

## Calculating a Length From a Physical Description

**Example:** The volume flux of a fluid through a surface perpendicular to its motion is defined as the product of the cross-sectional area of the surface, in square meters, and the velocity of the fluid, in meters per second. If the volume flux of water through a circular cross-section of a pipe is $180$ cubic meters per second and the water moves at a velocity of $15$ meters per second, what is the radius of the cross-section, in meters? Round your answer to one decimal place.

**Explanation**

First, let's examine the definition that's given to us:

> The volume flux of a fluid through a surface perpendicular to its motion is defined as the **product** of the **cross-sectional area** of the surface, in square meters, and the **velocity of the fluid,** in meters per second.

So, let's define the following variables:

- $F$ is the volume flux, in cubic meters per second.
- $A$ is the cross-sectional area, in square meters.
- $v$ is the velocity of the fluid, in meters per second.
- $r$ is the radius of the circular cross-section, in meters.

From the definition that's given, we have that

$$
F = A \cdot v
$$

and since the cross-sectional area is a circle, we also have

$$
A = \pi r^2
$$

Now, we are given the following data:

- $F = 180$ cubic meters per second
- $v = 15$ meters per second

First, solve for the area by rearranging

$$
F = A \cdot v
$$

:

$$
A = \frac{F}{v} = \frac{180}{15} = 12
$$

Next, substitute this into the circle area formula:

$$
\pi r^2 = 12
$$

Solving for $r$, we get

$$
r = \sqrt{\frac{12}{\pi}} \approx 2.0
$$

So, the radius of the cross-section is approximately $2.0$ meters, rounded to one decimal place.

## Rates of Change in Physical Phenomena

**Example:** The force exerted on a stretched spring is defined as the product of the spring constant, in newtons per meter, and the extension of the spring, in meters. A spring has a natural length of $0.46$ meters. If the spring constant is $120$ newtons per meter and the spring experiences a force of $30$ newtons, what is the stretched length of the spring, in meters?

**Explanation**

First, let's examine the definition that's given to us:

> The force exerted on a stretched spring is defined as the **product** of the **spring constant**, in newtons per meter, and the **extension of the spring**, in meters.

So, let's define the following variables:

- $F$ is the force exerted on the spring, in newtons.
- $k$ is the spring constant, in newtons per meter.
- $\Delta x$ is the extension of the spring, in meters.
- $L_0$ is the natural length of the spring, in meters.
- $L$ is the stretched length of the spring, in meters.

The extension of the spring equals the change in length of the spring:

$$
\Delta x = L - L_0
$$

Therefore, the force is related to the extension by

$$
F = k \cdot \Delta x
$$

We can check that the formula looks correct by comparing the units:

$$
\underbrace{\textrm{newtons}}_{\textrm{units of} F} = \underbrace{(\textrm{newtons per meter})(\textrm{meters})}_{\textrm{units of} k \cdot \Delta x}
$$

The units on both sides are the same, so our formula looks correct.

Now, we are given the following information:

- $F = 30$ newtons
- $k = 120$ newtons per meter
- $L_0 = 0.46$ meters

Rearranging the formula for the extension, we get

$$
\Delta x = \frac{F}{k}
$$

Substituting the given values:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Δx &= \frac{30}{120} = 0.25m
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the stretched length of the spring is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= L_{0} + Δx \\
&= 0.46 + 0.25 \\
&= 0.71m
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the stretched length of the spring is $0.71$ meters.
