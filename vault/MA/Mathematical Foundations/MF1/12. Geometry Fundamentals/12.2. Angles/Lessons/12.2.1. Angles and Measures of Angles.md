## Introduction

An **angle** is formed whenever two rays meet at a point.

The common point shared by the rays is called the **vertex**, and the rays themselves are called the **sides** of the angle.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/b2821eb8a93b32cdf08a250239a05a84.png>)

Consider the following angle, made up of the rays $\overrightarrow{{\color{blue}{A}}B}$ and $\overrightarrow{{\color{blue}{A}}C}$.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/ed9f9ff80c81a82c7c073f12591bd735.png>)

There are several different ways of labeling this angle.

- One way to write this angle is as follows:
$\angle C\!{\color{blue}{A}}B$
The symbol $\angle$ means "angle." Notice that the vertex ${\color{blue}{A}}$ is in the middle.
- We can also swap the order in which we write the endpoints. For example,
$\angle B{\color{blue}{A}}C$.
Notice that the vertex ${\color{blue}{A}}$ is still in the middle.
- To save space, we sometimes write $\angle {\color{blue}{A}}$.
- Finally, instead of using the $\angle$ symbol, we can write $\widehat{C{\color{blue}{A}}B}$ or $\widehat{B{\color{blue}{A}}C}$.

<a id="expressing-angles-using-angle-notation"></a>
## Expressing Angles Using Angle Notation

**Example:** Which numbered angle refers to the same angle as $\widehat{GHA}$?

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/369ef3594ab132c9b7e2ce26e2efe6b7.png>)

**Explanation**

If we trace along the letters of the angle name, from $G$ to $H$ to $A$, we see that $\widehat{GHA}$ refers to the angle $\angle 3$.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/55105f72b2586246544f32eaa5f3723d.png>)

Therefore, $\angle{3}$ is another name for $\widehat{GHA}$.

<a id="angle-measures"></a>
## Angle Measures

The **measure** of an angle is the amount of "turn" from one side of the angle to the other.

We measure angles in **degrees**, which have the symbol $^\circ$.

Let's look at a few examples of angles with their measures.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/2a88b5b3bedfc66ff8abeee37c4acb3b.png>)

Note the following:

- The more "turn" there is, the larger the angle's measure.
- A corner angle (like the angle $C$ above) has a measure of $90^\circ$. An angle with measure $90^\circ$ is called a **right angle.**

We use the letter $m$ to represent the measure of the angle. For example,

$$
m \angle C = 90^\circ
$$

We'd read this as "the measure of the angle $C$ is $90$ degrees."

## Determining an Angle With a Given Measure

**Example:** Which angle has a measure of $90^{\circ}$?

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/be03e769dd4afcaee1c3612e65b25c57.png>)

**Explanation**

Let's highlight the angle with a measure $90^\circ$ on our diagram.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/e72bf2c8d596243d33aeb5073c3b0aa0.png>)

From the diagram, we see that the angle $\angle{COD}$ (or $\angle{DOC}$) has a measure of $90^\circ$.

<a id="reading-the-measure-of-an-angle"></a>
## Reading the Measure of an Angle

**Example:** What is the measure of the angle $\angle{NRP}$?

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/cf9a6774afbce5a54eecba8e3eb4da12.png>)

**Explanation**

If we trace along the letters of the angle name, from $N$ to $R$ to $P$, we can see the angle $\angle{NRP}$.

![](<../Source/12.2.1. Angles and Measures of Angles/Images/38d6f011063cf5548f2401a8e189fdf0.png>)

Therefore,

$$
m\angle{NRP}=110^\circ
$$
