## Introduction

Let's consider the complex number

$$
z= x+\textrm{i}y
$$

on the argand diagram below, where $\mid z \mid$ is the magnitude of $z$ and $\theta$ is the argument of $z$.

![](<../Source/6.2.1. The Polar Form of a Complex Number/Images/49ff5870e01ba2ed9b733280e275c3e6.png>)

Using trigonometry, we see that

$$
x= \mid z \mid \cos\theta
$$

and

$$
y= \mid z \mid \sin\theta
$$

Substituting these into

$$
z=x+\textrm{i}y
$$

we obtain the **polar form** of the complex number:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= x + \textrm{i}y \\
z &= \begin{vmatrix}z & \cos θ + \textrm{i} & z\end{vmatrix}\sin θ \\
z &= \mid z \mid (\cos θ + \textrm{i}\sin θ)
\end{aligned}
$$

The coordinates

$$
\left(\mid z \mid, \theta\right)
$$

are the polar coordinates of $z$.

To demonstrate, let's write

$$
z=3+3\textrm{i}
$$

in polar form. First, we compute the magnitude of $z$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid z \mid &= \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{3^{2} + 3^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{18} \\
&= 3\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since $z$ is in the first quadrant, we can find the angle $\theta$ using the arctangent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= \arctan (\frac{y}{x}) \\
&= \arctan (\frac{3}{3}) \\
&= \arctan (1) \\
&= \frac{π}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the polar form of $z$ is

$$
z = 3\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) +\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) \right)
$$

## Expressing a Complex Number in Polar Form

**Example:** Express $z=-5+12\textrm{i}$ in the form $r(\cos\theta + \textrm{i}\sin\theta)$. Round $\theta$ to three decimal places.

**Explanation**

In polar form, $r$ represents the magnitude $\mid z \mid$. For a complex number

$$
z=x+y\textrm{i}
$$

the magnitude of $z$ is given by

$$
\mid z \mid = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}
$$

So, for

$$
z = -5+12\textrm{i}
$$

the magnitude is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r = \mid z \mid &= \sqrt{(-5)^{2} + (12)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{25 + 144} \\
&= \sqrt{169} \\
&= 13
\end{aligned}
$$

In polar form, $\theta$ represents the argument $\arg(z)$. Since $x = -5$ is negative and $y = 12$ is positive, $z$ lies in the second quadrant. So, we can find $\theta$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ = \arg (z) &= π - \arctan \mid \frac{y}{x} \mid \\
&= π - \arctan \mid \frac{12}{-5} \mid \\
&= π - \arctan (\frac{12}{5}) \\
&\approx 1.966
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to three decimal places.

Finally, we write the complex number

$$
z = -5 + 12\textrm{i}
$$

in polar form:

$$
z = 13(\cos(1.966)+\textrm{i}\sin(1.966))
$$

<a id="expressing-a-quotient-of-complex-numbers-in-polar-form"></a>
## Expressing a Quotient of Complex Numbers in Polar Form

**Example:** Express $z=\dfrac{3}{2-\textrm{i}}$ in the form $r(\cos\theta+\textrm{i}\sin\theta)$.

**Explanation**

First, we express the given complex number in the form

$$
z=x+y\textrm{i}
$$

To do this, we remove $\textrm{i}$ from the denominator by multiplying the numerator and denominator of $z$ by the complex conjugate of the denominator, which is $2+\textrm{i}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= \frac{3}{2 - \textrm{i}} \\
&= (3(2 + \textrm{i}))/((2 - \textrm{i})(2 + \textrm{i})) \\
&= (3(2 + \textrm{i}))/(2^{2} + 1^{2}) \\
&= \frac{3}{5}(2 + \textrm{i}) \\
&= \frac{6}{5} + \frac{3}{5}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

In polar form, $r$ represents the magnitude $\mid z \mid$. For a complex number

$$
z=x+y\textrm{i}
$$

the magnitude of $z$ is given by

$$
\mid z \mid = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}
$$

So, for

$$
z= \dfrac{6}{5} +\dfrac{3}{5}\textrm{i}
$$

the magnitude is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r = \mid z \mid &= \sqrt{(\frac{6}{5})^{2} + (\frac{3}{5})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{36 + 9}{25}} \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{45}{25}} \\
&= \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

In polar form, $\theta$ represents the argument $\arg(z)$. Since

$$
x = \dfrac{6}{5}
$$

and

$$
y = \dfrac{3}{5}
$$

are both positive, $z$ lies in the first quadrant. So, we can find $\theta$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\arg (z) &= \arctan (((\frac{3}{5}))/((\frac{6}{5}))) \\
&= \arctan (\frac{1}{2}) \\
&= 0.464
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to three decimal places.

Finally, we write the complex number $z$ in polar form:

$$
z = \dfrac{3\sqrt{5}}{5} \left (\cos(0.464)+ \textrm{i} \sin(0.464) \right)
$$

<a id="converting-from-polar-form-to-standard-form"></a>
## Converting from Polar Form to Standard Form

Previously, we wrote the complex number

$$
z=3+3\textrm{i}
$$

using polar coordinates:

$$
z = 3\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) +\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) \right)
$$

To check that this result is correct, we can convert it back to Cartesian form by simplifying the right-hand side.

Since

$$
\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

and

$$
\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= 3\sqrt{2}(\cos (\frac{π}{4}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{π}{4})) \\
&= 3\sqrt{2}(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \textrm{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \\
&= 3 + 3\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

This matches up with our original complex number, so we can be sure that we have the correct polar form.

<a id="converting-from-polar-form-to-cartesian-form"></a>
## Converting From Polar Form to Cartesian Form

**Example:** Express $z=4\left(\cos\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)+\textrm{i}\sin\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right)$ in the form $z=x+\textrm{i}y$.

**Explanation**

We will simplify the right-hand side. Since

$$
\cos\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
$$

and

$$
\sin\left(\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= 4(\cos (\frac{5π}{6}) + \textrm{i}\sin (\frac{5π}{6})) \\
&= 4(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \textrm{i}\frac{1}{2}) \\
&=-2\sqrt{3} + 2\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$
