## Introduction

Suppose we would like to find the arc length of the curve defined parametrically as

$$
x=2\cos{t}, \quad y=2\sin{t}
$$

from $t=0$ to

$$
t=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/fba5b3189129d2cee312509b6ce706a1.png>)

It can be shown that the arc length $L$ of a curve defined parametrically by

$$
x=x(t), \quad y=y(t), \qquad t\in [t_1,t_2]
$$

is given by the formula

$$
L = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}x}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2}\,\textrm{d}t
$$

The intuition behind this formula is given at the end of this tutorial.

According to the formula, the length of the blue arc is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\sqrt{(\frac{d}{dt}(2\cos t))^{2} + (\frac{d}{dt}(2\sin t))^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\sqrt{(-2\sin t)^{2} + (2\cos t)^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\sqrt{4(\sin^{2} t + \cos^{2} t)}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}2dt \\
&= 2t \mid _{0}^{π/2} \\
&= 2(\frac{π}{2} - 0) \\
&= π
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding an Integral Expression For the Arc Length of a Parametric Curve

**Example:** Find an integral expression for the arc length of the curve defined parametrically as

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/c85ca457e7f02850924980f54ba585bf.png>)

$x=1-t, \quad y= \dfrac{2}{3} t^{3/2}, \qquad t\in[0,1]$.

**Explanation**

To compute the arc length $L$ of a parametrically defined curve, we use the formula

$$
L = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}x}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2}\:\textrm{d}t
$$

Computing the derivatives of $x$ and $y$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(1 - t) =-1
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(\frac{2}{3}t^{3/2}) = t^{1/2}
\end{aligned}
$$

We substitute these derivatives in the formula for arc length, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}\sqrt{(-1)^{2} + (t^{1/2})^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1 + t}dt
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding an Integral Expression For the Arc Length of a Parametric Curve Containing Special Functions

**Example:** Find the expression that gives the length of the curve defined by the parametric equations
$x=t, \, y=\ln{(\cos{t})}$
from $t=0$ to $t=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ (see graph below).

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/f10352be16212d467425c8695c797467.png>)

**Explanation**

To compute the arc length $L$ of a parametrically defined curve, we use the formula

$$
L = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}x}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2}\:\textrm{d}t
$$

Computing the derivatives of $x$ and $y$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(t) = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(\ln (\cos t)) =-\tan t
\end{aligned}
$$

We substitute these derivatives in the formula for arc length, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sqrt{(1)^{2} + (-\tan t)^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sqrt{1 + \tan^{2} t}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sqrt{\sec^{2} t}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sec tdt
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-arc-length-of-a-parametric-curve"></a>
## Finding the Arc Length of a Parametric Curve

**Example:** Find the arc length of the curve defined parametrically as

$x=e^t(\cos{t}+\sin{t}), \quad y=e^t(\cos{t}-\sin{t}), \quad t\in \left[0,\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right]$.

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/01cb59beb0b1df4a0cf0402429bafa19.png>)

**Explanation**

Computing the derivatives of $x$ and $y$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(e^{t}(\cos t + \sin t)) \\
&= e^{t}(\cos t + \sin t) + e^{t}(-\sin t + \cos t) \\
&= 2e^{t}\cos t
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(e^{t}(\cos t - \sin t)) \\
&= e^{t}(\cos t - \sin t) + e^{t}(-\sin t - \cos t) \\
&=-2e^{t}\sin t
\end{aligned}
$$

We substitute these derivatives in the formula for arc length, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sqrt{(2e^{t}\cos t)^{2} + (-2e^{t}\sin t)^{2}}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}\sqrt{4e^{2t}(\cos^{2} t + \sin^{2} t)}dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4}2e^{t}dt \\
&= 2e^{t} \mid _{0}^{π/4} \\
&= 2(e^{π/4} - 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

## The Intuition Behind the Main Formula

Let's go back to our first example, which was to find the arc length of the curve defined by

$$
x=2\cos{t}, \quad y=2\sin{t}, \quad t\in\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]
$$

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/b1e3cd196df295e05a912b4b43882b5b.png>)

First, we divide the interval

$$
\left[ 0, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right]
$$

into small subintervals of equal length. Consider a part of the blue arc that corresponds to one of those subintervals and let the length of that part be $\Delta{s}$.

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/27415db192d1ddfa89ddc350a95f12fd.png>)

We can now denote the horizontal displacement as $\Delta{x}$ and the vertical shift as $\Delta{y}$.

![](<../Source/3.1.7. The Arc Length of a Parametric Curve/Images/484bdaf55d0db351802a45758c78cf15.png>)

To approximate the length $\Delta{s}$ we apply the Pythagorean theorem, as follows:

$$
Δs \mid \approx \sqrt{(Δx)^{2} + (Δy)^{2}}
$$

To approximate the total length $L$, we need to sum up all of the little $\Delta s$ terms, so

$$
L \approx \sum \Delta s = \sum \sqrt{\left(\Delta{x} \right)^2 + \left(\Delta{y} \right)^2}
$$

We then take the limit as $\Delta x, \Delta y \rightarrow 0$. As we do this, the sum becomes a definite integral, and our approximation approaches the exact value of $L$. So, using the chain rule, we arrive at

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫\sqrt{(dx)^{2} + (dy)^{2}} \\
&= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt} \cdot dt)^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt} \cdot dt)^{2}} \\
&= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} \cdot (dt)^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2} \cdot (dt)^{2}} \\
&= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}} \cdot \sqrt{dt^{2}} \\
&= ∫_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dt})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dt})^{2}}dt
\end{aligned}
$$
