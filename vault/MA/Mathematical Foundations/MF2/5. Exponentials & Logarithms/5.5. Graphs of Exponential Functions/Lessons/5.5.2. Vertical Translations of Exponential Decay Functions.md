## Introduction

Adding or subtracting a value to the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^x
$$

will **translate** (shift) the graph up or down the $y$-axis.

For instance,

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^x+3
$$

has the same shape as

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^x
$$

but it's shifted $3$ units up.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/72716eb23ad3000031ce534baa96c863.png>)

Note the following points:

- The $y$-intercept $(0,1)$ has moved up $3$ units to the point $(0,4)$.
- The horizontal asymptote $y=0$ has moved up $3$ units to $y=3$.

Now, consider the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^x-3
$$

In this case, the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{2} \right)^x
$$

is shifted $3$ units *down*.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/09b8609c45f82bb4e203de9f12a70dbf.png>)

## Identifying a Vertically Shifted Exponential Decay Curve

**Example:** Plot the graph of $y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^{x}-3$.

**Explanation**

First we plot the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^{x}{:}
$$

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/695bd620f29881bf3d1d12a15a27402a.png>)

To sketch the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^{x}-3
$$

we take the graph of

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^{x}
$$

and shift it $3$ units down. The $y$-intercept moves from $(0,1)$ to $(0,-2)$, and the horizontal asymptote moves from $y=0$ to $y=-3$.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/06e822096d671208bbf430caf55e8190.png>)

<a id="identifying-the-shift-applied-to-an-exponential-decay-curve"></a>
## Identifying the Shift Applied to an Exponential Decay Curve

**Example:** The graph below shows the curve $y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4} \right)^{x} + c$. Determine the value of $c$ and find the equation of the curve.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/54cd2e1f9633f7e5d5c1536079217c29.png>)

**Explanation**

The horizontal asymptote of

$$
y = \left(\dfrac{1}{4} \right)^x
$$

is $y = 0$.

The vertical translation

$$
y=\left(\dfrac{1}{4} \right)^x+c
$$

shifts the asymptote to $y=c$.

In the graph above, the horizontal asymptote is $y=1$. Therefore, $c=1$.

<a id="combining-vertical-shift-and-vertical-stretch"></a>
## Combining Vertical Shift and Vertical Stretch

When an exponential decay function is vertically shifted *and* stretched, we apply the stretch first and the shift second.

For example, to plot the function

$$
y=2\left(\dfrac{1}{4} \right)^x+3
$$

we start by plotting the graph of

$$
y = \left(\dfrac{1}{4} \right)^x{:}
$$

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/1ae9dae1a67ab9bc2d155ac842129ca0.png>)

Next, we plot the graph of

$$
y = 2\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^x
$$

To do this, we scale the graph above by a factor of $2$. The $y$-intercept moves from $(0,1)$ to $(0,2)$ but the horizontal asymptote stays at $y=0$.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/1f60e0901ab2e15d4b87d91a71b1ac3d.png>)

Finally, we plot the graph of

$$
y = 2\left(\dfrac{1}{4}\right)^x + 3
$$

To do this, we shift the above graph $3$ units above. The $y$-intercept moves from $(0,2)$ to $(0,5)$ and the horizontal asymptote moves from $y=0$ to $y=3$.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/c86baccc8565e2b128266a93bda38af3.png>)

<a id="identifying-the-shift-and-stretch-applied-to-an-exponential-decay-curve"></a>
## Identifying the Shift and Stretch Applied to an Exponential Decay Curve

**Example:** The graph below shows the curve $y = k\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{x}+c$. Determine the values of $k$ and $c$ and find the equation of the curve.

![](<../Source/5.5.2. Vertical Translations of Exponential Decay Functions/Images/d8f79039924340963dd585e4535b185a.png>)

**Explanation**

The horizontal asymptote of the curve

$$
y=k\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{x}+c
$$

is $y=c$. In the graph above, the horizontal asymptote is $y=-5$, hence $c=-5$.

We can solve for $k$ by finding a point on the graph and substituting for $x$ and $y$. The labeled point on the graph is $(0,-1)$, so we substitute $x=0$ and $y=-1$ into the function and solve for $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k(\frac{1}{2})^{x} + c &= y \\
k(\frac{1}{2})^{x} - 5 &= y \\
k(\frac{1}{2})^{0} - 5 &= -1 \\
k - 5 &= -1 \\
k &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the equation of the curve is

$$
y = 4\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{x} - 5
$$
