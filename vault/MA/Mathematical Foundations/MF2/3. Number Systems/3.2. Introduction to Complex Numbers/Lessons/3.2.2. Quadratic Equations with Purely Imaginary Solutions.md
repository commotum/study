## Introduction

When solving an equation, we sometimes require taking the square root of a negative number. In such cases, we will not end up with real solutions, but imaginary solutions.

To demonstrate, let's solve the equation

$$
2x^2 +8 = 0
$$

for $x$. First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} 2x^2 + 8 &= 0 \\[3pt] 2x^2 &= -8 \\[3pt] x^2 &= -4 \end{aligned}
$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$
x = \pm \sqrt{-4}
$$

We now rewrite $x$ using the imaginary unit

$$
\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= ± \sqrt{-4} \\
&= ± \sqrt{4 \cdot (-1)} \\
&= ± \sqrt{4} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± 2\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
x = \pm 2 \textrm{i}
$$

## Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Solutions

**Example:** What are the solutions to $x^2 + 16 = 0$?

**Explanation**

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} x^2 + 16 &= 0 \\[3pt] x^2 &= -16 \end{aligned}
$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$
x = \pm \sqrt{-16}
$$

We now rewrite $x$ using the imaginary unit

$$
\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= ± \sqrt{-16} \\
&= ± \sqrt{16 \cdot (-1)} \\
&= ± \sqrt{16} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± 4\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
x = \pm 4 \textrm{i}
$$

## Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Solutions With Simplification

**Example:** If $p^2 + 27 = 0$, then $p =$

**Explanation**

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} p^2 + 27 & = 0 \\[3pt] p^2 & = -27 \end{aligned}
$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$
p = \pm \sqrt{-27}
$$

We now rewrite $p$ using the imaginary unit

$$
\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= ± \sqrt{-27} \\
&= ± \sqrt{27 \cdot (-1)} \\
&= ± \sqrt{27} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \sqrt{3^{2} \cdot 3} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± 3\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± 3\sqrt{3}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
p = \pm 3\sqrt{3} \, \textrm{i}
$$

## Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Fractional Solutions

**Example:** If $16z^2 + 49 = 0$, then $z =$

**Explanation**

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} 16z^2 + 49 & = 0 \\[5pt] 16z^2 & = -49 \\[5pt] z^2 & = -\dfrac{49}{16} \end{aligned}
$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$
z = \pm \sqrt{-\dfrac{49}{16}}
$$

We now rewrite $z$ using the imaginary unit

$$
\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= ± \sqrt{-\frac{49}{16}} \\
&= ± \sqrt{\frac{49}{16} \cdot (-1)} \\
&= ± \sqrt{\frac{49}{16}} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \frac{\sqrt{49}}{\sqrt{16}} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \frac{7}{4}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
z=\pm \dfrac{7}{4} \textrm{i}
$$

## Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Fractional Roots With Solutions

**Example:** What are the solutions of the equation $2y^2 + 9 = 0$?

**Explanation**

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} 2y^2 + 9 &= 0 \\[5pt] 2y^2 &= -9 \\[5pt] y^2 & = -\dfrac{9}{2} \end{aligned}
$$

By taking square root, we obtain

$$
y = \pm \sqrt{-\dfrac{9}{2}}
$$

We now rewrite $y$ using the imaginary unit

$$
\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= ± \sqrt{-\frac{9}{2}} \\
&= ± \sqrt{\frac{9}{2} \cdot (-1)} \\
&= ± \sqrt{\frac{9}{2}} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \frac{\sqrt{9}}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we rationalize the denominator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= ± \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\textrm{i} \\
&= ± \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \\
&= ± \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}\textrm{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solutions are

$$
y=\pm \dfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{2} \textrm{i}
$$
