## Introduction

Many real-world problems involve two quantities that must satisfy certain limits at the same time, like staying under a budget while mixing two items. Two-variable inequalities help model these situations.

For example, suppose Marie wants to buy some gifts for her friends. Her budget is $\[math]90$, and she has narrowed the gifts down to two possibilities:

- calendars, each costing $\[/math]18$, and
- battery packs, each costing $\[math]15$.

Can we form a mathematical statement describing all possible combinations of calendars and battery packs Marie can purchase?

Let $c$ be the number of calendars and $b$ the number of battery packs Marie purchases. Then, the total amount (in dollars) she will pay is given by the expression

$$
18c+15b
$$

Marie's budget is $\[/math] 90$. This means that the expression above can be no greater than $90$. Therefore,

$$
18c+15b \leq 90
$$

We can simplify this inequality by dividing both sides by $3$. This gives

$$
6c+5b \leq 30
$$

## Representing Situations Using Two-Variable Inequalities

**Example:** One-fifth of a number ($x$) minus one-quarter of another number ($y$) is less than $1$. Find an inequality that represents the relationship described.

**Explanation**

The expression "one-fifth of a number ($x$) minus one-quarter of another number ($y$)" can be written as

$$
\dfrac{1}{5}x-\dfrac{1}{4}y
$$

We're told that this difference is *less than* $1$. Therefore, we obtain

$$
\dfrac{1}{5}x-\dfrac{1}{4}y <1
$$

We can simplify this inequality by multiplying it by $20$, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{5}x - \frac{1}{4}y & < 1 \\ 20 \cdot (\frac{1}{5}x - \frac{1}{4}y) & < 20 \cdot 1 \\ 4x - 5y & < 20\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="determining-a-feasible-region"></a>
## Determining a Feasible Region

A graph is often the best way to represent the solution to a system of two-variable linear inequalities. We'll illustrate with an example.

Suppose Mr. Cook is preparing cakes for a bake sale. He has a total budget of $\[math]120$ for all the ingredients. Each chocolate cake costs $\[/math]12$ to make, and each sponge cake costs $\[math]6$. Let's formulate this situation as a two-variable inequality, and plot its solution on a graph.

Let $x$ be the number of chocolate cakes he makes and let $y$ be the number of sponge cakes. Since he has a total budget of $\[/math]120$, we have the following inequality:

$$
12x + 6y \leq 120
$$

By dividing both sides of this inequality by $6$, we get

$$
2x+y \leq 20
$$

To plot this inequality, we will first convert the inequality into slope-intercept form. This gives

$$
y \le - 2x + 20
$$

Let's now draw the region.

- Since the inequality involves a 'less than or equal to' sign, the line $y = -2x + 20$ is part of the solution. Therefore, we draw a solid line.
- Then, we must decide whether to shade above or below the line. The inequality $y \leq -2x + 20$ refers to the $y$-values that are *less than* or equal to those on the line $y = -2x +20$. So, we shade *below* the line.
- Lastly, because $x$ and $y$ cannot be negative (we cannot bake a negative number of cakes), we limit our shading to the first quadrant.

Therefore, we get the following graph:

## Interpreting a Feasible Region

When modeling situations in context, the solution (i.e., the shaded part of the graph) is called the **feasible region.** Any point inside the feasible region is a potential solution to our problem.

To understand this, let's go back to the feasible region for Mr. Cook's bake sale.

![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/bd36f901ea40d94561120923ded604a9.png>)

- If Mr. Cook wants to make $5$ chocolate and $5$ sponge cakes, then our solution tells us that this is a possibility since the point $(5,5)$ lies in the feasible region:![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/c72c11a969d69dd6d4cbf29e36654148.png>)
- However, if Mr. Cook wants to make $10$ chocolate and $5$ sponge cakes, our solution tells us that this is *not* a possibility since the point $(10,5)$ does *not* lie in the feasible region:![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/1d25ac6166db3d1d268c9e62b48ae8f5.png>)

## Representing a Situation in Context With a Graph

**Example:** An event planner rents tables and chairs. Each table costs $\[math]70$ and each chair costs $\[/math]35$. Let $x$ be the number of tables rented and $y$ be the number of chairs rented.

Which of the following shaded regions represents all possible combinations $(x,y)$ if the total rental cost must be **less than** $\[math]700$?

**Explanation**

The total cost of renting is $70x + 35y$. Since the event planner's budget is less than $700$, we have the following inequality:
$70x + 35y \mid < 700$

To plot this inequality, we will first convert the inequality into slope-intercept form:
$\begin{bmatrix}70x + 35y & < 700 \\ 2x + y & < 20 \\ y & < - 2x + 20\end{bmatrix}$

Let's now draw the region.

- Since the inequality involves a 'less than' sign, the line $y = -2x + 20$ is not part of the solution. Therefore, we draw a dashed line.
- Then, we must decide whether to shade above or below the line. The inequality $y \lt -2x + 20$ refers to the $y$-values that are *less than* those on the line $y = -2x + 20$. So, we shade *below* the line.
- Lastly, because $x$ and $y$ cannot be negative, we limit our shading to the first quadrant.

![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/0983890ef0d9a4092003b55e33050534.png>)

## Interpreting the Feasible Region

**Example:** An artist sells sculptures and paintings for $\[math]1\,600$ and $\[/math]1\,200$ each, respectively. Let $x$ denote the number of sculptures and $y$ denote the number of paintings the artist can create in a month. The feasible region representing the constraints on $x$ and $y$ is shown below.

![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/e0fb9ed22b8e7670770b1f866f77928e.png>)

A local art gallery wishes to place an order for $4$ sculptures and $3$ paintings in a month. Can the artist fulfill this order? If so, how much revenue would he make?

**Explanation**

Let's answer the questions in turn.

- To determine whether the artist can fulfill the order, we need to test if the point $(4,3)$ belongs to the feasible region. The graph shows that $(4,3)$ belongs to the region. Therefore, the artist can fulfill the order.

![](<../Source/2.4.5. Modeling With Two-Variable Linear Inequalities/Images/b5ce338640eeda3eec95025cb0a66dfd.png>)

- Selling $x=4$ sculptures and $y=3$ paintings, the artist obtains a revenue of
$1600x + 1200y|= \[math]1600 \cdot 4 + \[/math]1200 \cdot 3; = \[math]6400 + \[/math]3600; = \[math]10000$.
