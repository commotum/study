## Introduction

When a circle is drawn inside a square and touches all four sides, the circle is said to be **inscribed** in the square. An example of a circle inscribed in a square is shown below.

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/469088adb228555e0063b3a3621b2193.png>)

Whenever a circle is inscribed in a square, the *length of a diameter* of the circle equals the *side length* of the square, as shown.

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/89695dab246770181ffd3faad6608e2c.png>)

The center of a square is simply the point where the two diagonals meet. Whenever a circle is inscribed in a square, the centers of the circle and the square coincide.

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/a4aa60719c06f88a1116e58cca95942c.png>)

Whenever we have information about a square, we can use this to determine key characteristics of a circle that's inscribed within it.

Let's see an example.

---

<a id="solving-for-a-circles-parameters"></a>
## Solving for a Circle's Parameters

**Example:** Find the area of a circle inscribed in a square with sides of length $18\,\textrm{m}$.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/cece340e435972c8e8d9db3cc7ebff35.png>)

The side of the square is $18 \: \textrm{m}$, which means that the diameter $d$ of the circle is

$$
d = 18 \: \textrm{m}
$$

Thus, the radius $r$ of the circle is

$$
r = \dfrac{d}{2} = \dfrac{18}{2} = 9 \: \textrm{m}
$$

Therefore, the area of the circle is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= πr^{2} \\
&= π(9)^{2} \\
&= 81πm^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Determining Properties of a Square Given Data on Its Inscribed Circle

Whenever we have information about a circle, we can use this to determine key characteristics of the square that inscribes it.

For example, suppose we have a circle with radius

$$
r=2\:\textrm{cm}
$$

inscribed in a square, as shown below. What is the area of the square?

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/46c4a96cd524b79f2f72054931a9ad24.png>)

If $s$ is the length of the square's sides, then $s$ equals the diameter of the inscribed circle, and the diameter is twice the radius. Therefore,

$$
s = 2r = 2(2) = 4\,\textrm{cm}
$$

Now, the area of the square is given by

$$
\mathcal{A} = s^2
$$

Substituting the known side length, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= s^{2} \\
&= 4^{2} \\
&= 16cm^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the area of the square is $16\,\textrm{cm}^2$.

Let's look at another example.

<a id="solving-for-a-squares-parameters"></a>
## Solving for a Square's Parameters

**Example:** The circumference of a circle inscribed in a square is $7\pi$. What is the area of the square?

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/188e56a7d5faa7ecad372afe1637941d.png>)

The formula for a circle's circumference is $C = \pi d$, where $d$ is the diameter. So, we can find the diameter as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d &= \frac{C}{π} \\
&= \frac{7π}{π} \\
&= 7
\end{aligned}
$$

The diameter of the circle is $7$, which means that the side $s$ of the square is
$s = 7$.

Therefore, the area of the square is

$$
\mathcal{A} = s^2 = (7)^2 = 49
$$

## Problems Involving Diagonals of Squares

**Example:** The area of a circle inscribed in a square is $32\pi \,\textrm{mm}^2$. Find the diagonal of the square.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/10.5.10. Circles Inscribed in Squares/Images/3c61ae142274a8328e99b2606f49007a.png>)

The area of a circle is given by

$$
\mathcal{A} = \pi r^2
$$

Substituting the known area and solving for the radius $r$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= πr^{2} \\
32π &= πr^{2} \\
32π &= πr^{2} \\
32 &= r^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

and taking the square root gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{32} \\
&= \sqrt{16 \cdot 2} \\
&= \sqrt{16} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= 4\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the radius of the circle equals $4\sqrt 2\,\textrm{mm}$.

The length $s$ of the square's side equals the diameter of the inscribed circle, and the diameter is twice the radius. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= 2r \\
&= 2(4\sqrt{2}) \\
&= 8\sqrt{2}mm
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the length $d$ of the square's diagonal is

$$
\begin{aligned}
d &= s\sqrt{2} \\
&= 8\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} \\
&= 8 \cdot 2 \\
&= 16mm
\end{aligned}
$$
