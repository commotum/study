## Introduction

In the coordinate plane, dilations are typically centered at the origin. When a dilation is centered at the origin, it can be represented by a function

$$
(x,y) \mapsto (kx, ky)
$$

where $k$ is the scale factor of the dilation.

For example, let's compute the image of the point $(3,2)$ (shown below) under the action of a dilation with center $O$ and scale factor $3$.

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/36d91a0e4df454a4e97904cd089f0077.png>)

A dilation with center at the origin and a scale factor of $3$ can be represented by the function

$$
(x,y) \mapsto \left(3x,3y\right)
$$

Applying this function to the point $(3,2)$, we have

$$
(3,2) \mapsto \left(3\cdot 3, \:3 \cdot 2 \right) = (9,6)
$$

Therefore, the resulting point is $(9,6)$.

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/de2816ca7398c59274e0b8425f72245b.png>)

## Dilating Points in the Coordinate Plane

**Example:** Find the image of the point $(-5,2)$ under the action of a dilation with center $O$ and scale factor $-3$?

**Explanation**

A dilation with center at the origin and a scale factor of $-3$ can be represented by the function

$$
(x,y) \mapsto (-3x,-3y)
$$

Applying this function to the point $(-5,2)$, we have

$$
(-5,2) \mapsto \big((-3) \cdot (-5), \: (-3) \cdot 2 \big) = (15,-6)
$$

Therefore, the resulting point is $(15,-6)$.

## Dilating Figures in the Coordinate Plane

**Example:** What is the image of the polygon shown above under a dilation that is centered at the origin and has a scale factor of $-2$?

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/bad87db48b547ec45e83fb076e751d4a.png>)

**Explanation**

The dilation with center at the origin and a scale factor of $-2$ can be represented by the function

$$
(x,y) \mapsto \left(-2x,-2y\right)

## Finding a Center of Dilation

Let's discuss how to find a center of a dilation

The center of a dilation lies on any line that passes through corresponding points on a figure and its image. Therefore, we can find a center of dilation as the intersection of any two of these lines.

Let's look at some examples:

- Consider the rectangle $ABCD$ and its image $A'B'C'D'$ under the action of a dilation, shown below.![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/cdd1ee9e8b9751a0b54cc5a21f1fc3ff.png>)
We can find the center of dilation $O$ by connecting two corresponding points with a straight line and finding their intersection.

For example, if we draw the lines $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{AA'}$ and $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{BB'}$ and find their intersection, we get the center of dilation $O$ shown below.![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/7d853ccfc48aad6e1ba0e04dc3df4695.png>)

- Sometimes, a figure and its image lie on opposite sides of the center of dilation. For example, consider the rectangle $ABCD$ and its image $A'B'C'D'$ shown below.![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/c56f215171913225f777c5f0622b86f7.png>)
If we draw the lines $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{AA'}$ and $\overset{\longleftrightarrow}{BB'}$, we find that the center of dilation $O$ lies between the two shapes.![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/1c4c4668c0b1714a24fe5b8b1aa90b49.png>)

## Finding the Scale Factor of a Dilation in the Coordinate Plane

**Example:** The point $P'(-2,-4)$ is the image of the point $P(1,2)$ under a dilation with the center at the origin, as shown above. What is the scale factor of the transformation?

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/f57f86200b68d1ea8f6f28ea80991238.png>)

**Explanation**

A dilation with center at the origin and a factor of $k$ can be represented by the function
$(x,y) \mapsto (kx,ky)\,$.

Applying this function to the point $P$ and equating the result to $P'$, we get

$$
(k \cdot 1, k \cdot 2) = (-2,-4)⟹{k =-2; 2k =-4
$$

Therefore,

$$
k=\dfrac{(-4)}{2}=-2
$$

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/b0c44825722228b6332e2b5c8709b2d7.png>)

<a id="the-image-of-a-line-under-a-dilation"></a>
## The Image of a Line Under a Dilation

If $l$ is a line passing through the center of dilation, then the image $l'$ under the dilation is the same as the original line.

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/b5c04b2bed1c15c54f950b2357fa2a9f.png>)

However, if $k$ is a line that does not pass through the center of dilation, then the image $k'$ is parallel to the original line.

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/e67b50311d304bc544afde8280bbe19c.png>)

## The Image of a Line Under a Dilation

**Example:** A dilation with center $O$ is defined by the function $(x,y) \mapsto \left(\dfrac53x, \dfrac53y\right)$. What is the image of the line shown above under the action of this transformation?

![](<../Source/10.2.7. Dilations of Figures in the Coordinate Plane/Images/36c58e0cafac8c07a5b20ae80b28a43c.png>)

**Explanation**

A dilation maps a line $l$ not passing through the center of the dilation onto a parallel line $l'$.

To find the image of the line, we proceed as follows:

- Take a point $B \in l$.
- Find the image $B'$ of $B$ under the dilation.
- Find $l'$ as the line that passes through $B'$ and is parallel to $l$.

Notice that $B(-3,0)$ lies on $l$, as shown below. The image of this point under the dilation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\frac{5}{3} \cdot (-3), \frac{5}{3} \cdot 0) &= (-5, 0)
\end{aligned}
$$
