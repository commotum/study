## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to prove the midpoint theorem for triangles. Let's begin by stating the theorem.

> *The line segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side, and its length is equal to half the length of the third side.*

Notice that the midpoint theorem can be broken into two separate parts:

- **Part 1:** The segment joining the midpoints is *parallel to* the third side.
- **Part 2:** The segment joining the midpoints is *half the length* of the third side.

We'll start by proving the first part.

Consider the diagram below, where $L$ and $N$ are midpoints of the sides $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, respectively, of the triangle $\triangle{ABC}$.

![](<../Source/4.3.10. Proving the Midpoint Theorem/Images/320bdfc88093b843eb7fbe286cffa963.png>)

We wish to prove the following statement:

> $\overline{BC} \parallel \overline{LN}$

The proof strategy involves proving that $\triangle ABC\sim ALN$, and then using the converse corresponding angles postulate to show that $\overline{BC}$ and $\overline{LN}$ are parallel.

We proceed as follows:

- **Step 1**: We are given that $L$ and $N$ are the midpoints of $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, respectively.
- **Step 2**: By the *definition of a midpoint*, we have
$AB = 2AL, \qquad AC = 2AN$.
- **Step 3**: Using the *multiplication principle*, we obtain
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = 2, \qquad \dfrac{AC}{AN} = 2$.
- **Step 4**: Applying the *transitive property of equality* to the result from step 3, we get
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = \dfrac{AC}{AN}$.
- **Step 5**: $\angle{BAC}$ and $\angle{LAN}$ represent the same angle. Therefore, by the *reflexive property of congruence*,
$\angle BAC \cong \angle LAN$,
as shown below.

![](<../Source/4.3.10. Proving the Midpoint Theorem/Images/d0ee4968c23483e9569fc8a6a883a566.png>)

- **Step 6**: In steps 4 and 5, respectively, we showed that
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = \dfrac{AC} {AN}, \qquad \angle BAC \cong \angle LAN$.
Therefore, $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{ALN}$ by the *SAS similarity criterion*.
- **Step 7**: Since $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{ALN}$, we have
$\angle{ABC} \cong \angle{ALN}$
since they're *corresponding angles in similar triangles*.

![](<../Source/4.3.10. Proving the Midpoint Theorem/Images/57cf143a57d576d6cf6059c77ee467df.png>)

- **Step 8**: Finally, notice that $\angle{ABC}$ and $\angle{ALN}$ are corresponding angles. Furthermore, they are congruent. Therefore, by the *converse corresponding angles postulate*,
$\overline{BC} \parallel \overline{LN}$,
as required.

## Proving the Second Part of the Midpoint Theorem

We'll now learn how to prove the second part of the midpoint theorem.

Consider the diagram below, where $L$ and $N$ are midpoints of the sides $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, respectively, of the triangle $\triangle ABC$.

![](<../Source/4.3.10. Proving the Midpoint Theorem/Images/4e052933fc2f74fe2c32f43edea5b50f.png>)

We wish to prove the following statement:

> $BC =2LN$

The strategy is to show that $\triangle ABC\sim \triangle ALN$, and then use the properties of similar triangles (CPSTP) to prove the desired result.

We complete the proof as follows:

- **Step 1**: We are given that $L$ and $N$ are the midpoints of $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{AC}$, respectively.
- **Step 2**: By the definition of a midpoint, we have
$AB = 2AL, \qquad AC = 2AN$.
- **Step 3**: Using the multiplication principle, we obtain
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = 2, \qquad \dfrac{AC}{AN} = 2$.
- **Step 4**: Applying the *transitive property of equality* to the results from step 3, we get
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = \dfrac{AC}{AN}$.
- **Step 5**: $\angle{BAC}$ and $\angle{LAN}$ represent the same angle. Therefore, by the *reflexive property of congruence*,
$\angle BAC \cong \angle LAN$,
as shown below.

![](<../Source/4.3.10. Proving the Midpoint Theorem/Images/0ade8c4a3b5c0b9086e77c2ff496f80f.png>)

- **Step 6**: In steps 4 and 5, respectively, we showed that
$\dfrac{AB}{AL} = \dfrac{AC} {AN}, \qquad \angle BAC \cong \angle LAN$.
Therefore, $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{ALN}$ by the *SAS similarity criterion*.
- **Step 7**: Since $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{ALN}$, we have
$\dfrac{BC}{LN} = \dfrac{AB}{AL}$
by *CPSTP*.
- **Step 8**: In steps 7 and 3 we showed that
$\dfrac{BC}{LN} = \dfrac{AB}{AL}, \qquad \dfrac{AB}{AL} = 2$.
Then, the *transitive property of equality* gives
$\dfrac{BC}{LN} = 2$.
- **Step 9**: Finally, using the *multiplication principle*, we conclude that
$BC = 2LN$,
as required.

| 6 | $\overline{AC} \parallel \overline{ML}$ | $\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}\textrm{Converse corresponding angles postulate}}$ |

Let's examine each row in turn.

1. Notice that $\angle ABC$ and $\angle LBM$ represent the same angle. Therefore, by the reflexive property of congruence, we have $\angle{ABC} \cong \angle{LBM}$.
2. We're given that $\dfrac{BC}{BM} = 2$ and $\dfrac{AB}{BL} = 2$.
3. From row 2, we have
${\color{blue}{\dfrac{BC}{BM}}} = 2 \quad\textrm{and}\quad 2 = {\color{blue}{\dfrac{AB}{BL}}}$.
Therefore, by the transitive property of equality, we get
${\color{blue}{\dfrac{BC}{BM}}} = {\color{blue}{\dfrac{AB}{BL}}}$.
4. From rows 3 and 1, we have
$\dfrac{BC}{BM} = \dfrac{AB}{BL}, \qquad \angle{ABC} \cong \angle{LBM}$.
Therefore, by the SAS similarity criterion, $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{LBM}$.
5. From row 4, $\triangle{ABC} \sim \triangle{LBM}$. Therefore,
$\angle{BAC} \cong \angle{BLM}$
since they're corresponding angles in similar triangles.
6. Finally, notice that $\angle{BAC}$ and $\angle{BLM}$ are corresponding angles. Furthermore, they are congruent. Therefore, by the converse corresponding angles postulate,
$\overline{AC} \parallel \overline{ML}$.
