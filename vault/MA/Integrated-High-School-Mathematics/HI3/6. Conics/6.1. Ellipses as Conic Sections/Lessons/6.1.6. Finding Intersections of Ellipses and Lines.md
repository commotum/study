## Introduction

Consider the ellipse

$$
\dfrac{x^2}{12} + \dfrac{y^2}{24} = 1
$$

and line $y = 2x$, sketched below. We can see from the diagram that the line and the ellipse intersect at $(2,4)$ and $(-2,-4)$.

![](<../Source/6.1.6. Finding Intersections of Ellipses and Lines/Images/80ceaead2e0a4d58189c9990ae731bd7.png>)

But how can we determine this mathematically, without relying on the graph?

To find the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points, we can substitute $y = 2x$ into the equation of the ellipse and solve the resulting equation for $x$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x^{2}}{12} + \frac{y^{2}}{24} &= 1 \\
\frac{x^{2}}{12} + ((2x)^{2})/(24) &= 1 \\
\frac{x^{2}}{12} + \frac{4x^{2}}{24} &= 1 \\
2x^{2} + 4x^{2} &= 24 \\
6x^{2} &= 24 \\
x^{2} &= 4 \\
x &= ± 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, to find the $y$-coordinates of the intersection points, we can substitute $x = \pm 2$ into the equation of the line and compute the $y$-values.

- Substituting $x = -2$, we get
$y|= 2x; y|= 2 \cdot (-2); y|=-4$.
This tells us that one intersection point is $(-2, -4)$.
- Substituting $x = 2$, we get
$y|= 2x; y|= 2 \cdot 2; y|= 4$.
This tells us that the other intersection point is $(2, 4)$.

So we find that the two intersection points are $(-2,-4)$ and $(2,4)$, which matches up perfectly with what we see in the graph.

## Finding the Points of Intersection of an Ellipse and a Line: Two Points of Intersection

**Example:** Find the points where the ellipse $\dfrac{(x-2)^2}{4}+\dfrac{(y-1)^2}{16}=1$ intersects the line $y = -2x + 9$.

**Explanation**

Let's start by multiplying the equation of the ellipse by $16$ to remove the fractions. This isn't absolutely necessary, but it will make the equation simpler to work with after we substitute in the equation of the line.

$$
\begin{aligned}
((x - 2)^{2})/(4) + ((y - 1)^{2})/(16) &= 1 \\
16 \cdot (((x - 2)^{2})/(4) + ((y - 1)^{2})/(16)) &= 16 \cdot 1 \\
4(x - 2)^{2} + (y - 1)^{2} &= 16
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the points of intersection, we substitute

$$
y =-2x+9
$$

into the equation of the ellipse and solve for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
4(x - 2)^{2} + (y - 1)^{2} &= 16 \\
4(x - 2)^{2} + (-2x + 9 - 1)^{2} &= 16 \\
4(x - 2)^{2} + (-2x + 8)^{2} &= 16 \\
4(x^{2} - 4x + 4) + 4x^{2} - 32x + 64 &= 16 \\
4x^{2} - 16x + 16 + 4x^{2} - 32x + 64 &= 16 \\
8x^{2} - 48x + 64 &= 0 \\
x^{2} - 6x + 8 &= 0 \\
(x - 2)(x - 4) &= 0 \\
x &= 2, 4
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the corresponding $y$-values, we substitute $x=2,4$ into the equation of the line

$$
y = -2x + 9
$$

- Substituting $x=2$, we get
$y = -2\cdot2+9 = 5$.
This tells us that the first point of intersection is $\left(2,5\right)$.
- Substituting $x=4$, we get
$y = -2\cdot4+9 = 1$.
This tells us that the second point of intersection is $\left(4,1\right)$.

Therefore, the intersection points are

$$
\left(2,5\right)
$$

and

$$
\left(4,1\right)
$$

## Finding the Points of Intersection of an Ellipse and a Line: One Point of Intersection

**Example:** Find the point where the ellipse $\dfrac{(x+3)^2}{6}+\dfrac{(y-1)^2}{12}=1$ intersects the line $y = -2x+1$, shown above.

![](<../Source/6.1.6. Finding Intersections of Ellipses and Lines/Images/f54b389a68e147e6100412b44b19ef8b.png>)

**Explanation**

We start by multiplying the equation of the ellipse by $12$ to remove the fractions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
((x + 3)^{2})/(6) + ((y - 1)^{2})/(12) &= 1 \\
12 \cdot (((x + 3)^{2})/(6) + ((y - 1)^{2})/(12)) &= 12 \cdot 1 \\
2(x + 3)^{2} + (y - 1)^{2} &= 12
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the point of intersection, we substitute

$$
y = -2x+1
$$

into the equation of the ellipse and solve for $x$ by completing the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(x + 3)^{2} + (y - 1)^{2} &= 12 \\
2(x + 3)^{2} + (-2x + 1 - 1)^{2} &= 12 \\
2(x + 3)^{2} + 4x^{2} &= 12 \\
2(x^{2} + 6x + 9) + 4x^{2} &= 12 \\
2x^{2} + 12x + 18 + 4x^{2} &= 12 \\
6x^{2} + 12x + 6 &= 0 \\
x^{2} + 2x + 1 &= 0 \\
(x + 1)^{2} &= 0 \\
x &= -1
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the corresponding $y$-value, we substitute $x=-1$ into the equation of the line

$$
y = -2 x+1
$$

This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= -2 \cdot (-1) + 1 = 3
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the point of intersection is

$$
\left(-1,3\right)
$$

## Finding the Points of Intersection of an Ellipse and a Line: No Points of Intersection

**Example:** Find the points where the ellipse $\dfrac{(x-4)^2}{10}+\dfrac{(y+2)^2}{5} = 1$ intersects the line $y = x - 1$.

**Explanation**

We start by multiplying the equation of the ellipse by $10$ to remove the fractions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
((x - 4)^{2})/(10) + ((y + 2)^{2})/(5) &= 1 \\
10(((x - 4)^{2})/(10) + ((y + 2)^{2})/(5)) &= 10 \cdot 1 \\
(x - 4)^{2} + 2(y + 2)^{2} &= 10
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the points of intersection, we substitute $y = x-1$ into the equation of the ellipse and solve for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x - 4)^{2} + 2(y + 2)^{2} &= 10 \\
(x - 4)^{2} + 2(x - 1 + 2)^{2} &= 10 \\
(x - 4)^{2} + 2(x + 1)^{2} &= 10 \\
x^{2} - 8x + 16 + 2x^{2} + 4x + 2 &= 10 \\
3x^{2} - 4x + 8 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

If we compute the discriminant $\mathcal{D}$ of the above quadratic equation, then we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
D &= (-4)^{2} - 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 8 \\
&= 16 - 96 \\
&=-80
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\mathcal{D} =-80 < 0
$$

there are no real solutions of the equation

$$
3x^2 - 4x + 8=0
$$

Therefore, we conclude that the ellipse and the line do not intersect.
