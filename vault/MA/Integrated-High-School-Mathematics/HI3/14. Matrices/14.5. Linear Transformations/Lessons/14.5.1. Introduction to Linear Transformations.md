## Introduction

Consider the function $f(x)$, defined as

$$
f(x) = 2x+1
$$

This function $f$ takes a real number $x$ as input, and it outputs a real number. We can describe this fact using the following notation:

$$
f: x \longrightarrow 2x+1
$$

Similarly, we can define a function $\mathbf T$, also called a **transformation** or a **map**, that takes a 2D-vector (or 3D-vector) as input and outputs a new 2D-vector (or, respectively, 3D-vector).

For example, let's consider the following transformation:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}
$$

This means that for any 2D-vector $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ the corresponding image $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ under the action of $\mathbf{T}$ can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}
$$

We can evaluate this transformation just like we would with any other function. For example, applying the transformation $\mathbf{T}$ to the input vector
$\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$
we get

$$
T\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1 + 3 \\ 2 \cdot 3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}
$$

We say that the transformation $\mathbf T$ *maps* the input vector $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ to the output vector $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="linear-transformations"></a>
## Linear Transformations

A transformation $\mathbf{T}$ is called a **linear transformation** (or **linear map**) if the following two properties are satisfied:

1. $\mathbf{T}(k\mathbf{v})=k\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})$ for any vector $\mathbf{v}$ and any scalar $k$
2. $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2)=\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_1)+\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_2)$ for any vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$

If we let $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ y_{1}\end{bmatrix}$ $\begin{bmatrix}x_{2} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ then the conditions above can be written as follows:

1. $\begin{bmatrix}kx \\ ky\end{bmatrix}$
2. $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + x_{2} \\ y_{1} + y_{2}\end{bmatrix}$

We can determine whether the transformation $\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$ is linear by checking if these two conditions are true.

- Checking the first condition, we have
$T\begin{bmatrix}kx \\ ky\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}(kx) + (ky) \\ 2(ky)\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}k(x + y) \\ k(2y)\end{bmatrix}; = k\begin{bmatrix}x + y \\ 2y\end{bmatrix}; = kT\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}.✓$
- Checking the second condition, we have
$T\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + x_{2} \\ y_{1} + y_{2}\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}(x_{1} + x_{2}) + (y_{1} + y_{2}) \\ 2(y_{1} + y_{2})\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}(x_{1} + y_{1}) + (x_{2} + y_{2}) \\ 2y_{1} + 2y_{2}\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + y_{1} \\ 2y_{1}\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}x_{2} + y_{2} \\ 2y_{2}\end{bmatrix}; = T\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ y_{1}\end{bmatrix} + T\begin{bmatrix}x_{2} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix}.✓$

Both conditions are satisfied, so this transformation is linear.

## Determining the Image of a Vector Under a Map Using the Scalar Multiplication Property

**Example:** If $\mathbf{T}$ is a linear transformation and $\begin{bmatrix}12 \\ 24\end{bmatrix}$ then find $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Since $\mathbf{T}$ is a linear transformation, it must satisfy

$$
\mathbf{T}(k\mathbf{v})=k\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})
$$

Noting that $\begin{bmatrix}12 \\ 24\end{bmatrix}$ we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\begin{bmatrix}12 \\ 24\end{bmatrix}) &= T(6\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}) = 6T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix})
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since we're told that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}12 \\ 24\end{bmatrix}
$$

we must have

$$
\begin{aligned}
6T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}18 \\ -30\end{bmatrix} \\
T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}) &= \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix}18 \\ -30\end{bmatrix} \\
T(\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}) &= \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Determining the Image of a Vector Under a Map Using Vector Addition and Scalar Multiplication

**Example:** Consider a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$. If $\begin{bmatrix}-18 \\ 24\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}14 \\ -12\end{bmatrix}$ then find $\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v}_1)$.

**Explanation**

Since $\mathbf{T}$ is a linear transformation, we have that

$$
T(kv + w) = T(kv) + T(w) = kT(v) + T(w)
$$

for any vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(4v_{1} + v_{2}) &= 4T(v_{1}) + T(v_{2}) \\
\begin{bmatrix}-18 \\ 24\end{bmatrix} &= 4T(v_{1}) + \begin{bmatrix}14 \\ -12\end{bmatrix} \\
4T(v_{1}) &= \begin{bmatrix}-18 \\ 24\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}14 \\ -12\end{bmatrix} \\
4T(v_{1}) &= \begin{bmatrix}-32 \\ 36\end{bmatrix} \\
T(v_{1}) &= \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}-32 \\ 36\end{bmatrix} \\
T(v_{1}) &= \begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 9\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Determining the Image of a Vector Sum Under a Map Using Vector Addition and Scalar Multiplication

**Example:** Consider a linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$. If $\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}9 \\ -12 \\ 8\end{bmatrix}$ then find $\mathbf{T}(17\mathbf{v}_1+12\mathbf{v}_2)$.

**Explanation**

First, since

$$
\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}
$$

we have that

$$
\begin{aligned}
17v_{1} + 12v_{2} &= 5v_{1} + 12v_{1} + 12v_{2} \\
&= 5v_{1} + 12(v_{1} + v_{2}) \\
&= 5v_{1} + 12(0) \\
&= 5v_{1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have

$$
\mathbf{T}(17\mathbf{v}_1+12\mathbf{v}_2)=\mathbf{T}(5\mathbf{v}_1)
$$

Now, since $\mathbf{T}$ is a linear transformation, we have that

$$
\mathbf{T}(k\mathbf{v})=k\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{v})
$$

So

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(5v_{1}) &= 5T(v_{1}) \\
&= 5\begin{bmatrix}9 \\ -12 \\ 8\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}45 \\ -60 \\ 40\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In conclusion, $\begin{bmatrix}45 \\ -60 \\ 40\end{bmatrix}$

## Identifying Linear Transformations

**Example:** Are the following transformations linear?
$\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

A linear transformation $\mathbf{T}$ satisfies two properties:

$$
T\begin{bmatrix}kx \\ ky\end{bmatrix} = kT\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix},; T\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + x_{2} \\ y_{1} + y_{2}\end{bmatrix} = T\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ y_{1}\end{bmatrix} + T\begin{bmatrix}x_{2} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

First, let's consider $\mathbf{T}_1$. Checking the first condition, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{1}\begin{bmatrix}kx \\ ky\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}(kx)^{2} \\ (kx)(ky)\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}k^{2}x^{2} \\ k^{2}xy\end{bmatrix} \\
&= k\begin{bmatrix}kx^{2} \\ kxy\end{bmatrix} \\
&\ne kT_{1}\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}.\cdot
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $\textbf{T}_1$ is not a linear transformation.

Now, let's consider $\mathbf{T}_2$.

- Checking the first condition, we have
$T_{2}\begin{bmatrix}kx \\ ky\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}3kx \\ kx + 2ky\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}k(3x) \\ k(x + 2y)\end{bmatrix}; = k\begin{bmatrix}3x \\ x + 2y\end{bmatrix}; = kT_{2}\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}.✓$

- Checking the second condition, we have
$T_{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + x_{2} \\ y_{1} + y_{2}\end{bmatrix}|= \begin{bmatrix}3(x_{1} + x_{2}) \\ (x_{1} + x_{2}) + 2(y_{1} + y_{2})\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}3x_{1} + 3x_{2} \\ (x_{1} + 2y_{1}) + (x_{2} + 2y_{2})\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}3x_{1} \\ x_{1} + 2y_{1}\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3x_{2} \\ x_{2} + 2y_{2}\end{bmatrix}; = T_{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ y_{1}\end{bmatrix} + T_{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{2} \\ y_{2}\end{bmatrix}.✓$

Therefore, $\textbf{T}_2$ is a linear transformation.
