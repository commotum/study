## Introduction

Remember that the **identity matrix** $I_n$ is an $n \times n$ square matrix with $1$'s on the diagonal, and where all other elements are $0$. For example, the $3 \times 3$ identity matrix $I_3$ is given below:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

The identity matrix gets its name from the fact that whenever we multiply a matrix by the corresponding identity matrix, the matrix remains unchanged.

For example, let's consider the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 3 & 10 \\ 6 & -2 & 2 \\ 23 & 4 & 8\end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying the matrix $A$ by the identity matrix $I_3$, we find that the result is just $A$ itself:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A \cdot I_{3} &= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 3 & 10 \\ 6 & -2 & 2 \\ 23 & 4 & 8\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}(-2) \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0 + 10 \cdot 0 & (-2) \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 1 + 10 \cdot 0 & (-2) \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 0 + 10 \cdot 1 \\ 6 \cdot 1 + (-2) \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0 & 6 \cdot 0 + (-2) \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 & 6 \cdot 0 + (-2) \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 \\ 23 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 + 8 \cdot 0 & 23 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 0 & 23 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0 + 8 \cdot 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 3 & 10 \\ 6 & -2 & 2 \\ 23 & 4 & 8\end{bmatrix} \\
&= A
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
A \cdot I_3 = A
$$

You can check that

$$
I_3 \cdot A = A
$$

using the same method.

This result holds in general:

> For *any* square $n \times n$ matrix $A$, we have
> $A \cdot I_n = I_n \cdot A = A$.

In other words, the $n \times n$ identity matrix $I_n$ has a similar property to the number $1$, namely that

$$
a\cdot 1 = 1\cdot a = a
$$

for any real number $a$.

## Multiplying a Matrix by the Identity Matrix

**Example:** Calculate the product $I_3 B$ given that $I_3$ is the $3 \times 3$ identity matrix and
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3 & -1 \\ 5 & 2 & 7 \\ 0 & 4 & 6\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Any matrix, when multiplied by an identity matrix, remains unchanged (assuming that the multiplication is well defined).

The matrix $I_3$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix and $B$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix too.

Therefore, the multiplication is well defined and
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 3 & -1 \\ 5 & 2 & 7 \\ 0 & 4 & 6\end{bmatrix}$

## Multiplying a Matrix by a Column of the Identity Matrix

Whenever we multiply a matrix $A$ by the $n$th column of the identity matrix, the result is the $n$th column of $A$.

For example, consider the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first column of the identity matrix $I_2$ is given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

If we multiply $A$ by the column on the *right*, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The product is just the first column of the matrix $A$.

Using the same method, you can check for yourself that when we multiply $A$ by the second column of $I_2$, the result is the second column of $A$.

On the other hand, if we multiply $A$ by the first *row* of the identity matrix $I_2$ on the *left*, we will obtain the first row of $A$. Indeed,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & (-1) \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Multiplying a Matrix by a Column or Row of the Identity Matrix

**Example:** Calculate the product $C D$, if
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & -4 \\ 5 & 4 & -2\end{bmatrix}$
and
$\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Notice that $D$ is the $2$nd *column* of the identity matrix $I_3$.

Since we're multiplying the $3\times 3$ matrix $C$ by the $2$nd *column* of the identity matrix $I_3$, the result must be the $2$nd *column* of $C$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Don't believe it? Let's check the long way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
CD &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & -4 \\ 5 & 4 & -2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ -1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 + (-4) \cdot 0 \\ 5 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 + (-2) \cdot 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Proving Properties Related to the Identity Matrix

We've been using the fact that

$$
AI_n = I_n A = A
$$

for any $n \times n$ matrix $A$. But how do we know for sure?

Let's prove the case of $n=2$. That is, let's prove that

$$
AI_2=I_2A = A
$$

where $A$ is any $2\times2$ matrix.

Any general $2 \times 2$ matrix can be written as
$\begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix}$

Then, multiplying by $I_2$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
AI_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}a_{11} \cdot 1 + a_{12} \cdot 0 & a_{11} \cdot 0 + a_{12} \cdot 1 \\ a_{21} \cdot 1 + a_{22} \cdot 0 & a_{21} \cdot 0 + a_{22} \cdot 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= A
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{2}A &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= A
\end{aligned}
$$

The above proof shows that the property

$$
A I_2 = I_2 A = A
$$

holds for all $2\times2$ matrices $A$.

In general, to prove that the property

$$
AI_n = I_n A = A
$$

holds for all $n \times n$ matrices $A$, we can use the same method.
