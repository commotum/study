## Introduction

To specify the position of a point $P$ in the Cartesian plane, we normally use its $x$- and $y$- coordinates, also called the **Cartesian coordinates** of $P$. We then write the position of the point as $P(x,y)$.

For example, the point below has coordinates $x=3$ and $y=4$, so we write it as $P(3,4)$.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/c1ff724a9b5c8c1c287c5faea1cf3ca4.png>)

However, this is not the only way of specifying the position of a point.

We could identify a point by writing down its distance to the origin $(r)$ and the angle that it makes with the positive $x$-axis $(\theta)$, also called the **polar angle**, defined such that

$$
0\leq \theta \lt 2\pi
$$

The coordinates $r$ and $\theta$ are called **polar coordinates**.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/47ab69f1dce2665fe765aaedbdb5b4e6.png>)

Let's see how to express a point $P(x,y)$ in polar coordinates $P(r,\theta)$.

---

<a id="converting-from-cartesian-coordinates-to-polar-coordinates-first-or-fourth-quadrant"></a>
## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates: First or Fourth Quadrant

To express a point $P(x,y)$ in polar coordinates $P(r,\theta)$, we use the Cartesian coordinates and some trigonometry.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/b470db1ef018d925fb321a99ce147d0f.png>)

First, let's find $r$, which is the distance from $P$ to the origin. Notice that in the diagram above, the distance $r$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides $x$ and $y$. So, according to the Pythagorean theorem, we have

$$
r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}
$$

Furthermore, the polar angle $\theta$ is an angle in this right triangle. The opposite side has length $y$ and the adjacent side has length $x$, so we have

$$
\theta = \arctan\left(\dfrac y x\right)
$$

For the point $P(3,4)$, for example, we have that the distance from the origin is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{9 + 16} \\
&= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

and the polar angle is

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= \arctan (\frac{4}{3}) \\
&\approx 0.927
\end{aligned}
$$

rounded to three decimal places.

**Watch out!** The polar angle is always expressed in radians.

Therefore, the point $P(3,4)$ can be expressed in polar coordinates as $P(5,0.927)$.

Computing the polar angle in this instance was pretty straightforward because $P$ is in the first quadrant. When our point lies in one of the other quadrants, it's usually the safest to compute the reference angle $\theta_R$, and then compute the polar angle $\theta$. Let's see an example.

---

<a id="converting-from-cartesian-coordinates-to-polar-coordinates-first-or-fourth-quadrant"></a>
## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates: First or Fourth Quadrant

**Example:** Express the Cartesian point $P (1,-\sqrt{3})$ in polar coordinates.

**Explanation**

The first thing to realize is that the point $P$ lies in the fourth quadrant.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/353b325d00952160420a21721fdb8c2c.png>)

First, we determine $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned} r &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{1^2 + (-\sqrt{3})^2} \\[3pt] &=\sqrt{1+3} \\[3pt] &= 2 \end{aligned}
$$

We then compute the reference angle $\theta_R$:

$$
\begin{aligned} \theta_R &= \left\begin{vmatrix}\arctan\left(\dfrac y x\right)\right & \\ &= \left & \arctan\left(\dfrac{-\sqrt{3}}{1}\right)\right & \\ &= \left & \arctan\left(-\sqrt 3\right)\right & \\ &= \left & -\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right\end{vmatrix} \\ &= \dfrac{\pi}{3} \end{aligned}
$$

Since the point lies in the fourth quadrant, we subtract the reference angle from $2\pi$, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= 2π - θ_{R} \\
&= 2π - \frac{π}{3} \\
&= \frac{5π}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the polar coordinates of $P$ are

$$
\left(2, \dfrac{5\pi}{3}\right)
$$

## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates: Second or Third Quadrant

As we've already seen, if a point does not lie in the first quadrant, then it's usually safest to compute the reference angle $\theta_R$ first.

To demonstrate, let's express the Cartesian point $P (-5, -5)$ in polar coordinates, shown below.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/390bb60b6ea75fb921df0ca8d214d533.png>)

First, we determine $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned} r &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\ &= \sqrt{(-5)^2 + (-5)^2} \\ &=\sqrt{50} \\ &= 5\sqrt{2}. \end{aligned}
$$

For the reference angle, we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \theta_R &= \left\begin{vmatrix}\arctan\left(\dfrac y x\right)\right & \\[5pt] &= \left & \arctan\left(\dfrac {-5}{-5}\right)\right & \\[5pt] &= \left & \arctan(1)\right & \\[5pt] &= \left & \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right\end{vmatrix} \\[5pt] &=\dfrac\pi 4. \end{aligned}
$$

Since the point lies in the third quadrant, we add the reference angle to $\pi$, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= π + θ_{R} \\
&= π + \frac{π}{4} \\
&= \frac{5π}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the polar coordinates of $P$ are

$$
\left(5\sqrt{2},\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\right)
$$

---

<a id="converting-from-cartesian-coordinates-to-polar-coordinates-second-or-third-quadrant"></a>
## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates: Second or Third Quadrant

**Example:** Express the Cartesian point $P (-6, 8)$ in polar coordinates. Round your answer to three decimal places where appropriate.

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/40b1c3db885180812cfbf4edd958ae6a.png>)

First, we determine $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned} r &= \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{(-6)^2 + 8^2} \\[3pt] &=\sqrt{100} \\[3pt] &= 10 \end{aligned}
$$

For the reference angle, we get

$$
\begin{aligned} \theta_R &= \left\begin{vmatrix}\arctan\left(\dfrac y x\right)\right & \\[3pt] &= \left & \arctan\left(-\dfrac 8{6}\right)\right\end{vmatrix} \\[3pt] &\approx 0.927\,29. \end{aligned}
$$

Since the point $P$ lies in the second quadrant, we subtract the reference angle from $\pi$, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= π - θ_{R} \\
&= π - 0.92729 \\
&\approx 2.21429 \\
&\approx 2.214
\end{aligned}
$$

to three decimal places. Therefore, the polar coordinates of $P$ are $(10,2.214)$.

## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates for a Point on an Axis

When the point lies on an axis, we can determine the polar angle by inspection, rather than calculating an inverse tangent that might not be defined.

![](<../Source/11.1.1. Introduction to Polar Coordinates/Images/c9d46895dd1a298bfc816b5ce34b5f2c.png>)

If the point lies on:

- the positive half of $x$-axis, then the polar angle is $\theta=0$.
- the positive half of $y$-axis, then the polar angle is $\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
- the negative half of $x$-axis, then the polar angle is $\theta=\pi$.
- the negative half of $y$-axis, then the polar angle is $\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

---

<a id="converting-from-cartesian-coordinates-to-polar-coordinates-for-a-point-on-an-axis"></a>
## Converting from Cartesian Coordinates to Polar Coordinates for a Point on an Axis

**Example:** Express the Cartesian point $P (0,-7)$ in polar coordinates.

**Explanation**

First, we determine $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned} r &= \sqrt{0^2 + (-7)^2} \\ &= \sqrt{7^2} \\ &= 7 \end{aligned}
$$

Since the point $P$ lies on the negative half of the $y$-axis, we have

$$
\theta=\dfrac{3\pi}{2}
$$

Therefore, the polar coordinates of $P$ are

$$
\left(7, \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right)
$$
