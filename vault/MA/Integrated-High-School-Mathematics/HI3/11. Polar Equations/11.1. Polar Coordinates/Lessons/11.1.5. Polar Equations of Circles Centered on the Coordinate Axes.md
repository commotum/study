## Introduction

When a circle is not centered at the origin, we can still write its equation in polar form. In fact, the polar equation of a circle is particularly straightforward when it passes through the origin and its center lies on the $x$-axis.

For example, let $a$ be some nonzero real number, and consider a circle of radius $\mid a \mid$ with center at the point $(a,0)$.

![](<../Source/11.1.5. Polar Equations of Circles Centered on the Coordinate Axes/Images/903f0955385e8781168dc71dbe77e6e4.png>)

With a little bit of geometry and trigonometry, it turns out that the equation of this circle in polar form is given by

$$
r=2a\cos\theta
$$

We'll derive this result at the end of the lesson.

So, for instance, if $a=2$, then we have a circle of radius $2$ centered at $(2,0)$, and its polar equation is

$$
r= 4 \cos\theta
$$

Similarly, if $a=-2$, then we have a circle of radius

$$
\mid {-2} \mid = 2
$$

centered at $(-2,0)$, and its polar equation is

$$
r= -4 \cos\theta
$$

## The Polar Equation of a Circle Centered on the X-Axis

**Example:** Plot the curve defined by the equation $r=2\cos\theta$ in polar coordinates.

**Explanation**

$$
r=2a\cos\theta
$$

defines a circle of radius $\mid a \mid$ centered at the point $(a,0)$.

$$
r=2\cos\theta
$$

and therefore

$$
a = \dfrac{2}{2} = 1
$$

$$

gives a circle of radius

$$

## Finding the Polar Equation of a Circle Given Its Graph

**Example:** What is the polar equation of the curve shown below?

![](<../Source/11.1.5. Polar Equations of Circles Centered on the Coordinate Axes/Images/fb3e7cb97ab707e8a94a0dfb9c2bf215.png>)

**Explanation**

The diagram shows a circle of radius

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}a & =\end{vmatrix}-4.5 &= 4.5
\end{aligned}
$$

centered at the point $(0,-4.5)$.

In polar coordinates, the equation of such a circle is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 2a\sin θ \\
&= 2 \cdot (-4.5) \cdot \sin θ \\
&=-9\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-polar-equation-of-a-circle-given-a-point-on-the-circumference"></a>
## Finding the Polar Equation of a Circle Given a Point on the Circumference

**Example:** The polar curve $r=k\cos\theta$, where $k$ is a constant, passes through the point $A$ with Cartesian coordinates $\left(-\dfrac72,\dfrac72\right)$. What is the value of $k$?

**Explanation**

First, we find the polar coordinates of the point $A$. The distance from $A$ to the origin is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-\frac{7}{2})^{2} + (\frac{7}{2})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{2(\frac{7}{2})^{2}} \\
&= \frac{7\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since $A$ lies in the second quadrant, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
θ &= π - \arctan \mid \frac{y}{x} \mid \\
&= π - \arctan \mid - ((\frac{7}{2}))/((\frac{7}{2})) \mid \\
&= π - \frac{π}{4} \\
&= \frac{3π}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the polar coordinates of $A$ are

$$
\left(r, \theta\right)=\left(\dfrac{7\sqrt{2}}{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)
$$

To find the value of $k$, we substitute the polar coordinates of the point $A$ into the polar equation of the curve:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= k\cos θ \\
\frac{7\sqrt{2}}{2} &= k\cos (\frac{3π}{4}) \\
\frac{7\sqrt{2}}{2} &= k(-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \\
k &= -7
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the equation of the curve is

$$
r=-7\cos\theta
$$

as shown below.

![](<../Source/11.1.5. Polar Equations of Circles Centered on the Coordinate Axes/Images/99695ad164fced23c816d5d18f52c0c8.png>)

<a id="deriving-the-polar-equation-of-a-circle-centered-at-a0"></a>
## Deriving the Polar Equation of a Circle Centered at (a,0)

![](<../Source/11.1.5. Polar Equations of Circles Centered on the Coordinate Axes/Images/e8e161cc73f98b78d480bfe29225c76d.png>)

Suppose we want to derive the polar equation of a circle with

- center at $(a, 0)$, where $a \ne 0$, and
- radius $\mid a \mid$, so that it passes through the origin.

Let a point $P$ on the circle have polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$. Then, the Cartesian coordinates of $P$ are

$$
(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, \, r\sin\theta)
$$

The distance from $P$ to the center $(a, 0)$ is equal to the radius $\mid a \mid$. We can describe this using the distance formula:

$$
\sqrt{(r\cos\theta - a)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2} = \mid a \mid
$$

Squaring both sides, we get

$$
(r\cos\theta - a)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2 = a^2
$$

Expanding both terms yields

$$
r^2\cos^2\theta - 2ar\cos\theta + a^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta = a^2
$$

Then, we combine like terms:

$$
r^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) - 2ar\cos\theta + a^2 = a^2
$$

Since

$$
\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1
$$

, we can simplify the above as follows:

$$
r^2 - 2ar\cos\theta + a^2 = a^2
$$

Subtracting $a^2$ from both sides gives

$$
r^2 - 2ar\cos\theta = 0
$$

Then, we factor our equation:

$$
r(r - 2a\cos\theta) = 0
$$

So, either $r = 0$ (which corresponds to the origin, already on the circle), or

$$
r = 2a\cos\theta
$$

which is the desired polar equation of the circle.

## Deriving the Polar Equation of a Circle Centered at (0,a)

![](<../Source/11.1.5. Polar Equations of Circles Centered on the Coordinate Axes/Images/20230f2bf8ee224225026f59c7136416.png>)

Suppose we want to derive the polar equation of a circle with

- center at $(0, a)$, where $a \ne 0$, and
- radius $\mid a \mid$, so that it passes through the origin.

Let a point $P$ on the circle have polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$. Then, the Cartesian coordinates of $P$ are

$$
(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, \, r\sin\theta)
$$

The distance from $P$ to the center $(0, a)$ is equal to the radius $\mid a \mid$. We can describe this using the distance formula:

$$
\sqrt{(r\cos\theta - 0)^2 + (r\sin\theta - a)^2} = \mid a \mid
$$

Squaring both sides, we get

$$
(r\cos\theta)^2 + (r\sin\theta - a)^2 = a^2
$$

Expanding both terms yields

$$
r^2\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta - 2ar\sin\theta + a^2 = a^2
$$

Then, we combine like terms:

$$
r^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) - 2ar\sin\theta + a^2 = a^2
$$

Since

$$
\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1
$$

, we simplify:

$$
r^2 - 2ar\sin\theta + a^2 = a^2
$$

Subtracting $a^2$ from both sides gives

$$
r^2 - 2ar\sin\theta = 0
$$

Then, we factor our equation:

$$
r(r - 2a\sin\theta) = 0
$$

So, either $r = 0$ (which corresponds to the origin, already on the circle), or

$$
r = 2a\sin\theta
$$

which is the desired polar equation of the circle.
