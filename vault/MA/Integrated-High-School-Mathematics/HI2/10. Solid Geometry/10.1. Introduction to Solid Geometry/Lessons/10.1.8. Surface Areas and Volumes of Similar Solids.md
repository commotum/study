## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll explore the relationship between surface areas and volumes of similar three-dimensional objects.

We start by stating the relationship between the surface areas of similar shapes:

> *If the lengths of a three-dimensional object are enlarged by a scale factor of $k$, then the surface area of the new shape will be enlarged by a scale factor of $k^2$.*

Consider the following (similar) cubes.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/040e92c44d50456ab7858c431b452f51.png>)

For the smaller cube, the area of each face is

$$
1\,\textrm{cm}\cdot 1\,\textrm{cm} = 1\,\textrm{cm}^2
$$

so the total surface area $\mathcal A_S$ is

$$
\mathcal A_{S} = 6\cdot 1\,\textrm{cm}^2 = 6\,\textrm{cm}^2
$$

The scale factor is

$$
k = \dfrac{{\color{red}{2}}\,\textrm{cm}}{{\color{blue}{1}}\,\textrm{cm}} = 2
$$

Therefore, using the fact that $k=2$, we can compute the surface area of the larger cube as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{L} &= k^{2} \cdot A_{S} \\
&= 2^{2} \cdot 6cm^{2} \\
&= 4 \cdot 6cm^{2} \\
&= 24cm^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can verify that this is correct by computing the surface area of the larger cube directly. Since the area of each face is

$$
2\,\textrm{cm}\cdot 2\,\textrm{cm} = 4\,\textrm{cm}^2
$$

the total surface area $\mathcal A_L$ is

$$
\mathcal A_{L} = 6\cdot 4\,\textrm{cm}^2 = 24\,\textrm{cm}^2
$$

which agrees with our previous calculation.

<a id="volumes-of-similar-solids"></a>
## Volumes of Similar Solids

We now move on to the relationship between the *volumes* of similar shapes:

> *If the lengths of a three-dimensional object are enlarged by a scale factor of $k$, then the volume of the new shape will be enlarged by a scale factor of $k^3$.*

Let's consider our cubes once more.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/082fb1c898581c5dd51b21d91493022c.png>)

The total volume of the smaller cube $V_S$ is

$$
V_{S} = (1\,\textrm{cm})^3 = 1\,\textrm{cm}^3
$$

The scale factor is

$$
k = \dfrac{{\color{red}{2}}\,\textrm{cm}}{{\color{blue}{1}}\,\textrm{cm}} = 2
$$

Using the fact that $k=2$, we can compute the volume of the larger cube as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{L} &= k^{3} \cdot V_{S} \\
&= 2^{3} \cdot 1cm^{3} \\
&= 8 \cdot 1cm^{3} \\
&= 8cm^{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can verify that this is correct by computing the volume of the larger cube directly.

$$
V_{L} = (2\,\textrm{cm})^3 = 8\,\textrm{cm}^3
$$

which agrees with our previous calculation.

<a id="important-similar-solids"></a>
## Important Similar Solids

Before we move on, we should note the following important results.

- All spheres are similar.
- By extension, all hemispheres are similar, all quarter-spheres are similar, and all one-eighth spheres are similar.
- All cubes are similar.

## Computing a Ratio of Two Surface Areas or Volumes

**Example:** Consider the two spheres above (picture not to scale). What is the ratio of the larger sphere's volume to the smaller one's?

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/aa8fc1e05c0e7bf84ff6ba592edca8a8.png>)

**Explanation**

Recall that all spheres are similar.

If two solids are similar with scale factor $k$, then the ratio of the corresponding volumes equals $k^3$.

Here, the larger solid is similar to the smaller one with a scale factor of

$$
k = \dfrac{6\,\textrm{cm}}{3\,\textrm{cm}} = 2
$$

Thus, the volume of the larger solid is
$k^3=2^3=8$
times bigger than the volume of the smaller one.

Therefore, the ratio of the larger volume to the smaller is $8:1$.

## Computing Surface Areas and Volumes

**Example:** The two right cylinders above are similar with a scale factor $k=2$ (not drawn to scale). What is the surface area of the larger cylinder if the surface area of the smaller one is $12\pi \: \textrm{in}^2$?

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/8e231e80530d39e812dc1045081a642a.png>)

*Note: $\pi\approx 3.141\,593$*

**Explanation**

If two solids are similar with scale factor $k$, then the ratio of the corresponding surface areas equals $k^2$.

Therefore,

$$
k^2 = 2^2 = 4
$$

and we have

$$
\dfrac{\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{larger}}}{\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{smaller}}} = 4
$$

Solving for $\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{larger}}$ and substituting

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{smaller}} = 12\pi\,\textrm{in}^2
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{larger} &= 4 \cdot A_{smaller} \\
&= 4 \cdot 12πin^{2} \\
&= 48πin^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-scale-factors-given-surface-areas-and-volumes"></a>
## Computing Scale Factors Given Surface Areas and Volumes

**Example:** Consider the two spheres above (picture not to scale). If the volume of the smaller sphere is equal to $16\pi\,\textrm{mm}^3$ and the volume of the larger sphere is $60\pi\,\textrm{mm}^3$, what is the ratio of the radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ for these solids? Round your answer to one decimal place.

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/d4f411179f082262c3f81de9971abcbb.png>)

**Explanation**

Recall that all spheres are similar.

If two solids are similar with scale factor $k$, then the ratio of the corresponding volumes equals $k^3$.

Computing the ratio of the volumes, we have

$$
\dfrac{\mathcal{V}_{\textrm{larger}}}{\mathcal{V}_{\textrm{smaller}}} = \dfrac{60\pi}{16\pi} = \dfrac{15}{4}
$$

This means that the solids are similar with a scale factor of

$$
k = \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{15}{4}} \approx 1.553\,6
$$

Thus, the radius $r_2$ is $1.553\,6$ times larger than the radius $r_1$. Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{r_2}{r_1} = k = 1.553\,6 \qquad \Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{r_1}{r_2} = \dfrac{1}{1.553\,6} \approx 0.6
$$

## Working With Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids

**Example:** The height of the larger pentagonal prism is $18\, \textrm{in}$. The ratio of the surface area of the smaller prism to the surface area of the larger prism is $49:81$ (not drawn to scale). Given that the two prisms are similar, what is the height of the smaller prism?

![](<../Source/10.1.8. Surface Areas and Volumes of Similar Solids/Images/29791237d9b42c0a89937576c8d1bce7.png>)

**Explanation**

If two solids are similar with scale factor $k$, then the ratio of the corresponding surface areas equals $k^2$.

We are told that

$$
\dfrac{\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{smaller}}}{\mathcal{A}_{\textrm{larger}}} = \dfrac{49}{81}
$$

So, we have that

$$
k^2 = \dfrac{49}{81}\quad\Rightarrow\quad k = \sqrt{\dfrac{49}{81}} = \dfrac{7}{9}
$$

Thus, the height of the smaller prism is

$$
\dfrac{7}{9}
$$

times the height of the larger prism. Therefore, the height of the smaller prism is

$$
h = 18 \cdot \dfrac{7}{9} = 14 \,\textrm{in}
$$
