## Introduction

To solve a radical equation where the radical is isolated on one side of the equation, like

$$
\sqrt{x+1} = 3
$$

we square both sides. Remember that squaring is the opposite of the square root, so

$$
\left(\sqrt{x+1}\right)^2 =x+1
$$

and we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{x + 1})^{2} &= 3^{2} \\
x + 1 &= 9
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we can solve the equation in the standard way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x + 1 &= 9 \\
x &= 8
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's check the result by plugging it into the original equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{(8) + 1} &= ^(?)3 \\
\sqrt{9} &= ^(?)3 \\
3 &= 3✓
\end{aligned}
$$

The value $x=8$ satisfies the original equation, so we can accept it as a solution.

Therefore, the solution to

$$
\sqrt{x+1}=3
$$

is $x=8$.

<a id="solving-a-radical-equation-with-an-isolated-radical"></a>
## Solving a Radical Equation With an Isolated Radical

**Example:** Solve $\sqrt{2x-6} = 2$.

**Explanation**

Squaring both sides of the equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{2x - 6} &= 2 \\
(\sqrt{2x - 6})^{2} &= 2^{2} \\
2x - 6 &= 4 \\
2x &= 10 \\
x &= 5
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's check the result by plugging it into the original equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{2(5) - 6} &= ^(?)2 \\
\sqrt{4} &= ^(?)2 \\
2 &= 2✓
\end{aligned}
$$

The value $x=5$ satisfies the original equation, so we can accept it as a solution.

Therefore, the solution to

$$
\sqrt{2x-6} = 2
$$

is $x=5$.

## Radical Equations with No Solutions

Some radical equations have no solutions. For example, consider the radical equation

$$
\sqrt{3-x} = -2
$$

The square root of a number is always positive or zero. So in the equation above, the right-hand side is negative, while the left-hand side is always positive or zero. Consequently the two sides will never be equal, and there is no real solution.

We could also determine that there is no solution by using the same process as usual. If we try to solve this equation by squaring both sides of the equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{3 - x} &= -2 \\
(\sqrt{3 - x})^{2} &= (-2)^{2} \\
3 - x &= 4 \\
x &= -1
\end{aligned}
$$

However, when we plug this result into the original equation, we get a false statement:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{3 - (-1)} &= ^(?) - 2 \\
\sqrt{4} &= ^(?) - 2 \\
&= 2 \mid \ne - 2\cdot
\end{aligned}
$$

The value $x=-1$ does *not* satisfy the original equation, so we *cannot* accept it as a solution.

Therefore, the equation

$$
\sqrt{3-x}=-2
$$

has no real solution.

<a id="identifying-radical-equations-with-no-real-solution"></a>
## Identifying Radical Equations With No Real Solution

**Example:** Which of the following equations have no real solution?

1. $\sqrt{5-x} = -2$
2. $\sqrt{4-x} = 5$
3. $\sqrt{2x+1} = -1$

**Explanation**

Remember that a square root is always positive or zero. So, a radical equation has no real solution if it states that a square root is equal to a negative number.

Of the given equations, only I and III state that a square root is equal to a negative number. Therefore, equations I and III have no real solution.

So, the correct answer is "I and III only".

<a id="solving-a-radical-equation-when-the-radical-is-not-isolated"></a>
## Solving a Radical Equation When the Radical Is Not Isolated

**Example:** Solve $\sqrt{x^2-5x+8} -2 = 0$.

**Explanation**

First, we isolate the square root by adding $2$ to both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{x^{2} - 5x + 8} - 2 &= 0 \\
\sqrt{x^{2} - 5x + 8} &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Now that the square root is isolated, we can proceed as usual. Squaring both sides of the equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{x^{2} - 5x + 8})^{2} &= (2)^{2} \\
x^{2} - 5x + 8 &= 4 \\
x^{2} - 5x + 4 &= 0 \\
x^{2} - 4x - x + 4 &= 0 \\
(x - 4)(x - 1) &= 0 \\
&= x = 4, 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's check the results by plugging them into the original equation.

- For $x=1$, we get
$\sqrt{1^{2} - 5(1) + 8} - 2|= ^(?)0; \sqrt{4}|= ^(?)2; 2|= 2.✓$
- For $x=4$, we get
$\sqrt{4^{2} - 5(4) + 8} - 2|= ^(?)0; \sqrt{4}|= ^(?)2; 2|= 2.✓$

Both $x=1$ and $x=4$ satisfy the original equation, so we can accept them both as solutions.

Therefore, the solutions to

$$
\sqrt{x^2-5x+8} -2 = 0
$$

are $x=1$ and $x=4$.

<a id="equations-containing-additional-linear-terms"></a>
## Equations Containing Additional Linear Terms

When we want to solve a square root radical equation which has the square root on the left-hand side and a linear function on the right-hand side, like

$$
\sqrt{2x-1}=x
$$

we raise both sides to the power of $2$. This allows us to get rid of the radical, and gives us a quadratic equation that is easier to solve:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{2x - 1})^{2} &= (x)^{2} \\
2x - 1 &= x^{2} \\
x^{2} - 2x + 1 &= 0 \\
(x - 1)^{2} &= 0 \\
x &= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Remember that in square root equations, we should always check the obtained value(s) to ensure that they are actually solutions of the original equation.

Here, we check that the obtained value $x=1$ is actually a solution of the original equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{2(1) - 1} &= ^(?)(1) \\
\sqrt{1} &= 1^(?) \\
1 &= 1✓
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the equation

$$
\sqrt{2x-1}=x
$$

has the solution $x=1$.

## Solving a Radical Equation With an Additional Linear Term

**Example:** Solve $\sqrt{x+10} = x-2$.

**Explanation**

To solve the equation

$$
\sqrt{x+10} = x-2
$$

we raise both sides to the power of $2$. This allows us to get rid of the radical, and gives us a quadratic equation that is easier to solve:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{x + 10} &= x - 2 \\
(\sqrt{x + 10})^{2} &= (x - 2)^{2} \\
x + 10 &= x^{2} - 4x + 4 \\
x^{2} - 5x - 6 &= 0 \\
x^{2} - 6x + x - 6 &= 0 \\
(x + 1)(x - 6) &= 0 \\
x &= -1, 6
\end{aligned}
$$

Remember that in square root equations, we should always check the obtained value(s) to ensure that they are actually solutions of the original equation.

- First, we check whether the obtained value $x=-1$ is a solution of the original equation:
$\sqrt{-1 + 10}|= ^(?) - 1 - 2; \sqrt{9}|= ^(?) - 3; 3 \mid \ne - 3\cdot$
The value $x=-1$ does *not* satisfy the original equation, so we discard it.
- Second, we check whether the obtained value $x=6$ is a solution of the original equation:
$\sqrt{6 + 10}|= ^(?)6 - 2; \sqrt{16}|= ^(?)4; 4|= 4✓$
Indeed, the value $x=6$ does satisfy the original equation.

Therefore, the only solution of

$$
\sqrt{x+10} = x-2
$$

is $x=6$.
