## Introduction

To simplify radical expressions, we can use the quotient rule for radicals, which states

$$
\dfrac {\sqrt {a}} {\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\dfrac a b}
$$

For example, let's simplify

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{16w^2}{25}}
$$

First, we put the numerator and denominator under separate radicals and then simplify the result:

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac{16w^2}{25}} = \dfrac {\sqrt{16w^2}} {\color{blue}\sqrt{25}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{16w^2}}{\color{blue}\sqrt{5^2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{16w^2}}{\color{blue}5}
$$

Now, notice that the expression inside the radical is a perfect square. So, we can continue simplifying by writing the expression under the radical as a perfect square and taking its square root:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{16w^{2}}}{5} &=  \\
(\sqrt{(4w)^{2}})/(5) &=  \\
\frac{\mid 4w\begin{vmatrix}}{5} &= \\ \frac{\begin{vmatrix}4 & \cdot & w\end{vmatrix}}{5}|= \\ \frac{4 \cdot \mid w \mid}{5}|= \\ \frac{4 \mid w\end{vmatrix}}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying Radical Expressions With Perfect Squares Under the Radical

**Example:** Simplify the expression $\sqrt{\dfrac{4z^2}{9}}$.

**Explanation**

First, we put the numerator and denominator under separate radicals and then simplify the result:

$$
\sqrt{\dfrac {4z^2} {9}} = \dfrac {\sqrt{4z^2}} {\sqrt{9}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{4z^2}}{\sqrt{3^2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{4z^2}}{3}
$$

Now, notice that the expression inside the radical is a perfect square. So, we can continue simplifying by writing the expression as this perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{4z^{2}}}{3} &=  \\
(\sqrt{(2z)^{2}})/(3) &=  \\
\frac{\mid 2z\begin{vmatrix}}{3} &= \\ \frac{\begin{vmatrix}2 & \cdot & z\end{vmatrix}}{3}|= \\ \frac{2 \cdot \mid z \mid}{3}|= \\ \frac{2 \mid z\end{vmatrix}}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying Radical Expressions With Perfect Fourths Under the Radical

**Example:** Simplify the expression $\dfrac{\sqrt{45a^4}}{\sqrt{5}}$.

**Explanation**

First, we bring the quotient under a single radical and then simplify the result:

$$
\dfrac{\sqrt{45a^4}}{\sqrt{5}} = \sqrt{\dfrac {45a^4} {5}}= \sqrt{9a^4}
$$

Now, notice that the expression inside the radical is a perfect square. So, we can continue simplifying by writing the expression as this perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{9a^{4}} &=  \\
\sqrt{(3a^{2})^{2}} &=  \\
\mid 3a^{2} \mid &=  \\
\begin{vmatrix}3 & \cdot & a^{2}\end{vmatrix} &=  \\
&= 3 \cdot \mid a^{2} \mid
\end{aligned}
$$

A squared number is always positive or zero, and the absolute value of any positive (or zero) number is itself, so we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \cdot \mid a^{2} \mid &=  \\
3 \cdot a^{2} &=  \\
&= 3a^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying a Radical Expression With Domain Restrictions

**Example:** Simplify $\dfrac {\sqrt {32x^5}} {\sqrt{2x^3}}$ given that $x > 0$.

**Explanation**

First, we bring the quotient under a single radical and then simplify the result:

$$
\begin{aligned} \dfrac {\sqrt {32x^5}} {\sqrt{2x^3}} = \sqrt {\dfrac {32x^5} {2x^3}} = \sqrt {16x^2} \end{aligned}
$$

Now, notice that the expression inside the radical is a perfect square. So, we can continue simplifying by writing the expression as this perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt {16x^2} \\[5pt] &= \sqrt {4^2x^2} \\[5pt] &= \sqrt {(4x)^2} \\[5pt] &= \begin{vmatrix}4x & \\[5pt] &= & 4 & \cdot & x & \\[5pt] &= 4 \cdot & x\end{vmatrix} \end{aligned}
$$

Lastly, we are told that $x>0$, so we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid x &= x
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the final answer is

$$
\begin{aligned} 4 \cdot \mid x \mid \\[5pt] &= 4 \cdot x \\[5pt] &= 4x. \end{aligned}
$$

## The Quotient Rule for Cube Roots and Other Radicals

The quotient rule for radicals also works for higher-order roots, like the cube root:

$$
\sqrt[3]{\dfrac a b} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{b}}
$$

In general, we have the following result:

$$
\sqrt[n]{\dfrac a b} = \dfrac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}
$$
