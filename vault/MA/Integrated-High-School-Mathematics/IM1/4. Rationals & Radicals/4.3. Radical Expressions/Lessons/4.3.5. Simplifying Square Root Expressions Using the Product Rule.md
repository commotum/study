## Introduction

The **product rule for radicals** states that the square root of a product equals the product of the square roots:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{ab} &=\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} \end{aligned}
$$

We can use the product rule to rewrite the square roots of algebraic expressions.

As an example, let's suppose we want to simplify the following expression:

$$
\sqrt{32y}
$$

First, we rewrite $32y$ as the product $16 \cdot 2y$, which contains a perfect square $(16){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{32y} \\[5pt] &= \sqrt{16 \cdot 2y} \\[5pt] &= \sqrt{4^2 \cdot 2y}& \end{aligned}
$$

Then, we separate the product into two separate radicals using the product rule:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{4^2\cdot 2y} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{4^2}\cdot \sqrt{2y}& \end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we simplify the radical containing the perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{4^2}\cdot \sqrt{2y} \\[3pt] &= \mid 4 \mid \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\[3pt] &= 4\sqrt{2y}& \end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying a Radical Expression That Is Not a Perfect Square

**Example:** Simplify $\sqrt{8z^3}$.

**Explanation**

We first write $8z^3$ as the product $4z^2 \cdot 2z$ which contains a perfect square $(4z^2){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{8z^3} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{4z^2 \cdot 2z}& \end{aligned}
$$

Then, we can separate the product into two separate radicals:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{4z^2 \cdot 2z} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{(2z)^2\cdot 2z} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{(2z)^2}\cdot \sqrt{2z}& \end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we simplify the radical containing the perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{(2z)^2}\cdot \sqrt{2z} \\[3pt] &= \begin{vmatrix}2z & \cdot \sqrt{2z} \\[3pt] &= & 2 & \cdot & z & \cdot \sqrt{2z} \\[3pt] &= 2 & z\end{vmatrix}\sqrt{2z}& \end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying a Radical Expression That Is Not a Perfect Square With Larger Powers

**Example:** Simplify $\sqrt{50u^7}$.

**Explanation**

We first write $50u^7$ as the product $25u^6\cdot 2u$ which contains a perfect square $(25u^6){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{50u^7} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{25u^6\cdot 2u}& \end{aligned}
$$

Then, we separate the product into two separate radicals:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{25u^6\cdot 2u} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{\left(5u^3\right)^2\cdot 2u} \\[3pt] &= \sqrt{\left(5u^3\right)^2}\cdot\sqrt{2u}& \end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we simplify the radical containing the perfect square:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\left(5u^3\right)^2}\cdot\sqrt{2u} \\[3pt] &= \begin{vmatrix}5u^3 & \cdot\sqrt{2u} \\[3pt] &= & 5 & \cdot & u^3 & \cdot\sqrt{2u} \\[3pt] &= 5 & u\end{vmatrix}^3 \sqrt{2u} \end{aligned}
$$

## Simplifying a Radical Expression That Is Not a Perfect Square With Domain Restrictions

**Example:** Simplify $\sqrt{18y^3}$ given that $y \geq 0$.

**Explanation**

We first write $18y^3$ as the product $9y^2\cdot 2y$ which contains a perfect square $(9y^2)$. Then, we separate the product into two separate radicals, one of which can be simplified:

$$
\begin{aligned} \sqrt{18y^3} &= \ \sqrt{9y^2 \cdot 2y} &= \ \sqrt{9y^2} \cdot \sqrt{2y} &= \ \sqrt{(3y)^2} \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\ &= \begin{vmatrix}3y & \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\ &= & 3 & \cdot & y & \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\ &= 3 \cdot & y\end{vmatrix} \cdot \sqrt{2y} \end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
y \geq 0
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid y &= y
\end{aligned}
$$

and we can simplify further. Therefore, our final answer is

$$
\begin{aligned} 3 \cdot \mid y \mid \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\ &= 3 \cdot y \cdot \sqrt{2y} \\ &= 3y\sqrt{2y}. & \end{aligned}
$$
