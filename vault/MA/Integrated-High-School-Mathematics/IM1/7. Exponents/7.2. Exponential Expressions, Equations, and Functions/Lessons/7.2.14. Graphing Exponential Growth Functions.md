## Introduction

Suppose we wish to plot the graph of $y=2^x$. First, we create a table of values:

| $x$ | $-3$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y = 2^x$ | $\dfrac18$ | $\dfrac14$ | $\dfrac12$ | $1$ | $2$ | $4$ | $8$ |

Now, we can plot the graph:

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/0c77a293f9850617e3abd9c17cdfc6d7.png>)

Note the following key points:

1. The graph has a $y$-intercept at $(0,1)$.
2. The graph is increasing.
3. For $x>0$, the graph grows very rapidly as $x$ increases. Symbolically, we write $y\rightarrow \infty$ as $x\rightarrow \infty$.
4. For $x<0$, the graph gets very close to zero as $x$ decreases. However, it never actually reaches zero, so the graph has the horizontal asymptote $y=0$. Symbolically, we write $y\rightarrow 0$ as $x\rightarrow -\infty$.
5. The domain is $x\in (-\infty, \infty)$, and the range is $y>0$.

These key points apply to *any* exponential curve $y=a^x$ with $a>1$. These graphs always have the same general shape:

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/3e4c82d1435a936406afb42993a1fb47.png>)

## Sketching the Graph of an Exponential Growth Function

**Example:** Sketch the graph of $f(x)=3^x$.

**Explanation**

The graph of $y=3^x$ has a $y$-intercept at $(0,1)$, grows rapidly for $x>0$, and approaches the asymptote $y=0$ for $x<0$.

When $x=1$, we have $y=3^1 = 3$. Therefore, another point on the graph is $(1,3)$.

We draw the graph through this point as follows:

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/d3c3eb9ec60b3448bb0e56c8bba5fa66.png>)

<a id="identifying-true-statements-about-exponential-growth-functions"></a>
## Identifying True Statements about Exponential Growth Functions

**Example:** Which of the following statements are true in relation to the function $f(x) = 3^x$?

1. It passes through the point $(1,3)$.
2. $f(x)\rightarrow -\infty$ as $x\rightarrow -\infty$.
3. The graph of $y=f(x)$ has a vertical asymptote $x=0$.

**Explanation**

Let's start by recalling the graph of $y=3^x$:

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/ded7522f9e1df47f11118c35775c5268.png>)

Now, let's look at each statement:

- Statement I is true because $f(1) = 3^1 = 3$.
- Statement II is false, since $f(x)\rightarrow 0$ as $x\rightarrow -\infty$.
- Statement III is false. The graph has a *horizontal* asymptote $y=0$.

In conclusion, only statement I is true.

<a id="vertical-stretching"></a>
## Vertical Stretching

Multiplying an exponential growth function by a positive number **scales** the graph in the vertical direction.

For example, the curve $y=2(3^x)$ represents a vertical scaling of $y=3^x$ by a factor of $2$. It takes all of the $y$-coordinates on $y=3^x$ and multiplies them by $2$. This has the effect of **stretching** the curve upwards.

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/f0553484ff419d168523e5533688538a.png>)

Notice that the horizontal asymptote at $y=0$ remained the same, and the $y$-intercept moved from $(0,1)$ to $(0,2)$.

In general, for *any* vertically stretched exponential curve $y=k(a^x)$,

- the horizontal asymptote at $y=0$ remains the same, and
- the $y$-intercept moves from $(0,1)$ to $(0,k)$.

<a id="vertical-compression"></a>
## Vertical Compression

The curve

$$
y=\dfrac{1}{3}(3^x)
$$

represents a vertical scaling of $y=3^x$ by a factor of

$$
\dfrac{1}{3}
$$

It takes all of the $y$-coordinates on $y=3^x$ and multiplies them by

$$
\dfrac{1}{3}
$$

This has the effect of squashing, or **compressing**, the curve downwards.

![](<../Source/7.2.14. Graphing Exponential Growth Functions/Images/60cb0930982c6cf1b6e40530e79a6198.png>)

Notice that the horizontal asymptote at $y=0$ remained the same, and the $y$-intercept moved from $(0,1)$ to

$$
\left(0,\dfrac{1}{3} \right)
$$

In general for *any* vertically stretched exponential curve $y=k(a^x)$,

- the horizontal asymptote at $y=0$ remains the same, and
- the $y$-intercept moves from $(0,1)$ to $(0,k)$.
