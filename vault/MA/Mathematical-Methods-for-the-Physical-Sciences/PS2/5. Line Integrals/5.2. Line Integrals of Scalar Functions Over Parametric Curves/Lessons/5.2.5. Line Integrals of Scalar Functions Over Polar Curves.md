## Introduction

Suppose that we want to evaluate the line integral with respect to arc length

$$
\int\limits_Cf(x,y)\:\textrm{d}s
$$

for some function $f(x,y)$, where $C$ is a curve in the $xy$-plane defined using plane polar coordinates as

$$
r = r(\theta),\qquad \theta_1 \leq \theta \leq \theta_2
$$

To calculate this line integral, we need to recall the following facts:

- A polar curve is just a special type of parametric curve in the parameter $\theta$, where
$x = r(\theta)\cos\theta, \quad y = r(\theta)\sin\theta, \qquad \theta_1 \leq \theta \leq \theta_2$.
- The arc length element $\textrm d s$ in plane polar coordinates is given by
$\textrm ds = \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm d r}{\textrm d \theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm d \theta\,$.

Substituting the above formulas into our line integral, we get the following result:

$$
\int\limits_C f(x,y) \, \textrm{d}s = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \,\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm d r}{\textrm d \theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm d \theta\,
$$

Let's go through an example in which we apply this result.

## Reducing the Line Integral of a Scalar Function Along a Curve to a Definite Integral

**Example:** Find a definite integral with respect to $\theta$ that is equivalent to $\displaystyle \int \limits_{C} (y^2-x) \, \textrm ds$, where $C$ is the section of the polar curve $r = \cos{\theta}\:$ for $0 \le \theta \le\dfrac{\pi}4$.

**Explanation**

In polar coordinates, the formula for the line integral is given by

$$
\int\limits_C f(x,y) \, \textrm{d}s = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \,\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm d r}{\textrm d \theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm d \theta\,
$$

The connection between polar and Cartesian coordinates gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos θ \\
&= \cos θ \cdot \cos θ \\
&= \cos^{2} θ, \\
y &= r\sin θ \\
&= \cos θ \cdot \sin θ \\
&= \cos θ\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

So, along our curve, we have

$$
x=\cos^2\theta
$$

and

$$
y=\cos\theta \sin{\theta}
$$

, where

$$
0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}4
$$

Now, if

$$
f(x,y) =y^2-x
$$

, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ) &= (\cos θ\sin θ)^{2} - \cos^{2} θ \\
&= \cos^{2} θ\sin^{2} θ - \cos^{2} θ \\
&= \cos^{2} θ(\sin^{2} θ - 1) \\
&= \cos^{2} θ(-\cos^{2} θ) \\
&=-\cos^{4} θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dr}{dθ} &= \frac{d}{dθ}(\cos θ) =-\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ &= \sqrt{(\cos θ)^{2} + (-\sin θ)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ} \\
&= \sqrt{1} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we can write the integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)(y^{2} - x)ds &= ∫_{0}^{π/4}f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ)\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/4} - \cos^{4} θ \cdot 1 \cdot dθ \\
&= - ∫_{0}^{π/4}\cos^{4} θdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Line Integral of a Scalar Function Along a Curve Given in Polar Form

**Example:** Evaluate the integral of the function $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{y^2}$ along the polar curve $r=1$ for $\dfrac{\pi}{6}\leq \theta \leq \dfrac\pi 2$.

**Explanation**

In polar coordinates, the formula for the line integral is given by

$$
\int\limits_C f(x,y) \, \textrm{d}s = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \,\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm d r}{\textrm d \theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm d \theta\,
$$

The connection between polar and Cartesian coordinates gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos θ = \cos θ, \\
y &= r\sin θ = \sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

So, along our curve, we have

$$
x=\cos\theta
$$

and

$$
y=\sin\theta
$$

, where

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{6} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}2
$$

Now, since

$$
f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2+y^2}{y^2}
$$

, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ) &= \frac{\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ}{\sin^{2} θ} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sin^{2} θ} \\
&= \csc^{2} θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dr}{dθ} &= \frac{d}{dθ}(1) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

and therefore

$$
\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ = \sqrt{1^{2} + 0^{2}} = 1
$$

Finally, we now evaluate the integral, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)\frac{x^{2} + y^{2}}{y^{2}}ds &= ∫_{π/6}^{π/2}f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ)\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/6}^{π/2}\csc^{2} θ \cdot 1 \cdot dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/6}^{π/2}\csc^{2} θdθ \\
&=-\cot θ \mid _{π/6}^{π/2} \\
&= - (0 - \sqrt{3}) \\
&= \sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Line Integral of a Scalar Function Along a Curve Using Substitution

**Example:** Evaluate the integral $\displaystyle\int_\limits{C} xy(1-x^2) \,\textrm{d}s$, where $C$ is the polar curve $r=\cos{\theta}\:$ for $0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

**Explanation**

In polar coordinates, the formula for the line integral is given by

$$
\int\limits_C f(x,y) \, \textrm{d}s = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta) \,\sqrt{r^2 + \left(\dfrac{\textrm d r}{\textrm d \theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm d \theta\,
$$

The connection between polar and Cartesian coordinates gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos θ \\
&= \cos θ \cdot \cos θ \\
&= \cos^{2} θ, \\
y &= r\sin θ \\
&= \cos θ \cdot \sin θ \\
&= \cos θ\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

So, along our curve, we have

$$
x=\cos^2\theta
$$

and

$$
y=\cos\theta\sin\theta
$$

, where

$$
0 \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

Now, if

$$
f(x,y) = xy(1-x^2)
$$

, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ) &= \cos^{2} θ \cdot \cos θ\sin θ(1 - (\cos^{2} θ)^{2}) \\
&= \cos^{3} θ\sin θ(1 - \cos^{4} θ)
\end{aligned}
$$

Next, we have

$$
\frac{dr}{dθ} = \frac{d}{dθ}(\cos θ) =-\sin θ
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ &= \sqrt{(\cos θ)^{2} + (-\sin θ)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now write the integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C)xy(1 - x^{2})ds &= ∫_{0}^{π/2}f(r\cos θ, r\sin θ)\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\cos^{3} θ\sin θ(1 - \cos^{4} θ) \cdot 1 \cdot dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π/2}\cos^{3} θ\sin θ(1 - \cos^{4} θ)dθ
\end{aligned}
$$

We can solve this using the substitution

$$
u=\cos\theta
$$

$$
\textrm d u=-\sin\theta\,\textrm d \theta
$$

as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{π/2}\cos^{3} θ\sin θ(1 - \cos^{4} θ)dθ &= ∫_{1}^{0} - u^{3}(1 - u^{4})du \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(u^{3} - u^{7})du \\
&= [\frac{u^{4}}{4} - \frac{u^{8}}{8}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= (\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{8}) - 0 \\
&= \frac{1}{8}
\end{aligned}
$$
