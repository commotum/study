## Introduction

Suppose we want to evaluate the line integral

$$
\oint\limits_{C}\left(e^{-x^2}- x^2y \right) \: \mathrm{d}x + \left(y+xy^2\right) \mathrm{d}y
$$

where $C$ is the unit circle

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 1
$$

oriented counterclockwise.

Direct evaluation of this integral is likely to be a difficult task. However, we can make light work of this tricky integral using Green's theorem.

Recall that Green's theorem states the following:

$$
\oint\limits_{C} P\,\mathrm{d}x + Q\,\mathrm{d}y = \iint\limits_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\,\textrm d A
$$

In our case, the region $D$ and its boundary $C$ (shown below) satisfy the conditions of Green's theorem.

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/88976489fcd564463c3155eaa2fca79c.png>)

Now, since

$$
P(x,y) = e^{-x^2}- x^2y, \qquad Q(x,y) = y+xy^2
$$

we obtain the partial derivatives

$$
\frac{∂P}{∂y} =-x^{2}, \frac{∂Q}{∂x} = y^{2}
$$

Applying Green's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∮_(C)(e^{-x^{2}} - x^{2}y)dx + (y + xy^{2})dy &= ∬_(D)(\frac{∂Q}{∂x} - \frac{∂P}{∂y})dA \\
&= ∬_(D)y^{2} - (-x^{2})dA \\
&= ∬_(D)x^{2} + y^{2}dA
\end{aligned}
$$

This double integral will be easier to evaluate if we switch to plane polar coordinates.

The region $D$ in $xy$-coordinates can be represented as the region $\Delta$ in polar coordinates, given by

$$
\Delta = \big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 0 \leq r \leq 1, \: 0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi \big\}
$$

The change of variables formula for plane polar coordinates states that

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r \cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Finally, we can calculate our double integral by applying the change of variables, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)x^{2} + y^{2}dA &= ∬_(Δ)(r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ)rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{0}^{1}r^{3}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ)drdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{0}^{1}r^{3}drdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}[\frac{r^{4}}{4}]_{0}^{1}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}\frac{1}{4}dθ \\
&= 2π \cdot \frac{1}{4} \\
&= \frac{π}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Expressing a Line Integral Along a Circular Path as a Double Integral Using Green's Theorem

**Example:** Given that
$AAAAAAAA$
where $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \left\langle y^2+\sin x, \: 3xy-\cos y \right\rangle$ and $C$ is the circle $x^2+y^2=4$ oriented counterclockwise, what is the missing part of the equation?

**Explanation**

First, notice that we can rewrite our integral as

$$
\displaystyle{\oint\limits_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}} = \oint\limits_{C} (y^2+\sin x) \: \mathrm{d}x + (3xy-\cos y) \: \mathrm{d}y
$$

Green's theorem states that

$$
\oint\limits_{C} P\,\mathrm{d}x + Q\,\mathrm{d}y = \iint\limits_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\,\textrm d A
$$

In our case, the region $D$ and its boundary $C$ satisfy the conditions of Green's theorem:

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/629b2ed342ba6afd917f54d19b0f1b7a.png>)

Since

$$
P(x,y)= y^2+\sin x
$$

and

$$
Q(x,y)= 3xy-\cos y
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{∂P}{∂y} = 2y, \frac{∂Q}{∂x} = 3y
$$

Applying Green's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∮_(C)(y^{2} + \sin x)dx + (xy - \cos y)dy &= ∬_(D)(\frac{∂Q}{∂x} - \frac{∂P}{∂y})dA \\
&= ∬_(D)(3y - 2y)dA \\
&= ∬_(D)ydA
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, notice that the region $D$ can be represented in polar coordinates as

$$
\Delta = \big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 0\leq r \leq 2,\: 0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi \big\}
$$

The change of variables formula for plane polar coordinates states that

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r \cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Therefore, we can write our double integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)ydA &= ∬_(Δ)r\sin θrdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{0}^{2}r 2 sin ⁡ θdrdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the missing expression is $r^2\sin\theta$.

## Expressing a Line Integral Along a Path as a Double Integral Using Green's Theorem

**Example:** Consider the path $C$ shown above and the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \left\langle 2y + \ln{(\sqrt{x+5})}, \: e^{y/3} + 3x \right\rangle$.
Find a repeated integral in plane polar coordinates that's equivalent to $\displaystyle \oint\limits_{C} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r} \,$.

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/f328d63e617baaed38300a4f68a3df63.png>)

**Explanation**

First, notice that we can rewrite our integral as

$$
\displaystyle{\oint\limits_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}} = \oint\limits_{C} (2y + \ln{(\sqrt{x+5})}) \: \mathrm{d}x + \left(e^{y/3} + 3x \right) \: \mathrm{d}y
$$

Green's theorem states that

$$
\oint\limits_{C} P\,\mathrm{d}x + Q\,\mathrm{d}y = \iint\limits_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\,\textrm d A
$$

In our case, the region $D$ and its boundary $C$ satisfy the conditions of Green's theorem:

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/e8dfd43252a2b65794b5a03ca7b3995d.png>)

Since

$$
P(x,y)= 2y + \ln{(\sqrt{x+5})}
$$

and

$$
Q(x,y)=e^{y/3} + 3x
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{∂P}{∂y} = 2, \frac{∂Q}{∂x} = 3
$$

Applying Green's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∮_(C)(2y + \ln (\sqrt{x + 5}))dx + (e^{y/3} + 3x)dy &= ∬_(D)(\frac{∂Q}{∂x} - \frac{∂P}{∂y})dA \\
&= ∬_(D)(3 - 2)dA \\
&= ∬_(D)dA
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, notice that the region $D$ can be represented in polar coordinates as

$$
\Delta = \left\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 0 \leq r \leq 3, \: \dfrac{\pi}{2} \leq \theta \leq 2\pi \right\}
$$

The change of variables formula for plane polar coordinates states that

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r \cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Therefore, we can write our double integral as

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)dA &= ∬_(Δ)rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{π/2}^{2π}∫_{0}^{3}rdrdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating a Line Integral Using Green's Theorem

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle\oint\limits_{C} \left(e^{-x^2}-y^3\right) \mathrm{d}x + (e^{-y^2}+x^3) \: \mathrm{d}y$, where the path $C$ is shown above.

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/2e2cf25681d84163eb1887aebdea07b0.png>)

**Explanation**

Green's theorem states that

$$
\oint\limits_{C} P\,\mathrm{d}x + Q\,\mathrm{d}y = \iint\limits_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\,\textrm d A
$$

In our case, the region $D$ and its boundary $C$ satisfy the conditions of Green's theorem.

![](<../Source/5.6.2. Green's Theorem in Polar Coordinates/Images/80b659f9413d74a0171fcf45372ad34d.png>)

Now, since

$$
P(x,y) = e^{-x^2}-y^3, \qquad Q(x,y) = e^{-y^2}+x^3
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{∂P}{∂y} =-3y^{2}, \frac{∂Q}{∂x} = 3x^{2}
$$

Applying Green's theorem, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∮_(C)(e^{-x^{2}} - y^{3})dx + (e^{-y^{2}} + x^{3})dy &= ∬_(D)(\frac{∂Q}{∂x} - \frac{∂P}{∂y})dA \\
&= ∬_(D)(3x^{2} - (-3y^{2}))dA \\
&= ∬_(D)3(x^{2} + y^{2})dA
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, notice that the region $D$ can be represented in polar coordinates as

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 0 \leq r \leq 2, \: 0 \leq \theta \leq \pi \Big\}
$$

The change of variables formula for plane polar coordinates states that

$$
\iint\limits_{D} f(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ f(r \cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

Finally, we can calculate our double integral, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)(3x^{2} + y^{2})dA &= ∬_(Δ)3(r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ)rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}∫_{0}^{2}3r^{3}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ)drdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}∫_{0}^{2}3r^{3}drdθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}[\frac{3r^{4}}{4}]_{0}^{2}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}12dθ \\
&= 12[θ]_{0}^{π} \\
&= 12π
\end{aligned}
$$
