## Introduction

Let $\mathbf F$ be a vector field on $\mathbb R^2$ and $C$ be a *closed* curve in the $xy$-plane. Imagine that $\mathbf F$ represents the velocity field of a stream of flowing water, and $C$ is a curve that allows water to flow through it (like a fishing net).

The **flux of $\mathbf F$ across $\boldsymbol C$** is a scalar number that tells us how much water is escaping the region bounded by $C$.

We have three possible scenarios:

- If more water flows out through $C$ than in through $C$ (i.e., there is a net flow *out* through $C$), the flux is *positive*.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/df9e471bda3b6683a0dd149777d5e3f5.png>)
- If more water flows in through $C$ than out through $C$ (i.e., there is a net flow *in* through $C$), the flux is *negative*.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/e2d5922f878b632f2c86fea0a17b5c2a.png>)
- If the net flow through $C$ is zero, the flux equals zero.The following vector field has zero flux across $C$ because the amount of water flowing in through $C$ equals the amount of water flowing out through $C$.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/5ee9cd595acfac4ed2ff55d6f5e31338.png>)
The following two vector fields also have a zero flux across $C$ because the water flow is tangential to $C$ at every point along $C$.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/c42545d02cf09e50de1127972d6f188a.png>)

## Constructing an Integral Formula for the Flux

If $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ is a vector field on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by the line integral
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$. The symbol $\oint\limits$ means integration over the closed curve.

It's helpful to spend some time understanding where this definition comes from, so let's derive it using the closed curve $C$ below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/a83474f589135371e9d8b4711918297a.png>)

First, we pick a point $P$ on $C$. At this point, we draw the vector field $\mathbf F$ and the outward-pointing normal vector $\mathbf n$ to $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/e4437c120c2740c21a646ce4e06783c4.png>)

We wish to calculate the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$ at $P$. Now, $\mathbf F$ is made up of two components:

- a component parallel to $\mathbf n$, which contributes to the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$, and
- a component perpendicular to $\mathbf n$, which does *not* contribute to the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$.

We're only interested in the contribution made to the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$. This is given by the scalar projection of $\mathbf F$ in the direction of $\mathbf n$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/d8b7e55d4219a0de8784d477d59064be.png>)

Therefore, the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$ at $P$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
comp_{n}F &= \frac{F \cdot n}{\mid \mid n \mid \mid} = F \cdot n
\end{aligned}
$$

The expression above represents the flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$ at a single point only. Therefore, to calculate the total net flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$, we sum (integrate) this expression over the entire curve. This gives

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s
$$

<a id="orientation-independence"></a>
## Orientation-Independence

Consider the circle $S$ and vector field $\mathbf F$ below. Let $C$ be any path where $S$ is traversed once in the counterclockwise direction.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/a9b713f8649ae4eff3e9be07134f95ef.png>)

By drawing an outward-pointing unit normal vector, it's clear that there is a net flow *out* through $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/2be67e4389e0490310434cceafa60bb9.png>)

Therefore, the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is positive, and we have
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s > 0$.

When measuring flux through a closed curve, the curve's orientation does not matter because the outward-pointing unit normal vector is the same in either case.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/bd79bd973374cd19b8b0badf8e6444c0.png>)

So, the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $-C$ is also positive, and we have
$\oint\limits_{-C} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s > 0$.

## Determining a Flux's Sign From a Diagram

**Example:** Consider the circle $S$ and vector field $\mathbf F$ shown below. Let $C$ be any path where $S$ is traversed once in the counterclockwise direction.

Which of the following statements are true?

1. The flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is negative
2. The flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is zero
3. The flux of $\mathbf F$ across $-C$ is positive

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/0fc4f6ac431f4a118406f367a2049f25.png>)

**Explanation**

If $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$.

To understand the flux intuitively, we imagine that $\mathbf F$ represents a stream of flowing water. The flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ represents the net flow of water escaping the region bounded by $C\mathbin$:

- If more water flows out through $C$ than in through $C$ (i.e., there is a net flow *out* through $C$), the flux is *positive*.
- If more water flows in through $C$ than out through $C$ (i.e., there is a net flow *in* through $C$), the flux is *negative*.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/ceedec3a772973eda45dc8e75ea8a1b4.png>)

- If the net flow through $C$ is zero, the flux equals zero.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/7d3a45be0fad34026a756e0685ef72ed.png>)

The outward-pointing unit normal vector tells us which direction is "out."

With that in mind, let's check each statement.

- Statement I is false, while statement II is true. From the diagram below we see that $\mathbf F$ acts perpendicular to $\mathbf n$ at every point on $C$. Therefore, there is no net flow of $\mathbf F$ through $C$.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/f3f7b1aa12b9af600ca4107f8ba7963e.png>)
So, the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is zero, and we must have
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = 0$.
- Statement III is false. When measuring flux through a closed curve, the orientation of the curve does not matter because the outward-pointing unit normal vector is the same at each point on $C$ in either case.![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/f7f6796435787eeb8416a755b211bebc.png>)
So, the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $-C$ is zero, and we must have
$\oint\limits_{-C} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = 0$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

<a id="calculating-flux"></a>
## Calculating Flux

To calculate the flux of a vector field over a smooth, closed curve, we can use the formula

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s
$$

If

$$
C=C_1\cup C_2 \cup \cdots \cup C_n
$$

is piecewise smooth, we have to calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ over each smooth face. That is,

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s + \cdots + \int\limits_{C_n} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s
$$

where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$ on $C_i$.

As an example, let's consider the vector field

$$
\mathbf F(x,y) = xy\,\mathbf i + (y-x)^3\,\mathbf j
$$

and the rectangle $C$ shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/2db08b48b84e3bc59b026af27ab76169.png>)

To calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ over $C$, we sum the fluxes of $\mathbf F$ over each side:

$$
\oint\limits_{C} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s
$$

To demonstrate how to compute these integrals, let's construct the flux integral across the side $C_4$, given by

$$
\int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s
$$

The side $C_4$ and its outward-pointing unit normal are shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/fb2736a701d260b239001b108e3d6c40.png>)

Since this side is perpendicular to the $y$-axis and we require an outward-pointing unit normal vector, we must have

$$
\mathbf n = -\mathbf j
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C_{4})F \cdot nds &= ∫_(C_{4})(xyi + (y - x)^{3}j) \cdot (-j)ds \\
&= ∫_(C_{4})(xyi) \cdot (-j) + ((y - x)^{3}j)) \cdot (-j)ds \\
&= ∫_(C_{4})0 - (y - x)^{3}ds \\
&= ∫_(C_{4})(x - y)^{3}ds
\end{aligned}
$$

Following a similar procedure for the other sides, we can construct the full expression for the flux of $\mathbf F$ over $C$.

## Finding an Integral Expression for Flux

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F(x,y) = 8y\,\mathbf i + 4x\,\mathbf j$ and the circle $C$ given by $x^2+y^2 = 4$. If the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by

$AAAAAAA$

what is the missing expression?

*Hint: The outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$ is $\mathbf n = \dfrac x2\,\mathbf i + \dfrac y2\,\mathbf j$.*

**Explanation**

If $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/abb3c6599e722f86db8e3d48613846f1.png>)

In our case, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∮_(C)F \cdot nds &= ∮_(C)(8yi + 4xj) \cdot (\frac{x}{2}i + \frac{y}{2}j)ds \\
&= ∮_(C)4xy + 2xyds \\
&= ∮_(C)6xyds
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the missing expression is $6xy$.

<a id="using-symmetry-to-simplify-flux-calculations"></a>
## Using  Symmetry to Simplify Flux Calculations

Calculating the flux of a vector field across a rectangle or square requires computing four flux integrals. However, we can often simplify our calculations if the vector field displays symmetry.

To demonstrate, consider the vector field

$$
\mathbf F = -5\,\mathbf i
$$

and a square $C$ with side length $6$ centered at $O$, as shown below. Let's calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/50f4143a5147ced8cb410522d8e8db72.png>)

If $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$. In this case, we have

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_4 \: \textrm{d}s
$$

where the sides $C_i$ and unit vectors $\mathbf n_i$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/49c0ebfbc846d6ba7e683c10f0b0fc6d.png>)

Since the vector field

$$
\mathbf F = -5\,\mathbf i
$$

is parallel to $C_2$ and $C_4$, there is zero net flow through these two sides. Therefore,

$$
\int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_4 \: \textrm{d}s = 0
$$

This is easy to verify, since,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C_{2})F \cdot n_{2}ds &= ∫_(C_{2}) - 5i \cdot jds \\
&= ∫_(C_{2})0ds \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

and similarly for the integral along $C_4$.

So, our expression for the flux reduces to

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s
$$

Since the vector field is uniform, the net inflow through $C_1$ will cancel with the net outflow through $C_3$. That is

$$
\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s = -\int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s +\int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s =0
$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = 0
$$

<a id="calculating-flux-across-a-square-using-symmetry"></a>
## Calculating Flux Across a Square Using Symmetry

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F = 2x\,\mathbf i + 2y\,\mathbf j$ and a square $C$ with side length $2$ centered at $O$, as shown below. Calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/4ba14e820f544a5113eaea401b1ac37f.png>)

*Hint: You may use the fact that, due to symmetry, the flux is equal across all four sides of the square.*

**Explanation**

If $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$. In this case, we have

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_4 \: \textrm{d}s
$$

where the sides $C_i$ and unit vectors $\mathbf n_i$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/16258660947a1a027a68b4d411919586.png>)

First, consider the side $C_1$. We have that

$$
\mathbf n_1 = \mathbf i
$$

and therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{C_{1}}F \cdot n_{1}ds &= ∫_{C_{1}}(2xi + 2yj) \cdot ids \\
&= 2∫_{C_{1}}xds
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a line integral with respect to arc length, which we can evaluate in the usual way. Parameterizing the side $C_1$, we have

$$
\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf i + t\,\mathbf j, \quad \Rightarrow\quad \mathbf r'(t) = \mathbf j, \qquad t\in [-1,1]
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
2∫_{C_{1}}xds &= 2∫_{-1}^{1}1 \cdot \mid \mid r^{′}(t) \mid \mid dt \\
&= 2∫_{-1}^{1}dt \\
&= 2(1 - (-1)) \\
&= 2 \cdot 2 \\
&= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

By symmetry, the flux integrals over the sides $C_2, C_3$, and $C_4$ must be the same. Therefore,

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = 4\cdot 4 = 16
$$

## Calculating Flux Across a Rectangle Using Two Symmetry Properties

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F = 5x\,\mathbf i - 3y\,\mathbf j$ and a rectangle $C$ with base $4$ and height $2$ centered at $O$, as shown above. Calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/14fbee42939abe16d9fd3f09b62b7242.png>)

*Hint: You may use the fact that, due to symmetry, the flux is equal across the vertical sides, and similarly for the horizontal sides.*

**Explanation**

If $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $C$ is a piecewise-smooth, simple, closed curve, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C$ is given by
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s$,
where $\mathbf{n}$ denotes the outward-pointing unit normal vector to $C$. In this case, we have

$$
\oint\limits_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n} \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s + \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_4 \: \textrm{d}s
$$

where the sides $C_i$ and unit vectors $\mathbf n_i$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.3. Flux in Two-Dimensional Vector Fields/Images/8550241bbdc42d8ce5e3d1b6f1658d69.png>)

We compute this integral in two stages.

- First, consider the side $C_1$. We have that $\mathbf n_1 = \mathbf i$, and therefore,
$∫_{C_{1}}F \cdot n_{1}ds|= ∫_{C_{1}}(5xi - 3yj) \cdot ids; = 5∫_{C_{1}}xds$.
This is a line integral with respect to arc length, which we can evaluate in the usual way. Parameterizing the side $C_1$, we have
$\mathbf r(t) = 2\mathbf i + t\,\mathbf j, \quad \Rightarrow\quad \mathbf r'(t) = \mathbf j, \qquad t\in [-1,1]$.
Therefore,
$5∫_{C_{1}}xds|= 5∫_{-1}^{1}2 \cdot \mid \mid r^{′}(t) \mid \mid dt; = 10∫_{-1}^{1}dt; = 10 \cdot 2; = 20$.
By symmetry, the flux integral across the side $C_3$ must be the same. Therefore,
$\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_3} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_3 \: \textrm{d}s = 20$.

- Next, we consider the side $C_2$. We have that $\mathbf n_2 = \mathbf j$, and therefore,
$∫_{C_{2}}F \cdot n_{2}ds|= ∫_{C_{2}}(5xi - 3yj) \cdot jds; =-3∫_{C_{2}}yds$.
This is a line integral with respect to arc length, which we can evaluate in the usual way. Parameterizing the side $C_2$, we have
$\mathbf r(t) = t\,\mathbf i + \mathbf j, \quad \Rightarrow\quad \mathbf r'(t) = \mathbf i, \qquad t\in [-2,2]$.
Therefore,
$-3∫_{C_{2}}yds|=-3∫_{-2}^{2}1 \cdot \mid \mid r^{′}(t) \mid \mid dt; =-3∫_{-2}^{2}dt; = (-3) \cdot 4; =-12$.
By symmetry, the flux integral across the side $C_4$ must be the same. Therefore,
$\int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \: \textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_4} \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf{n}_4 \: \textrm{d}s = -12$.

Finally, then

$$
\oint\limits_{C}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,\textrm d s= 2 \cdot 20 + 2 \cdot (-12) = 16
$$
