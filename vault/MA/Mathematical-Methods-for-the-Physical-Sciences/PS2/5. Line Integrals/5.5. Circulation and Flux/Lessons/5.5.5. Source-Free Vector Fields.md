## Introduction

Consider the vector field $\mathbf F$, given by

$$
\mathbf F(x,y)= P(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + Q(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}
$$

which is defined on an open set $D\subseteq \mathbb R^2$. Recall that a function $\psi(x,y)$ is said to be a **stream function** of $\mathbf F$ if it satisfies

$$
P = \dfrac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad Q = -\dfrac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}
$$

If a vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ has a stream function $\psi(x,y)$, then $\mathbf{F}$ is called a **source-free vector field**.

Source-free vector fields have some interesting properties, summarized below:

- *The divergence of $\mathbf{F}$ at any point of $D$ is zero:*
$\textrm{div}\,\mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf F = \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y} = 0$
- *The*flux*of $\mathbf{F}$ along any closed curve $C \in D$ is zero:*
$\oint\limits_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = 0$
- *Flux is path-independent. That is, given any two curves $C_1, C_2 \in D$ with the same start and endpoints, we have that*
$\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s$.

Stream functions for source-free vector fields play a similar role to potential functions for conservative vector fields. So it's worth comparing them side-by-side.

## A Worked Example

Let's use what we know about source-free vector fields to calculate the flux of

$$
\mathbf{F}(x,y)=2y\,\mathbf{i}-2x\,\mathbf{j}
$$

across the curve $C$, shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Source-Free Vector Fields/Images/201ea211d60d0c1ad5562d6680badb3e.png>)

First, notice that this is a source-free vector field, since

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{∂P}{∂x} + \frac{∂Q}{∂y} &= \frac{∂}{∂x}(2y) + \frac{∂}{∂y}(-2x) \\
&= 0 + 0 \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, since $\mathbf{F}$ is source-free, the flux of $\mathbf{F}$ across any closed curve is zero. Hence,

$$
\oint\limits_{C} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = 0
$$

## Identifying True Statements Regarding Source-Free Vector Fields

**Example:** Given the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = 4y(x^2+y^2)\,\mathbf{i} - 4x(x^2+y^2)\,\mathbf{j}$, which of the following statements must be true?

1. $\nabla \cdot \mathbf F= 0$
2. $\mathbf{F}$ is a source-free vector field
3. For two arbitrary curves $C_1$ and $C_2$, we have $\displaystyle\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s \neq \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s$.

**Explanation**

If

$$
\mathbf{F} = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}
$$

is a vector field with simply connected domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, then the following are equivalent:

- $\mathbf{F}$ is a source-free vector field.
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf F = \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y} = 0$.
- The flux $\displaystyle{\oint\limits_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s}$ along any closed curve $C \in D$ is zero.
- Flux is path-independent. That is, given any two curves $C_1,C_2 \in D$ with the same start and endpoints, we have that
$\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s$.
- There exists a stream function $\psi(x,y)$ for $\mathbf{F}$.

Note that $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ is a vector field with the simply connected domain

$$
D=\mathbb{R}^2
$$

where

$$
P(x,y) = 4y(x^2+y^2),\qquad Q(x,y) =-4x(x^2+y^2)
$$

Computing the partial derivatives, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{∂P}{∂x} = 8xy, \frac{∂Q}{∂y} &= -8xy
\end{aligned}
$$

With that in mind, let's consider each statement.

- Statement I is true. Indeed, we have that
$\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y} = 8xy + (-8xy) = 0$.
- Statement II is true. This follows from statement I.
- Statement III is false. Since $\mathbf F$ is source-free, we have that $\displaystyle\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s$ for all curves *with the same start and endpoints*.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

## Calculating Flux in a Source-Free Vector Field

**Example:** The curve $C_1$ has endpoints $A(5,3)$ and $B(1,3)$. Calculate the flux of the vector field
$\mathbf{F}(x,y)=\sqrt{y^3}\,\mathbf{i}+\sqrt{(5-x)^3}\,\mathbf{j}$
through $C_1$ measured with respect to the unit normal vector $\mathbf{n}_1$, as shown above.

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Source-Free Vector Fields/Images/7a7b566a9bc20a63de1cb5e29e9a5c30.png>)

*Hint: You're given that $\mathbf F$ is source-free.*

**Explanation**

If

$$
\mathbf{F} = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}
$$

is a vector field with simply connected domain $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, then the following are equivalent:

- $\mathbf{F}$ is a source-free vector field.
- $\nabla \cdot \mathbf F = \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y} = 0$.
- The flux $\displaystyle{\oint\limits_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s}$ along any closed curve $C \in D$ is zero.
- Flux is path-independent. That is, given any two curves $C_1, C_2 \in D$ with the same start and endpoints, we have that
$\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}s$.
- There exists a stream function $\psi(x,y)$ for $\mathbf{F}$.

First, notice that

$$
\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\sqrt{y^3}\right) + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\sqrt{(5-x)^3}\right) = 0+0 = 0
$$

which shows that $\mathbf{F}$ is a source-free vector field.

Since $\mathbf{F}$ is source-free, we know the flux of $\mathbf{F}$ is independent of the path. In particular,

$$
\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}_1 \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \,\textrm{d}s
$$

where $C_2$ is the straight line from $A(5,3)$ to $B(1,3)$ and $\mathbf{n}_2$ is its unit normal, shown in the diagram below.

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Source-Free Vector Fields/Images/d67fb9c6a43c0d5100ffe8818b747f5c.png>)

Notice that both $\mathbf n_1$ and $\mathbf n_2$ are $90^\circ$ clockwise rotations of their respective unit tangent vectors.

To calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $C_2$, we will use the formula

$$
\int\limits_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}_2 \,\textrm{d}s = \int\limits_{C_2} P\,\textrm{d}y - Q\,\textrm{d}x
$$

To apply the formula,

- the curve should be parametrized as $\mathbf{r}(t)$ for $t \in [a,b]$, and
- the unit normal vector $\mathbf{n}_2$ *must* be a $90^\circ$ clockwise rotation of the unit normal tangent vector $\mathbf{T}(t)$.

Parametrizing $C_2$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
r(t) &= 5i + 3j + t \cdot [(i + 3j) - (5i + 3j)] \\
&= (5 - 4t)i + 3j
\end{aligned}
$$

where $t\in [0,1]$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(x, y) &= \sqrt{y^{3}} = \sqrt{(3)^{3}} = 3\sqrt{3}, \\
Q(x, y) &= \sqrt{(5 - x)^{3}} = \sqrt{(5 - (5 - 4t))^{3}} = 8\sqrt{t^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Computing the derivatives of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ along the curve, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(5 - 4t) =-4, \\
\frac{dy}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(3) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

We evaluate the flux integral as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_(C_{2})F \cdot n_{2}ds &= ∫_(C_{2})Pdy - Qdx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(P \cdot \frac{dy}{dt} - Q \cdot \frac{dx}{dt})dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(3\sqrt{3}) \cdot (0) - (8\sqrt{t^{3}}) \cdot (-4)dt \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}32t^{3/2}dt \\
&= [\frac{64t^{5/2}}{5}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= ((64(1)^{5/2})/(5)) - (((64(0)^{5/2})/(5)) \\
&= \frac{64}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we conclude that

$$
\int\limits_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}_1\, \textrm{d}s = \dfrac{64}{5}
$$
