<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction

In this lesson, we will represent a $2\pi$-periodic function $f(x)$ as an infinite sum of simple sine and cosine waves. This representation is called the **Fourier series** for $f(x)$:

$$
f(x)\sim \frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\big)
$$

The key idea is that we can "build" a complicated periodic graph by adding together basic waves. For example, below we have a discontinuous periodic function approximated by the first few terms of its Fourier series.

![](<../Source/10.2.1. Introduction to Fourier Series/Images/dd8d954f57a40344107408873f488516.png>)

This gives us a powerful way to analyze periodic behavior by breaking it down into simple oscillations. Fourier series play a central role in modeling vibrations, signals, and other repeating patterns.

To work with Fourier series, we typically start with a function defined on a single base interval, such as $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi),]$ and then extend it to be periodic over all real numbers. This process creates what is called a **periodic extension**.

In the next slide, we will define this concept formally and look at some examples.

<a id="periodic-extensions"></a>
## Periodic Extensions

Recall that a function $f(x)$ is *$p$-periodic* if

$$
f(x+p)=f(x)
$$

for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

Now, if $F(x)$ is defined on an interval $[MATH: x \in [a,b)]$ of length $p$, then its **$p$-periodic extension** $f$ is defined by the two properties:

- $f(x+p)=f(x)$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$
- $f(x)=F(x)$ for all $[MATH: x \in [a,b).]$

For example, let $F(x) = -x$ be defined on $[MATH: x\in [0,2).]$ Now, suppose $f(x)$ is the $2$-periodic extension of $F(x)$ from $[MATH: x\in [0,2)]$ to $x \in \mathbb{R}$. The graphs of $y=F(x)$ and $y=f(x)$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/10.2.1. Introduction to Fourier Series/Images/9fb8a259022c1450df8b28f36b9bc6c5.png>)

**Watch out!** We should always be aware of the difference between $F(x)$ and its extension $f(x)$. For instance, consider the function

$$
F(x)=2\sin(x)
$$

defined on $[MATH: x \in [0,\pi)]$ and its $\pi$-periodic extension $f(x)$. The graphs of $y=F(x)$ and $y=f(x)$ are shown below.

![](<../Source/10.2.1. Introduction to Fourier Series/Images/dca63a920b41ca81d4fbb2063e7b9efb.png>)

Notice that $f(x)$ is not just $2\sin(x)$! But it would coincide with $2\sin(x)$ if we were to consider the $2\pi$-periodic extension of

$$
F(x)=2\sin(x)
$$

from, say, $x \in [0,2\pi]$ to $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

<a id="understanding-the-periodic-extension-of-a-function"></a>
## Understanding the Periodic Extension of a Function

**Example:** Let $F(x) = \cos(2x)$ for $x\in\mathbb R$. Suppose $f(x)$ is the $\pi$-periodic extension of $F(x)$ from $[MATH: x \in [0,\pi)]$ to $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Which of the following statements are true?

1. $f(x+\pi) = f(x)$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$
2. $f(x) = F(x)$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$
3. $f\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = f(x)$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$

**Explanation**

Since $f$ is the $\pi$-periodic extension of $F$, we have

- $f(x+\pi)=f(x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$, and
- $f(x)=F(x)$ for all $[MATH: x\in[0,\pi).]$

With that in mind, we examine each statement in turn.

- Statement I is true. This is exactly the definition of $\pi$-periodicity:
$f(x+\pi)=f(x)$
for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.
- Statement II is true. Notice that $F$ itself is $\pi$-periodic, because for any $x\in\mathbb{R}$, we have
$F(x + π) = \cos (2(x + π)) = \cos (2x + 2π) = \cos (2x) = F(x)$.
Since $F$ already satisfies the periodicity rule, the $\pi$-periodic extension agrees with $F$ everywhere, so $f(x)=F(x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.
- Statement III is false. For example, take $x=0$. Then,

$$
f(x + \frac{π}{2}) = f(\frac{π}{2}) = F(\frac{π}{2}) = \cos (π) =-1
$$

while

$$
f(x) = f(0) = F(0)\cos (0) = 1
$$

Hence,

$$
f\left(x+\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \neq f(x)
$$

for $x=0$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only".

<a id="reminder-orthogonal-functions"></a>
## Reminder: Orthogonal Functions

Recall that we can define an inner product on functions over $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ given by

$$
\langle f(x), g(x)\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

Also recall that functions $f$ and $g$ are called *orthogonal* (with respect to this inner product) if

$$
\langle f(x), g(x)\rangle=0
$$

The key fact we will use throughout this lesson is that the functions
$1,\ \cos(x),\ \sin(x),\ \cos(2x),\ \sin(2x),\ \ldots$
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product defined above.

More precisely, for integers

$$
m,n\ge 1
$$

we have the following orthogonality relations on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$:

- $\langle \cos(mx),\cos(nx)\rangle=0$ if $m\ne n$,
- $\langle \sin(mx),\sin(nx)\rangle=0$ if $m\ne n$, and
- $\langle \sin(mx),\cos(nx)\rangle=0$ for all $m,n$.

Also, the constant function $1$ is orthogonal to every sine and cosine:

- $\langle 1,\cos(nx)\rangle=0$, and
- $\langle 1,\sin(nx)\rangle=0$ for all $n\ge 1$.

Finally, we will also use the squared norms of these functions:

$$
\langle 1,1\rangle=2\pi, \quad \langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle=\langle \sin(nx),\sin(nx)\rangle=\pi \quad \text{for all} n\ge 1
$$

Let's see how this works in concrete examples.

**Note:** Both intervals $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ and $[MATH: [0,2\pi)]$ are considered standard in this context. We’ll use $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ in tutorials, but some exercises use $[MATH: [0,2\pi).]$

<a id="checking-orthogonality-of-sines-and-cosines"></a>
## Checking Orthogonality of Sines and Cosines

**Example:** $\langle f(x),g(x) \rangle = \displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi} f(x) g(x) \, \textrm{d}x$
Given the inner product on the set of continuous functions over $[MATH: x\in [0,2\pi)]$ defined above, compute $\displaystyle \langle \sin(6x), \cos(4x) \rangle$ and determine if functions $f(x)=\sin(6x)$ and $g(x)=\cos(4x)$ are orthogonal with respect to the inner product.

**Explanation**

We begin by using the product-to-sum identity

$$
\sin(6x)\cos(4x) =\dfrac{1}{2}\big(\sin(10x)+\sin(2x)\big)
$$

Substituting this into the integral gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
⟨\sin (6x), \cos (4x)⟩ &= \int_{0}^{2π}\sin (6x)\cos (4x)dx \\
&= \int_{0}^{2π}\frac{1}{2}\sin (10x) + \frac{1}{2}\sin (2x)dx \\
&= [- \frac{1}{20}\cos (10x) - \frac{1}{4}\cos (2x)]_{0}^{2π} \\
&=-\frac{1}{20}(\cos (20π) - \cos (0)) - \frac{1}{4}(\cos (4π) - \cos (0)) \\
&=-\frac{1}{20}(1 - 1) - \frac{1}{4}(1 - 1) \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
f(x)=\sin(6x)
$$

and

$$
g(x)=\cos(4x)
$$

$\text{are orthogonal}$ with respect to the inner product.

<a id="fourier-series-form-for-2pi-periodic-functions"></a>
## Fourier Series Form For 2pi-Periodic Functions

Recall that we are considering a $2\pi$-periodic function $f(x)$ that is piecewise continuous on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi).]$ We can represent $f(x)$ by its Fourier series:

$$
f(x)\sim \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\big)
$$

Our goal is to find the formulas for the coefficients $a_0, a_n$, and $b_n$.

To do this, we'll use a powerful idea from linear algebra. We define an *inner product* for functions on the interval $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$ as

$$
\langle f(x), g(x)\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

With this inner product, the set of functions $\{1, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ for

$$
n\ge 1
$$

is an *orthogonal set*.

This means we can think of the Fourier coefficients as *projections* of the function $f(x)$ onto each of these orthogonal "directions". The coefficients $a_0,a_n,b_n$ simply tell us "how much" of $f(x)$ lies in each of these directions.

On the next slide, we will use this idea to derive the formula for the first coefficient, $a_0$.

---

<a id="the-formula-for-the-constant-term"></a>
## The Formula For the Constant Term

Let's derive the formula for the coefficient $a_0$ by taking the inner product of the entire Fourier series

$$
f(x)\sim \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\big)
$$

with the constant function $1$.

- **Step 1.** Start with the Fourier series and take the inner product of both sides with $1{:}$
$\langle f(x),1\rangle = \left\langle \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\big),\,1\right\rangle$
- **Step 2.** Use the linearity of the inner product to distribute it across the sum:
$\langle f(x),1\rangle = \dfrac{a_0}{2}\langle 1,1\rangle +\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n\langle \cos(nx),1\rangle +\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n\langle \sin(nx),1\rangle$
- **Step 3.** Apply the orthogonality property. For any $n\ge 1$, we have $\langle \cos(nx),1\rangle=0$ and $\langle \sin(nx),1\rangle=0$. This makes all the terms in the sums equal to zero:
$\langle f(x),1\rangle=\dfrac{a_0}{2}\langle 1,1\rangle$
- **Step 4.** Solve for $a_0$. We just need to calculate $\langle 1,1\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1\cdot 1\,\textrm{d}x=2\pi$.
$a_0=\dfrac{2\langle f(x),1\rangle}{\langle 1,1\rangle} =\dfrac{2\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cdot 1\,\textrm{d}x}{2\pi} =\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\,\textrm{d}x$

We find the formulas for $a_n$ and $b_n$ (for

$$
n\ge 1
$$

) in a similar way.

- To find $a_n$, we take the inner product of the Fourier series with $\cos(nx)$. By orthogonality, the only term that is not zero on the right-hand side is the one involving $\langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle$.
$\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle = a_n \langle \cos(nx), \cos(nx)\rangle$
Since $\langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle = \displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^2(nx)\,\textrm{d}x = \pi$, we can solve for $a_n{:}$
$a_n = \dfrac{\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle}{\langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle} =\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,\textrm{d}x$
- Similarly, to find $b_n$, we take the inner product with $\sin(nx){:}$
$b_n = \dfrac{\langle f(x),\sin(nx)\rangle}{\langle \sin(nx),\sin(nx)\rangle} =\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,\textrm{d}x$

**Note:** If $f$ is the $2\pi$-periodic extension of a function $F$ defined on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi),]$ then we can replace $f(x)$ with $F(x)$ inside the integrals.

Now that we have the formulas for all the coefficients, let's work through a concrete example.

<a id="determining-expressions-for-fourier-coefficients"></a>
## Determining Expressions For Fourier Coefficients

**Example:** Let $F(x) = 2x^2+5$ for $x\in\mathbb R$, and let $f(x)$ be the $2\pi$-periodic extension of $F$ from $[MATH: x\in [0,2\pi)]$ to $x\in \mathbb R$. The Fourier series of $f$ is given by
$f(x) \sim \dfrac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)\right)$.
Determine the integral expression for the coefficient $a_4$.

**Explanation**

The usual inner product of functions $f$ and $g$ over $[MATH: x\in [0,2\pi)]$ is given by

$$
\langle f(x),g(x) \rangle = \int_{0}^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

For a $2\pi$-periodic function $f(x)$ that is piecewise continuous over $[MATH: x\in [0,2\pi),]$ the Fourier series of $f$ is given by

$$
f(x) \sim \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\right)
$$

where $a_0$, $a_n$, and $b_n$ are constants to be determined.

Now, we find the inner product of $f(x)$ with $\cos(4x){:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
⟨f(x), \cos (4x)⟩ &=  \\
⟨\frac{a_{0}}{2} + ∑_(n = 1)^(∞)(a_{n}\cos (nx) + b_{n}\sin (nx)), \cos (4x)⟩ &=  \\
⟨\frac{a_{0}}{2}, \cos (4x)⟩ + ∑_(n = 1)^(∞)⟨a_{n}\cos (nx), \cos (4x)⟩ + ∑_(n = 1)^(∞)⟨b_{n}\sin (nx), \cos (4x)⟩ &=  \\
&= \frac{a_{0}}{2}⟨1, \cos (4x)⟩ + ∑_(n = 1)^(∞)a_{n}⟨\cos (nx), \cos (4x)⟩ + ∑_(n = 1)^(∞)b_{n}⟨\sin (nx), \cos (4x)⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the functions
$1, \sin(x), \cos(x), \sin(2x), \cos(2x), \ldots$
are orthogonal with respect to the defined inner product, the only nonzero term in the final expression above is

$$
a_4 \left\langle \cos(4x), \cos(4x) \right\rangle
$$

Computing the inner product, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
⟨\cos (4x), \cos (4x)⟩ &= \int_{0}^{2π}\cos^{2} (4x)dx \\
&= \int_{0}^{2π}\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cos (8x)dx \\
&= [\frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{16}\sin (8x)]_{0}^{2π} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(2π - 0) - \frac{1}{16}(\sin (16π) - \sin (0)) \\
&= π
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we obtain that

$$
\begin{aligned}
⟨f(x), \cos (4x)⟩ &= a_{4}⟨\cos (4x), \cos (4x)⟩ \\
a_{4} &= (⟨f(x), \cos (4x)⟩)/(⟨\cos (4x), \cos (4x)⟩) \\
a_{4} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{0}^{2π}f(x)\cos (4x)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{π} \cdot \int_{0}^{2π}(2x^{2} + 5)\cos (4x)dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Deriving the Formulas For the Fourier Coefficients

Once again, recall that we are considering a $2\pi$-periodic function $f(x)$ that is piecewise continuous on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi).]$ We represent $f$ by its Fourier series

$$
f(x)\sim \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)\big)
$$

To determine the coefficients, we use the inner product on $[MATH: [-\pi,\pi)]$:

$$
\langle f(x), g(x)\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

We have already seen the derivation for the coefficient $a_0$ early in the lesson.

Now, we derive the formula for $a_n$ for

$$
n\ge 1{:}
$$

- First, we take the inner product of both sides with $\cos(nx){:}$
$\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle = \left\langle \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(a_k\cos(kx)+b_k\sin(kx)\big),\,\cos(nx)\right\rangle$.
- Using linearity, we get
$\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle = \dfrac{a_0}{2}\langle 1,\cos(nx)\rangle \!+\! \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\langle \cos(kx),\cos(nx)\rangle \!+\! \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} b_k\langle \sin(kx),\cos(nx)\rangle$.
- By orthogonality, $\langle 1,\cos(nx)\rangle=0$, $\langle \sin(kx),\cos(nx)\rangle=0$ for all $k$, and $\langle \cos(kx),\cos(nx)\rangle=0$ whenever $k\ne n$, so
$\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle=a_n\langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle$.
Since $\displaystyle \langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle=\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos^2(nx)\,\textrm{d}x=\pi$, we obtain
$a_n=\dfrac{\langle f(x),\cos(nx)\rangle}{\langle \cos(nx),\cos(nx)\rangle} =\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,\textrm{d}x$.

Finally, we derive the formula for $b_n$ for

$$
n\ge 1{:}
$$

- First, we take the inner product of both sides with $\sin(nx){:}$
$\langle f(x),\sin(nx)\rangle = \left\langle \dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(a_k\cos(kx)+b_k\sin(kx)\big),\,\sin(nx)\right\rangle$.
- Using linearity, we get
$\langle f(x),\sin(nx)\rangle = \dfrac{a_0}{2}\langle 1,\sin(nx)\rangle \!+\! \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\langle \cos(kx),\sin(nx)\rangle \!+\! \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} b_k\langle \sin(kx),\sin(nx)\rangle$.
- By orthogonality, $\langle 1,\sin(nx)\rangle=0$, $\langle \cos(kx),\sin(nx)\rangle=0$ for all $k$, and $\langle \sin(kx),\sin(nx)\rangle=0$ whenever $k\ne n$, so
$\langle f(x),\sin(nx)\rangle=b_n\langle \sin(nx),\sin(nx)\rangle$.
Since $\displaystyle \langle \sin(nx),\sin(nx)\rangle=\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin^2(nx)\,\textrm{d}x=\pi$, we obtain
$b_n=\dfrac{\langle f(x),\sin(nx)\rangle}{\langle \sin(nx),\sin(nx)\rangle} =\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,\textrm{d}x$.

Putting these together, the Fourier coefficients are the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{0} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{- π}^{π}f(x)dx \\
a_{n} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{- π}^{π}f(x)\cos (nx)dx, n \ge 1 \\
b_{n} &= \frac{1}{π}\int_{- π}^{π}f(x)\sin (nx)dx, n \ge 1
\end{aligned}
$$
