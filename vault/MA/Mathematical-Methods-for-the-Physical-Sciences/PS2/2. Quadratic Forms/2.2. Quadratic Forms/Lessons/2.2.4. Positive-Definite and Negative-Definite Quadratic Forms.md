## Introduction

In certain applications involving quadratic forms, an important question is whether a particular quadratic form $Q(\mathbf x)$ is *always* positive or *always* negative.

We classify quadratic forms using the following terminology:

- A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is **positive-definite** if $Q(\mathbf{x}) > 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\mathbf{0} \}$.For example, the quadratic form
$S(\mathbf{x})=8x_1^2+3x_2^2$
is positive-definite since
$8x_1^2+3x_2^2 > 0$
for all nonzero $\mathbf{x}$.![](<../Source/2.2.4. Positive-Definite and Negative-Definite Quadratic Forms/Images/228f34de35a9f860678141504baca3f8.png>)
- A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is **negative-definite** if $Q(\mathbf{x}) < 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\mathbf{0} \}$.For example, the quadratic form
$T(\mathbf{x})=-x_1^2-3x_2^2$
is negative-definite since
$-x_1^2-3x_2^2 \lt 0$
for all nonzero $\mathbf{x}$.![](<../Source/2.2.4. Positive-Definite and Negative-Definite Quadratic Forms/Images/7a28ff4262ae69647a1720c5da5fbba3.png>)
- A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is **indefinite** if $Q(\mathbf{x}_1) > 0$ for some $\mathbf{x}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and$Q(\mathbf{x}_2) < 0$ for some $\mathbf{x}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^n$.For example, the form
$R(\mathbf{x})=x_1^2+2x_2^2-6x_1x_2$
is indefinite. Substituting $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$ we get
$R(x_{1})|= 1^{2} + 2 \cdot 0^{2} - 6 \cdot 1 \cdot 0; = 1 + 0 - 0; = 1 > 0$,
while substituting $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ we obtain
$R(x_{2})|= 1^{2} + 2 \cdot 1^{2} - 6 \cdot 1 \cdot 1; = 1 + 2 - 6; =-3 < 0$.

<a id="identifying-possible-quadratic-form-types-using-substitution"></a>
## Identifying Possible Quadratic Form Types Using Substitution

**Example:** Consider the vector $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}$ By evaluating the following quadratic forms at $\mathbf{x}$ **only,** determine which of them **cannot** be negative-definite.

1. $R(\mathbf{x})=x_1^2 - 3x_2^2 + 5x_1x_2$
2. $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$
3. $T(\mathbf{x})=x_2^2 + 8x_1x_2$

**Explanation**

A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is negative-definite if $Q(\mathbf{x}) \lt 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\mathbf{0} \}$.

With that in mind, let's examine each of the quadratic forms by substituting $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}$

- Quadratic form I *might* be negative-definite. Indeed,
$R(x)|= 2^{2} - 3 \cdot 7^{2} + 5 \cdot 2 \cdot 7; = 4 - 147 + 70; =-73 < 0$.
- Quadratic form II *cannot* be negative-definite since
$S(x)|= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 7\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}6 & 14\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}; = 110 > 0$.
- Quadratic form III *cannot* be negative-definite since
$T(x)|= 7^{2} + 8 \cdot 2 \cdot 7; = 49 + 112; = 161 > 0$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."

<a id="positive-and-negative-semi-definite-quadratic-forms"></a>
## Positive and Negative Semi-Definite Quadratic Forms

There are two further classifications of quadratic forms that turn out to be useful:

- A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is **positive semi-definite** if $Q(\mathbf{x}) \geq 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.For example, the form $A(\mathbf{x})=x_1^2$ over $\mathbb{R}^2$ is positive semi-definite. Notice that this particular form is not positive-definite since it gives zero on the nonzero vector $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$![](<../Source/2.2.4. Positive-Definite and Negative-Definite Quadratic Forms/Images/33b153067f14b362a417f3395e0d284e.png>)
- A quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x})$ is **negative semi-definite** if $Q(\mathbf{x}) \leq 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. For example, the form $B(\mathbf{x})=-x_1^2$ over $\mathbb{R}^2$ is negative semi-definite. Notice that this particular form is not negative-definite since it gives zero on the nonzero vector $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$![](<../Source/2.2.4. Positive-Definite and Negative-Definite Quadratic Forms/Images/b71719a9cc8309905bf30a86296fba9c.png>)

It's also worth noting that

- any positive-definite quadratic form is positive semi-definite, and
- any negative-definite quadratic form is negative semi-definite.

<a id="using-eigenvalues-to-classify-quadratic-forms"></a>
## Using Eigenvalues to Classify Quadratic Forms

Given a quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf x) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}
$$

with a symmetric matrix $A$, we have the following theorem:

- $Q$ is positive-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are positive,
- $Q$ is positive semi-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are non-negative,
- $Q$ is negative-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are negative,
- $Q$ is negative semi-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are non-positive,
- $Q$ is indefinite if and only if $A$ has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

Using the same criteria, we can also classify the symmetric matrix $A$ as positive-definite, positive semi-definite, etc.

For example, consider the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-5 & -1 \\ -1 & -5\end{bmatrix}
$$

Computing the characteristic equation of $A$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid A - λI \mid &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}-5 - λ & -1 \\ -1 & -5 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(-5 - λ)(-5 - λ) - 1 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} + 10λ + 24 &= 0 \\
(λ + 4)(λ + 6) &= 0 \\
λ &= -4,-6
\end{aligned}
$$

Since all the eigenvalues of $A$ are negative, the quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf x) = \mathbf{x}^T A \, \mathbf{x}
$$

(as well as the matrix $A$) is negative-definite.

<a id="identifying-a-quadratic-form-type-by-calculating-the-corresponding-eigenvalues"></a>
## Identifying a Quadratic Form Type by Calculating the Corresponding Eigenvalues

**Example:** Consider the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}-4 & 5 \\ 5 & -4\end{bmatrix}$ Which of the following statements are true?

1. The eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_1=-1$ and $\lambda_2=9$
2. $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ is indefinite
3. $A$ is positive-definite

**Explanation**

Given a quadratic form

$$
Q(\mathbf x) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}
$$

we have the following theorem:

- $Q$ is positive-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are positive,
- $Q$ is positive semi-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are non-negative (note that positive semi-definite quadratic forms include all positive-definite quadratic forms),
- $Q$ is negative-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are negative,
- $Q$ is negative semi-definite if and only if all the eigenvalues of $A$ are non-positive (note that negative semi-definite quadratic forms include all negative-definite quadratic forms), and
- $Q$ is indefinite if and only if $A$ has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is false. Computing the characteristic equation of $A$, we obtain
$\mid A - λI \mid |= 0;\begin{vmatrix}-4 - λ & 5 \\ 5 & -4 - λ\end{vmatrix}|= 0; (-4 - λ)(-4 - λ) - 25|= 0; λ^{2} + 8λ - 9|= 0; (λ - 1)(λ + 9)|= 0; λ|= 1,-9$
- Statement II is true, while statement III is false. Since $A$ has positive and negative eigenvalues, the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ (as well as the matrix $A$) is indefinite.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

<a id="the-principal-minors-of-a-square-matrix"></a>
## The Principal Minors of a Square Matrix

The **principal minors** of an $n\times n$ matrix are the $n$ determinants formed by the first $r$ rows and first $r$ columns of $A$ for

$$
r = 1,2,3,\ldots, n
$$

The principal minors of $A$ are denoted $\Delta_i$.

Note that:

- the first principal minor $\Delta_1$ equals $a_{11}$, and
- the $n$th principal minor $\Delta_n$ equals $\det(A)$.

For example, consider the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 & -4 \\ 6 & 9 & 8\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since $A$ is a $3\times3$ matrix, it has $3$ principal minors. Let's compute them:

- The first principal minor is the determinant formed by the first row and first column of $A$:
$\mid a_{11} \mid$
- The second principal minor is the determinant formed by the first $2$ rows and first $2$ columns of $A$:
$\begin{vmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3\end{vmatrix}$
- The third principal minor is the determinant formed by the first $3$ rows and first $3$ columns of $A$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix}1 & 0 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 & -4 \\ 6 & 9 & 8\end{vmatrix}
$$

---

<a id="sylvesters-criterion"></a>
## Sylvester's Criterion

Let $\Delta_i$ for

$$
i=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

denote the principal minors of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$.

According to **Sylvester's criterion**, the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ is

- positive-definite if and only if $\Delta_i > 0$ for all $i=1,2,\ldots,n$,
- negative-definite if and only if, for all $i=1,2,\ldots,n$, we have $\Delta_i < 0$ when $i$ is odd, and $\Delta_i > 0$ when $i$ is even.

In particular, if $A$ is negative-definite, then the principal minors alternate in sign, with the *first* principal minor being negative.

For example, consider the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5\end{bmatrix}
$$

Let's compute its principal minors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Δ_{1} &= a_{11} = 2 > 0 \\
Δ_{2} &= \begin{vmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1|= 1 > 0 \\ Δ_{3}|= & A\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 5 \cdot\begin{vmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 5 > 0
\end{aligned}
$$

All principal minors are positive. Therefore, by Sylvester's criterion, the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ is positive-definite.

<a id="identifying-a-quadratic-form-type-using-sylvesters-criterion"></a>
## Identifying a Quadratic Form Type Using Sylvester's Criterion

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}-6 & -5 & 1 \\ 5 & -2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -1\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrix $A$ shown above.
Which of the following statements are true?

1. $a_{11}=-6$ and $\det(A) \lt 0$
2. $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ is negative-definite
3. $A$ is positive-definite

**Explanation**

Let $\Delta_i$ for

$$
i=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

denote the principal minors of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$. Then, according to Sylvester's criterion, the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$ (as well as the matrix $A$) is

- positive-definite if and only if $\Delta_i > 0$ for all $i=1,2,\ldots,n$,
- negative-definite if and only if, for all $i=1,2,\ldots,n$, we have $\Delta_i < 0$ when $i$ is odd, and $\Delta_i > 0$ when $i$ is even.

With that in mind, let's examine each of the statements in turn.

- Statement I is true. Indeed, $a_{11}=-6$. Expanding $\det(A)$ along the top row, we get
$det(A)|=\begin{vmatrix}-6 & -5 & 1 \\ 5 & -2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -1\end{vmatrix}; = (-6)\begin{vmatrix}-2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1\end{vmatrix}- (-5)\begin{vmatrix}5 & 2 \\ 1 & -1\end{vmatrix}+ 1 \cdot\begin{vmatrix}5 & -2 \\ 1 & 2\end{vmatrix}; = (-6)(-2) + (5)(-7) + 12; =-11$.

- Statement II is true, while statement III is false. Notice the following:
$Δ_{1}|= a_{11} =-6 < 0; Δ_{2}|= \mid - 6\begin{vmatrix}-5 \\ 5\end{vmatrix}- 2|= 37 > 0; Δ_{3}|= \mid A|=-11 < 0$
So, by Sylvester's criterion, the quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \, \mathbf{x}$, and the matrix $A$, are negative-definite.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."
