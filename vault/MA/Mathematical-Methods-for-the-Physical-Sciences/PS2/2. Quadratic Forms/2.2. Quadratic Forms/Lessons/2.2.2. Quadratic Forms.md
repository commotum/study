## Introduction

Given a bilinear form $B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$ such that

$$
B:\Bbb R^n \times \Bbb R^n \to \Bbb R
$$

and the corresponding $n \times n$ matrix is *symmetric*, we can define the so-called **quadratic form** by substituting

$$
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}
$$

as follows:

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})= B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
$$

Notice that the new expression depends only on $\mathbf{x}$. So, a quadratic form is a function

$$
Q:\Bbb R^n \to \Bbb R
$$

In matrix notation, a quadratic form can be given as

$$
Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}
$$

where $A$ is a symmetric matrix. It's called the **matrix of the quadratic form**.

**Watch out!** By definition, the matrix of a quadratic form must be *symmetric*.

For example, given the bilinear form

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

the corresponding quadratic form is then

$$
Q(\mathbf{x})=B(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x})
$$

Explicitly, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(x_{1}, x_{2}) &= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} + 2x_{2} \\ 2x_{1} + x_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_{1}^{2} + 4x_{1}x_{2} + x_{2}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note**: In a quadratic form, all terms must be of degree two (considered as polynomials in the variables $x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n$). That's the reason we call it *quadratic*.

## Identifying Matrices of Quadratic Forms

**Example:** Which of the following could be matrices of quadratic forms defined on $\mathbb{R}^2$?

$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 & -2 \\ 1 & 4 & 5 \\ -2 & 5 & 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Recall that every quadratic form defined on $\mathbb{R}^2$ has an associated $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix.

With that in mind, let's examine each matrix in turn.

- $L$ *cannot* be a matrix of a quadratic form defined on $\mathbb{R}^2$ since it's a $3 \times 3$ matrix (not $2 \times 2$).
- $M$ *cannot* be a matrix of a quadratic form defined on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Although it's a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $M$ is not symmetric since $m_{12} \neq m_{21}$.
- $N$ could be a matrix of a quadratic form defined on $\mathbb{R}^2$ since it is a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix.

Therefore, the correct answer is "$N$ only."

<a id="evaluating-a-quadratic-form"></a>
## Evaluating a Quadratic Form

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}$
Given the matrix $A$ above, evaluate the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}$ when $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Substituting $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$ into the expression for $Q(\mathbf{x})$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(x) &= x^{T}Ax \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 1 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & 23\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= 86
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="writing-down-a-quadratic-form-given-its-matrix"></a>
## Writing Down a Quadratic Form Given Its Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$
Consider the matrix $A$ above. Which expression represents the corresponding quadratic form $\mathbf{x}^T \! A \mathbf{x}$ in terms of the components of $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

**Method 1**

Each entry $a_{ij}$ of $A$ corresponds to the term $a_{ij}x_i x_j$ in the expression of the quadratic form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{T}Ax &= -1x_{1}x_{1} + (1)x_{1}x_{2} + (1)x_{2}x_{1} + - 1x_{2}x_{2} \\
&=-x_{1}^{2} + x_{1}x_{2} + x_{2}x_{1} - x_{2}^{2} \\
&=-x_{1}^{2} + 2x_{1}x_{2} - x_{2}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Method 2**

To write down a quadratic form in terms of vector components, we compute the following expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{T}Ax &= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x_{1} & x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-x_{1} + x_{2} \\ x_{1} - x_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= x_{1}(-x_{1} + x_{2}) + x_{2}(x_{1} - x_{2}) \\
&=-x_{1}^{2} + x_{1}x_{2} + x_{2}x_{1} - x_{2}^{2} \\
&=-x_{1}^{2} + 2x_{1}x_{2} - x_{2}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-matrix-of-a-quadratic-form"></a>
## Finding the Matrix of a Quadratic Form

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}a & 0 & b \\ 0 & 2 & c \\ b & c & 0\end{bmatrix}$
The matrix of the quadratic form $Q(\mathbf{x}) = x_1^2 - 2x_1x_3 + 4x_2x_3 + 2x_2^2$ is given above. What is the value of $a+b+c$?

**Explanation**

Since the matrix of the quadratic form must be symmetric, we rewrite our expression as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(x) &= x_{1}^{2} - 2x_{1}x_{3} + 4x_{2}x_{3} + 2x_{2}^{2} \\
&= 1x_{1}^{2} + 0x_{1}x_{2} + (-1)x_{1}x_{3} \\
&= + 0x_{2}x_{1} + 2x_{2}^{2} + 2x_{2}x_{3} \\
&= + (-1)x_{3}x_{1} + 2x_{3}x_{2} + 0x_{3}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that to get a symmetric matrix, we must do the following splits:

- we split the coefficient of ${\color{blue}-2}x_1x_3$ between the entries $a_{13}=a_{31}=\dfrac{\color{blue}-2}{2}=-1$, and
- we split the coefficient of ${\color{blue}4}x_2x_3$ between the entries $a_{23}=a_{32}=\dfrac{\color{blue}4}{2}=2$.

So, the matrix corresponding to our quadratic form is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, $a=1$, $b=-1$, and $c=2$. Finally,

$$
a+b+c=1+(-1)+2 = 2
$$
