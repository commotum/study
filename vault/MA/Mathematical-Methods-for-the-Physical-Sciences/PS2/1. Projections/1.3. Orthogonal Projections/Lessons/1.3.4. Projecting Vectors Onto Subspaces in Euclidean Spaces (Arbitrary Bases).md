## Introduction

We've already seen how to find the orthogonal projection of a vector onto a subspace in the case when the subspace is defined using an orthogonal basis. In this lesson, we'll learn how to do the same thing in the case of non-orthogonal bases.

For example, consider the following vectors:
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
How can we find the orthogonal projection $\mathbf{x}_S$ of the vector $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace

$$
S = \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}
$$

Notice that since

$$
\mathbf a_1\cdot \mathbf a_2 \neq 0
$$

the vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ are not orthogonal.

As usual, we would like to write $\mathbf{x}$ as

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_S + \mathbf{x}_{S^\perp}
$$

where

$$
\mathbf{x}_S = k_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + k_2 \mathbf{a}_2 \in S
$$

and $\mathbf{x}_{S^\perp} \!\in S^\perp$.

![](<../Source/1.3.4. Projecting Vectors Onto Subspaces in Euclidean Spaces (Arbitrary Bases)/Images/647ee475350f13f6aab009bcc20a387d.png>)

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{S^{⊥}} &= x - x_{S} \\
&= x - (k_{1}a_{1} + k_{2}a_{2}) \\
&= x - k_{1}a_{1} - k_{2}a_{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\mathbf{x}_{S^\perp} \in S^\perp$, that is, $\mathbf{x}_{S^\perp} \perp S$, we obtain

$$
{x_{S^{⊥}}⊥a_{1}; x_{S^{⊥}}⊥a_{2}⟹{x_{S^{⊥}} \cdot a_{1} = 0; x_{S^{⊥}} \cdot a_{2} = 0
$$

Now, we substitute the expression for $\mathbf{x}_{S^\perp}$ into the system to get

$$
{(x - k_{1}a_{1} - k_{2}a_{2}) \cdot a_{1} = 0; (x - k_{1}a_{1} - k_{2}a_{2}) \cdot a_{2} = 0
$$

We can write this system in an equivalent matrix form as follows:
$\begin{bmatrix}-a_{1} - \\ -a_{2} -\end{bmatrix}$

If we denote
$[\mid \mid \mid; a_{1} \mid a_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$
and $\begin{bmatrix}k_{1} \\ k_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ then we obtain

$$
A^TA\mathbf{k} = A^T\mathbf{x} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{k}=(A^TA)^{-1}A^T\mathbf{x}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= Ak \\
&= A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}x
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a formula for the orthogonal projection!

Now, we return to our example. Using the formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}6 & 8 \\ 8 & 11\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 14\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}11 & -8 \\ -8 & 6\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 14\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note**: Pay attention to the order in which we carried out the multiplication. Carrying out the operations in the order shown above can save us a lot of work!

<a id="the-general-formula-for-the-orthogonal-projection-of-a-vector-onto-a-subspace"></a>
## The General Formula for the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector Onto a Subspace

If $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n \}$ is a set of *linearly independent* vectors, then the orthogonal projection of a vector $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace

$$
S = \textrm{span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n \}
$$

is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

where $A$ is the matrix whose columns are the vectors of $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$, that is,
$[\mid \mid \mid \mid … \mid \mid; a_{1}\begin{vmatrix}a_{2} & …\end{vmatrix}a_{n}; \mid \mid \mid \mid … \mid \mid]$

**Watch out!** The fact that $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n \}$ must be linearly independent is important. If the vectors are linearly dependent, we need to find the basis of the span before applying the formula.

<a id="finding-the-orthogonal-projection-of-a-vector-onto-a-plane"></a>
## Finding the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector Onto a Plane

**Example:** Find the orthogonal projection of the vector $\mathbf{x}$ onto the plane $\mathbf{r}=s\mathbf{v}+t\mathbf{w}$, where $s,t\in\mathbb R$, and
$\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ -2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, notice that $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf w$ are linearly independent.

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the columns of a matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A\left(A^T\!A\right)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

Since our plane passes through the origin, it defines the subspace

$$
S=\text{Span}\{\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}\}
$$

in $\mathbb{R}^3$. In our case, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; v \mid w; \mid \mid \mid]$
Therefore, we get the following projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 & 2 \\ -2 & -2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 & 2 \\ -2 & -2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 8\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}12 & 4 \\ 4 & 12\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}12 \\ -20\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{32}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}12 \\ -20\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 \\ -2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}7 \\ -9\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 8\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector Onto a Subspace

**Example:** Verify whether the vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3$ are linearly independent, and find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the vectors $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3 \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we verify whether $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3$ are linearly independent. So, we start by creating a matrix whose columns are made up of our vectors, and then we reduce the matrix to row echelon form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{3}↔R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + (-3)R_{1} \\
&= R_{3}:=R_{3} + (-4)R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & -2 & -10 \\ 0 & -3 & -15\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{3}:=R_{3} + (-\frac{3}{2})R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & -2 & -10 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, the pivot columns correspond to the linearly independent vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ that form the basis of our span.

Now, the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the linearly independent columns of a matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

In our case, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; a_{1} \mid a_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$

Therefore, we get the following projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}5 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}26 & 8 \\ 8 & 3\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}18 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{14}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -8 \\ -8 & 26\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}18 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="some-intuition-behind-the-formula"></a>
## Some Intuition Behind the Formula

As we've already seen, the orthogonal projection of a vector $\mathbf{x}$ onto the one-dimensional subspace $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{a}} \, \mathbf{x} = \dfrac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a}} \: \mathbf{a}
$$

If we suppose that $\mathbf{a}$ denotes the column-vector $[\mid; a; \mid]$ then the dot product $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a}$ is simply $\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{a}$, and we can write the formula as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{a}x &= ((a \cdot x))/((a \cdot a))a \\
&= [((a^{T}x))/((a^{T}a))]a \\
&= a[((a^{T}x))/((a^{T}a))] \\
&= a[(a^{T}a)^{-1}a^{T}x] \\
&= a(a^{T}a)^{-1}a^{T}x
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let's compare the above with the formula that we use when we want to project onto an arbitrary subspace:

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

As we can see, they are basically the same thing!
