## Introduction

We can use the following property to simplify expressions involving expected values:

> For any random variables $X$ and $Y$ and constants $a$ and $b$, we have
> $\textrm E[aX+bY] = a \textrm E[X] + b \textrm E[Y]$.

In other words, the expected value of a sum of scaled random variables equals the sum of the scaled expected values.

For example,

$$
\textrm E[2X+5Y] = 2 \textrm E[X] + 5 \textrm E[Y]
$$

Note that the property also works with subtraction:

$$
\textrm E[2X-5Y] = 2 \textrm E[X] - 5 \textrm E[Y]
$$

We can extend this idea to any number of random variables. For example, for any random variables $X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_n$, and real constants $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n$, we have

$$
\textrm{E}[a_1X_1+a_2X_2+\cdots+ a_nX_n]= a_1\textrm E[X_1]+a_2\textrm E[X_2]+\cdots +a_n\textrm{E}[X_n]
$$

or more concisely,

$$
\textrm{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_i\right] =\sum_{i=1}^n a_i \textrm E[X_i]
$$

Note that the random variables $X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_n$, are *not* required to be independent.

<a id="finding-the-expected-value-of-a-sum-of-random-variables"></a>
## Finding the Expected Value of a Sum of Random Variables

**Example:** Compute $\textrm E[5X-2Y]$ if $\textrm E[X]=1$ and $\textrm E[Y] = 2$.

**Explanation**

For any random variables $X,Y$ and any constants $a,b$, we have

$$
\textrm E[aX+bY] = a \textrm E[X] + b \textrm E[Y]
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[5X - 2Y] &= 5E[X] - 2E[Y] \\
&= 5(1) - 2(2) \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-expected-value-of-a-sum-of-random-variables-in-context"></a>
## Finding the Expected Value of a Sum of Random Variables in Context

**Example:** Andrea has two dice. One of them is fair, and the other is not. The rolls of the unfair die follow the probability mass function $f(y)$ shown in the table below. What is the expected sum of the outcomes of $2$ throws of the fair die and $4$ throws of the unfair one?

| $y$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ | $6$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $f(y)$ | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{16}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{8}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{16}$ |

**Explanation**

Let $X_i$ be the $i$th score obtained from the fair die, and let $Y_j$ be the $j$th score obtained from the unfair die. Also, let $X$ and $Y$ represent the scores obtained from a typical throw of the fair and unfair die, respectively.

Then, we need to calculate

$$
\textrm E\left[ \sum_{i=1}^2 X_i + \sum_{j=1}^4 Y_j \right]
$$

Using the properties of expected value, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[∑_(i = 1)^(2)X_{i} + ∑_(j = 1)^(4)Y_{j}] &= ∑_(i = 1)^(2)E[X_{i}] + ∑_(j = 1)^(4)E[Y_{j}] \\
&= 2E[X] + 4E[Y]
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last equality above is true because

$$
\textrm E[X_i] = \textrm E[X]
$$

and

$$
\textrm E[Y_j] = \textrm E[Y]
$$

for all

$$
1\leq i \leq 2,\: 1\leq j\leq 4
$$

The expected values of $X$ and $Y$ are calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= 1 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{6} + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{7}{2} \\
E[Y] &= 1 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{16} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{8} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{16} = \frac{13}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, we get

$$
\textrm E[2X+4Y]=2\textrm E[X]+4\textrm E[Y]= 2\cdot \dfrac{7}{2}+4\cdot \dfrac{13}{4} =20
$$

## The Expectation of a Product of Random Variables

The expected value of the product of two *independent* random variables is equal to the product of the expected values. So, if the random variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent, we have

$$
\textrm{E}[X\cdot Y] = \textrm E[X]\cdot \textrm E[Y]
$$

For example, if we know that $X$ are $Y$ are independent, and that

$$
\textrm E[X]=2
$$

and

$$
\textrm E[Y]=3
$$

, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X \cdot Y] &= E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= 2 \cdot 3 \\
&= 6
\end{aligned}
$$

When working with the expected value of a product of random variables, it's important to check that the variables are independent. The formula above is *not* true if $X$ and $Y$ are not independent.

More generally, if the random variables $X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_n$ are *mutually* independent, then

$$
\textrm E[X_1\cdot X_2 \cdots X_n]=\textrm E[X_1]\cdot \textrm E[X_2]\cdot\ldots\cdot \textrm E[X_n]
$$

or more concisely,

$$
\textrm{E}\left[ \prod_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \textrm E[X_i]
$$

where $\prod$ is the product operator.

<a id="finding-the-expected-value-of-a-product-of-random-variables"></a>
## Finding the Expected Value of a Product of Random Variables

**Example:** The probability distributions of the independent random variables $X$ and $Y$ are given below.

| $x$$-4$$-2$$2$$4$$f(x)$$\dfrac{1}{6}$$\dfrac{1}{3}$$\dfrac{1}{3}$$\dfrac{1}{6}$ |  | $y$$1$$2$$3$$f(y)$$\dfrac{1}{4}$$\dfrac{1}{4}$$\dfrac{1}{2}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |

Find $\textrm E[XY]$.

**Explanation**

Recall that if $X$ and $Y$ are *independent* random variables, then

$$
\textrm E[X\cdot Y]=\textrm E[X]\cdot \textrm E[Y]
$$

The expected values of $X$ and $Y$ are calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= -4 \cdot \frac{1}{6} - 2 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = 0 \\
E[Y] &= 1 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $X$ and $Y$ are independent, we have

$$
\textrm E[X\cdot Y] = \textrm E[X] \cdot \textrm E[Y] = 0 \cdot \dfrac{9}{4} = 0
$$
