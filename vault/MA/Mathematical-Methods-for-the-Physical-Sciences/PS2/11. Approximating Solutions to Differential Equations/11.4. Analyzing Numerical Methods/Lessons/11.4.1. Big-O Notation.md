## Introduction

When studying limiting behavior, it is often useful to compare the size of one function to another near a given point. **Big-O** notation provides a concise way to express such boundedness.

For example, saying that a function is $\mathcal O(1)$ near a point means that it remains *bounded* within some neighborhood of that point - it does not "blow up" as we approach the limit.

Formally, we say that $f(x)$ **is of order** $g(x)$ as $x\to a$, and we write

$$
f(x)=\mathcal O(g(x)) \qquad \text{as} \qquad x\to a
$$

if there exist positive constants $K$ and $\delta$ such that

$$
\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & \le K & g(x) & \qquad \text{for all} 0< & x-a\end{vmatrix}<\delta
$$

To illustrate this, consider the polynomial

$$
f(x) = -7x^3 + 3x + 4
$$

We will prove that

$$
f=\mathcal O(1)
$$

as $x\to 0$.

In this case, we must show that

$$
\begin{vmatrix}f & \leq K\cdot 1 \qquad \textrm{for} \qquad 0 < & x\end{vmatrix} < \delta
$$

i.e.,

$$
{\begin{vmatrix}f & \leq K} \qquad \textrm{for} \qquad {0 < \mid x\end{vmatrix} < \delta}
$$

By the triangle inequality, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & &= & -7x^{3} + 3x + 4\end{vmatrix} \\
&\le\begin{vmatrix}7x^{3} & + & 3x & + & 4\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 7\begin{vmatrix}x & ^{3} + 3 & x\end{vmatrix}+ 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let $\mid x \mid < 1$. Then, we have

$$
\mid f(x) \mid \mid \le 7\begin{vmatrix}x & ^{3} + 3 & x & + 4 \\ < 7 & 1 & ^{3} + 3 & 1\end{vmatrix}+ 4; = 14
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mid f(x) \mid \leq 14
$$

whenever $\mid x \mid < 1$. Thus, taking ${K=14}$ and ${\delta =1}$ satisfies the definition, and we conclude

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(1) \qquad \text{as} \qquad x\to 0
$$

<a id="proving-the-order-of-a-polynomial"></a>
## Proving the Order of a Polynomial

**Example:** For the polynomial $f(x) = -3x^4 + x^2 + 4x$, show that $f = \mathcal O(x)$ as $x\to 0$.

**Explanation**

We say $f(x)$ is of order $g(x)$ as $x\to a$, and we write

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(g(x)) \quad \textrm{as}\quad x\to a
$$

if there exist ${\textrm{positive}}$ constants $K$ and $\delta$ such that

$$
{\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & \leq K & g(x) &} \quad\textrm{for all}\quad 0 < & x-a\end{vmatrix} < \delta
$$

So, in our case, we need to show that

$$
{\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & \leq K & x &} \quad \textrm{for} \quad {0 < \mid x\end{vmatrix} < \delta}
$$

By the triangle inequality, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & &= & -3x^{4} + x^{2} + 4x\end{vmatrix} \\
&\le\begin{vmatrix}3x^{4} & + & x^{2} & + & 4x\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 3\begin{vmatrix}x & ^{4} + & x & ^{2} + 4 & x\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

If $\mid x \mid < 1$, then we have ${\begin{vmatrix}x & ^2 \lt & x\end{vmatrix}}$. Also, since $\mid x \mid <1$, we have $\begin{vmatrix}x & ^4 < & x\end{vmatrix}$. Therefore,

$$
3\begin{vmatrix}x & ^4 \le 3 & x & \quad\text{and}\quad & x & ^2 \le & x\end{vmatrix}
$$

Hence

$$
\mid f(x) \mid \mid \le 3\begin{vmatrix}x & ^{4} + & x & ^{2} + 4 & x\end{vmatrix}; \le 3\begin{vmatrix}x & + & x & + 4 & x\end{vmatrix}; = 8 \mid x \mid
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & \leq 8 & x\end{vmatrix}
$$

whenever $\mid x \mid < 1$. Thus, taking ${K=8}$ and

$$
{\delta = 1}
$$

satisfies the definition, and we conclude that

$$
f = \mathcal O(x)
$$

as $x\to 0$.

<a id="infinite-limit"></a>
## Infinite Limit

We say $f(x)$ *is of order* $g(x)$ as $x\to \infty$, and we write

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(g(x)) \qquad \textrm{as} \qquad x\to \infty
$$

if there exist positive constants $K$ and $M$ such that

$$
\begin{vmatrix}f(x) & \leq K & g(x)\end{vmatrix}\qquad\textrm{for all}\qquad x > M
$$

We often abbreviate this to simply

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(g(x))
$$

In other words, when there is no $x\to a$ statement, we assume $a$ is infinite.

---

<a id="the-limit-test"></a>
## The Limit Test

Suppose we wish to test whether the following statement is true:

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(g(x)) \qquad \textrm{as}\qquad x\to a
$$

To do this, we can examine the following limit:

$$
\lim\limits_{x\to a} \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}
$$

We have the following:

- If the limit is *finite*, the statement is true.
- If the limit is *infinite*, the statement is false.
- If the limit does not exist, the test is *inconclusive*.

Note that the *same* limit test works for $x\to a$ and $x\to\infty$ order statements!

Let's see some examples.

---

<a id="applying-the-limit-test"></a>
## Applying the Limit Test

**Example:** Suppose $f(x) = e^x + x\ln x$. Which of the following statements are true?

1. $f= \mathcal O(x)$ as $x\to\infty$
2. $f= \mathcal O(\ln x)$ as $x\to\infty$
3. $f= \mathcal O(xe^{x})$ as $x\to\infty$

**Explanation**

Suppose we wish to test whether the following statement is true:

$$
f(x) = \mathcal O(g(x)) \quad \textrm{as}\quad x\to a
$$

where $a$ is either finite or $+\infty$. To do this, we can examine the following limit:

$$
\lim\limits_{x\to a} \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}
$$

We have the following:

- If the limit is *finite*, the statement is true.
- If the limit is *infinite*, the statement is false.
- If the limit does not exist, the test is inconclusive.

Here, we must consider the function

$$
f(x) = e^x + x\ln x
$$

Now, let's consider each statement in turn.

- Statement I is false. Let $g(x) = x$. Then, we have
$lim_(x → ∞)(f(x))/(g(x))|= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + x\ln x}{x}$.
Since direct evaluation leads to the indeterminate form $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$, we can evaluate the limit using L'Hopital's rule:
$lim_(x → ∞)(f(x))/(g(x))|= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + x\ln x}{x}; = lim_(x → ∞)((e^{x} + x\ln x)^{′})/((x)^{′}); = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + \ln x + 1}{1}; = 1 + lim_(x → ∞)(e^{x} + \ln x); = 1 + ∞; = ∞$.
Hence, $f \neq \mathcal O(x)$ as $x\to\infty$.

- Statement II is false. Let $g(x) =\ln x$. Then, using L'Hopital's rule where needed, we have
$lim_(x → ∞)(f(x))/(g(x))|= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + x\ln x}{\ln x}; = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + x\ln x}{\ln x}; = lim_(x → ∞)((e^{x} + x\ln x)^{′})/((\ln x)^{′}); = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + \ln x + 1}{1/x}; = lim_(x → ∞)x(e^{x} + \ln x + 1); = ∞$.
Hence, $f \neq \mathcal O(x)$ as $x\to\infty$.

- Statement III is true. Let $g(x) = xe^{x}$. Then, using L'Hopital's rule where needed, we have
$lim_(x → ∞)(f(x))/(g(x))|= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{e^{x} + x\ln x}{xe^{x}}; = lim_(x → ∞)(\frac{1}{x} + \frac{\ln x}{e^{x}}); = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{1}{x} + lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\ln x}{e^{x}}; = 0 + lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\ln x}{e^{x}}; = lim_(x → ∞)((\ln x)^{′})/((e^{x})^{′}); = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{1/x}{e^{x}}; = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{1}{xe^{x}}; = 0$.
Hence, $f = \mathcal O(xe^x)$ as $x\to\infty$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."

<a id="properties-of-big-o"></a>
## Properties of Big-O

We also have the following facts:

- *The sum and product properties*. If $f = \mathcal O(h)$ and $g = \mathcal O(k)$ as $x\to a$, then
$f+g = \mathcal O(h+k)\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad fg = \mathcal O(hk)\qquad\textrm{as}\qquad x\to a$.
- *The constant multiple property*. If $f = \mathcal O(cg)$ as $x\to a$, then
$f = \mathcal O(g)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- *The reflexive property*.
$f = \mathcal O(f)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- *The transitive property*. If $f = \mathcal O(g)$ and $g = \mathcal O(h)$ as $x\to a$, then
$f = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- *The absorption property*. If $f = \mathcal O(g+h)$ and $g = \mathcal O(h)$ as $x\to a$, then
$f = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.

Let's see how they can be used in practice.

---

<a id="applying-properties-of-big-o"></a>
## Applying Properties of Big-O

**Example:** Consider the following statement.

> If $f(x) = e^{x}+ \sec x$, then $f = \mathcal O(1)$ as $x\to 0$.

A sketch of a proof of this statement is given below

> $\textrm{L1}{:}\; e^{x} = \mathcal O(1)$ as $x\to 0$
> $\textrm{L2}{:}\; \sec x = \mathcal O(1)$ as $x\to 0$
> $\textrm{L3}{:}\; f = \mathcal O(2)$ as $x\to 0$
> $\textrm{L4}{:}\; f = \mathcal O(1)$ as $x\to 0$

Fill in the blanks in the reasoning below that justify each step.

$\quad$ Line $\textrm{L3}$ follows from lines $XXXXXXXXXX$ and the $XXXXXXX$ property.

$\quad$ Line $\textrm{L4}$ follows from line $XXXXX$ and the $XXXXXXX$.

**Explanation**

Suppose $f(x), g(x), h(x)$ and $k(x)$ are functions and $c$ is a nonzero constant. Then, we have the following properties of Big-O notation.

- The sum and product properties. If
$f = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{and}\quad g = \mathcal O(k)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$,
then
$f+g = \mathcal O(h+k)\quad\textrm{and}\quad fg = \mathcal O(hk)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- The constant multiple property. If
$f = \mathcal O(cg)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$,
then
$f = \mathcal O(g)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- The reflexive property.
$f = \mathcal O(f)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- The transitive property. If
$f = \mathcal O(g) \quad\textrm{and}\quad g = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$,
then
$f = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.
- The absorption property. If
$f = \mathcal O(g+h)\quad\textrm{and}\quad g = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$,
then
$f = \mathcal O(h)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to a$.

With that in mind, let us justify each selected step.

- We first consider line $\textrm{L3}$. From line $\textrm{L1}$ we have
$e^{x} = \mathcal O(1)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to 0$,
and from line $\textrm{L2}$ we have
$\sec x = \mathcal O(1)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to 0$.
Thus, by lines $\bbox[4pt,border:solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{L1 and L3}}$ and $\bbox[4pt,border:solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{the sum property}}$, we obtain
$f = \mathcal O(1+1) = \mathcal O(2)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to 0$.
- Finally, we consider line $\textrm{L4}$. From line $\bbox[4pt,border:solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{L4}}$ we have
$f = \mathcal O(2)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to 0$.
Since $2$ is just a constant multiple of $1$, it follows by $\bbox[4pt,border:solid 1pt gainsboro]{\text{the constant multiple property}}$ that
$f = \mathcal O(1)\quad\textrm{as}\quad x\to 0$.
