## Introduction

Similar to the case of *closed* curves, there is always a well-defined notion of "inside" and "outside" a closed *surface*.

For closed surfaces, the convention is the positive orientation is always given by the *outward-pointing* unit normal vector.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/8544251e538c7bf97b3af0728aeab4ba.png>)

When calculating the flux of a vector field $\mathbf F$ across a closed surface, we always measure the flux relative to the surface's positive orientation.

Let's see an example.

---

<a id="computing-the-flux-of-a-vector-field-over-a-rectangular-solid"></a>
## Computing the Flux of a Vector Field Over a Rectangular Solid

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F(x,y,z) = \langle 0, \: 0, \: 1 + xyz \rangle$ and the surface $S$ defined by the bounding region of the rectangular solid shown above in the first octant. Calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/cac638a0e26e72df4bdfca80611d5bba.png>)

**Explanation**

If $S$ is an oriented surface with unit normal vector $\mathbf n$, and $\mathbf F$ is continuous vector field, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S$ measured with respect to $\mathbf n$ is given by

$$
\iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \textrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}S
$$

Notice that only the $z$-component of

$$
\mathbf F = \langle 0,\: 0,\: 1+xyz\rangle
$$

is non-zero. Since the lateral sides of $S$ are parallel to the $z$-axis, the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$ will be non-zero only at the top and bottom sides.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/af13f1982951f06cd09e8d9249599d81.png>)

Let's consider the flux of $\mathbf F$ through these two sides.

- Let $S_1$ denote the top surface. On this surface, we have $z = 1$ for $0 \leq x \leq 1$ and $0 \leq y \leq 1$. The outward-pointing unit normal vector to $S_1$ is
$\mathbf n_1 = \langle 0,\, 0,\, 1\rangle$.
Using the fact that $z = 1$ on $S_1$, we have
$F \cdot n_{1}|= ⟨0, 0, 1 + xyz⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0, 1⟩; = ⟨0, 0, 1 + xy⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0, 1⟩; = 1 + xy$.
Therefore, we can calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S_1$ as follows:
$∬_(S_{1})F \cdot dS|= ∬_(S_{1})F \cdot n_{1}dS; = ∬_(S_{1})1 + xydS; = ∬_(D)1 + xydA; = ∫_{0}^{1}∫_{0}^{1}1 + xydxdy; = ∫_{0}^{1}[x + \frac{1}{2}x^{2}y]_{x = 0}^{x = 1}dy; = ∫_{0}^{1}1 + \frac{1}{2}ydy; = [y + \frac{1}{4}y^{2}]_{0}^{1}; = \frac{5}{4}$
- Let $S_2$ denote the bottom surface. On this surface, we have $z = 0$ for $0 \leq x \leq 1$ and $0 \leq y \leq 1$. The outward-pointing unit normal vector to $S_2$ is
$\mathbf n_2 = \langle 0,\, 0,\, -1\rangle$.
Using the fact that $z = 0$ on $S_2$, we have
$F \cdot n_{2}|= ⟨0, 0, 1 + xyz⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0,-1⟩; = ⟨0, 0, 1⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0,-1⟩; =-1$.
Therefore, we can calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S_2$ as follows:
$∬_(S_{2})F \cdot dS_{2}|= ∬_(S_{2})F \cdot n_{2}dS; = ∬_(S_{2}) - 1dS; = ∬_(D) - 1dA; = - ∬_(D)dA; =-Area(D); = - (1 - 0)(1 - 0); =-1$

Finally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(S)F \cdot dS &= ∬_(S)F \cdot ndS \\
&= ∬_(S_{1})F \cdot ndS + ∬_(S_{2})F \cdot ndS \\
&= \frac{5}{4} + (-1) \\
&= \frac{1}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-the-flux-of-a-vector-field-over-a-cylinder"></a>
## Computing the Flux of a Vector Field Over a Cylinder

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F(x,y,z) = \langle 0,\: 0,\: z\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \rangle$ and the surface $S$ defined by the bounding region of a right circular cylinder of height $4$ whose base is a circle of radius $1$ centered at the origin, as shown above. Calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/0b8c76a27f89a5bf55914475ba7b8324.png>)

**Explanation**

If $S$ is an oriented surface with unit normal vector $\mathbf n$, and $\mathbf F$ is continuous vector field, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S$ measured with respect to $\mathbf n$ is given by

$$
\iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \textrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}S
$$

Notice that only the $z$-component of

$$
\mathbf F = \langle 0,\: 0,\: z\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \rangle
$$

is non-zero. Since the lateral side of $S$ is parallel to the $z$-axis, the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$ will be non-zero only at the top and bottom sides.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/f750ea73c79c9c2eddfa45ef88626057.png>)

Let's consider the flux of $\mathbf F$ through these two sides.

- Let $S_1$ denote the top surface. On this surface, we have $z = 4$ for $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1$. The outward-pointing unit normal vector to $S_1$ is
$\mathbf n_1 = \langle 0,\, 0,\, 1\rangle$.
Using the fact that $z = 4$ on $S_1$, we have
$F \cdot n_{1}|= ⟨0, 0, z\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0, 1⟩; = ⟨0, 0, 4\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}⟩ \cdot ⟨0, 0, 1⟩; = 4\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}$.
Therefore, the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S_1$ is given by
$∬_(S_{1})F \cdot dS|= ∬_(S_{1})F \cdot n_{1}dS; = ∬_(S_{1})4\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}dS; = 4∬_(D)\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}dA$.

Applying a change of variables

$$
(x,y) \to (r,\theta), \textrm d A = r\,\textrm d r\,\textrm d \theta
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
4∬_(D)\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}dA &= 4∬_(D)r \cdot rdrdθ \\
&= 4∫_{0}^{2π}∫_{0}^{1}r^{2}drdθ \\
&= 4∫_{0}^{2π}dθ∫_{0}^{1}r^{2}dr \\
&= 4 \cdot 2π \cdot [\frac{1}{3}r^{3}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= 8π(\frac{1}{3}) \\
&= \frac{8π}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

- Let $S_2$ denote the bottom surface. On this surface, we have $z = 0$ for $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1$. Therefore, for every point on $S_2$, we have
$F(x,y,0) = \langle 0,\: 0,\: 0 \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \rangle = \langle 0,\: 0,\: 0\rangle$.
Therefore,
$∬_(S_{2})F \cdot ndS|= ∬_(S_{2})0 \cdot ndS; = ∬_(S_{2})0dS; = 0$.

Finally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(S)F \cdot dS &= ∬_(S)F \cdot ndS \\
&= ∬_(S_{1})F \cdot ndS + ∬_(S_{2})F \cdot ndS \\
&= \frac{8π}{3} + 0 \\
&= \frac{8π}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-outward-pointing-unit-vector-to-a-sphere"></a>
## The Outward-Pointing Unit Vector to a Sphere

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/e8281d3cdd79132c4993e3be694e387b.png>)

Given a sphere of radius $r$ centered at the origin, an outward-pointing unit normal vector to the sphere at the point $P(x,y,z)$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{⟨x, y, z⟩}{r}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can sometimes use this to calculate the flux of a vector field through a sphere. Let's see an example.

---

<a id="computing-the-flux-of-a-vector-field-over-a-sphere"></a>
## Computing the Flux of a Vector Field Over a Sphere

**Example:** Consider the vector field $\mathbf F(x,y,z) = \langle 2x,\: 2y+xz,\: 2z-xy\rangle$ and the boundary $S$ of a spherical solid of radius $1$ centered at the origin. Calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$.

![](<../Source/6.3.4. Flux Through Closed Surfaces/Images/4b1c5dcc75491a1b343943157f8828e0.png>)

**Explanation**

If $S$ is an oriented surface with unit normal vector $\mathbf n$, and $\mathbf F$ is continuous vector field, then the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S$ measured with respect to $\mathbf n$ is given by

$$
\iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \textrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iint\limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \,\textrm{d}S
$$

For a sphere or radius $r$ centered at $O$, the unit normal to the sphere is given by

$$
\mathbf n = \dfrac{\langle x, y, z\rangle}{r}
$$

In our case, we have $r=1$. Therefore,

$$
\mathbf n = \langle x, y, z\rangle
$$

Using the fact that

$$
x^2+y^2+z^2=1
$$

on $S$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
F \cdot n &= ⟨2x, 2y + xz, 2z - xy⟩ \cdot ⟨x, y, z⟩ \\
&= 2x^{2} + (2y + xz) \cdot y + (2z - xy) \cdot z \\
&= 2x^{2} + 2y^{2} + xyz + 2z^{2} - xyz \\
&= 2(x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2}) \\
&= 2 \cdot 1 \\
&= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we can calculate the flux of $\mathbf F$ across $S$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(S)F \cdot dS &= ∬_(S)F \cdot ndS \\
&= ∬_(S)2dS \\
&= 2∬_(S)dS \\
&= 2 \cdot Area(S) \\
&= 2 \cdot 4π \cdot 1^{2} \\
&= 8π
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="deriving-the-unit-normal-vector-for-a-sphere"></a>
## Deriving the Unit Normal Vector for a Sphere

Let's derive the formula for a unit normal vector for a sphere of radius $r$ centered at the origin.

Consider the function

$$
w = f (x, y, z)= \dfrac 1 2\left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - r^2\right)
$$

The level surface corresponding to $w=0$ is the sphere $S$ with equation

$$
x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2
$$

Consider the point $P(x,y,z)$ that lies on $S$. Then, $\nabla f(x,y,z)$ is a normal vector to the level surface at the point $P$.

Computing $\nabla f(x,y,z)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∇f(x, y, z) &= ⟨f_{x}, f_{y}, f_{z}⟩ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}⟨2x, 2y, 2z⟩ \\
&= ⟨x, y, z⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $P(x,y,z)$ lies on $S$, we have

$$
x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2
$$

Normalizing $\nabla f(x,y,z)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= (∇f(x, y, z))/(‖∇f(x, y, z)‖) \\
&= \frac{⟨x, y, z⟩}{\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2}}} \\
&= \frac{⟨x, y, z⟩}{r}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the unit normal vector for a sphere of radius $r$ centered at the origin is

$$
\begin{aligned}
n &= \frac{⟨x, y, z⟩}{r}
\end{aligned}
$$
