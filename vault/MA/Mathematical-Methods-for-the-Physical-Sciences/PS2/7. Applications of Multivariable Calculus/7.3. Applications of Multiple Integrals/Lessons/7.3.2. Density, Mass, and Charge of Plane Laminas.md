## Introduction

The **mass density function** $\lambda (x,y)$ of a two-dimensional shape describes how its mass is distributed across the shape.

More precisely, $\lambda(x,y)$ tells us the *mass per unit area* at any point $(x,y)$ on the shape. The units of $\lambda$ are usually $\textrm{kg}/\textrm{m}^2$.

Suppose we want to determine the total mass of the semi-circular metal plate $D$ with mass density function $\lambda(x,y)$, shown below.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Density, Mass, and Charge of Plane Laminas/Images/12ebe88d2913def5cce7094b1f77efb2.png>)

The small rectangle shown has mass $\delta M$ equal to its average density (which we can assume is approximately $\lambda (x,y)$) times the area of the piece.

$$
\delta M = \lambda (x, y)\, \delta x \delta y = \lambda (x, y) \, \delta A
$$

By summing all contributions to the mass made by each small rectangle and taking the limit as $\delta A \to 0$ in the usual way, we find that the mass of the entire plate is given by

$$
M = \iint \limits_{D} \lambda(x,y) \, \textrm{d}A
$$

Note that since mass is never negative, we must have

$$
\lambda \geq 0
$$

for $\lambda$ to be a valid mass density function.

<a id="calculating-the-electric-charge-over-a-metal-plate"></a>
## Calculating the Electric Charge Over a Metal Plate

We can also use density functions to describe the distribution of electric charge across a metallic plate.

The distribution of electric charge is given by its **charge density function,** denoted $\rho(x,y)$. The units of $\rho$ are $\textrm{C}/\textrm{m}^2$, where $\textrm C$ is the standard unit of electric charge, known as the **coulomb**.

In general, the total electric charge $Q$ of a two-dimensional region $D$ with the charge density function $\rho(x,y)$ is given by

$$
Q = \iint\limits_D \rho(x,y) \: \mathrm d A
$$

Since electric charge can be positive *and* negative, $\rho$ can be any real number.

<a id="computing-mass-or-charge-over-a-rectangular-domain"></a>
## Computing Mass or Charge Over a Rectangular Domain

**Example:** Find the total charge $Q$ of a metal plate that occupies the region $D = \{(x,y): 0 \le x \le 1, \, 0 \le y \le 2\}$ with charge density $\rho (x,y) = 4 - 3y^2 + 2x$.

**Explanation**

The total charge $Q$ of a plate that occupies the region

$$
D = \{(x,y): 0 \leq x \leq 1, \, 0 \leq y \leq 2\}
$$

with charge density

$$
\rho (x,y) = 4 - 3y^2 + 2x
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= ∬_(D)(4 - 3y^{2} + 2x)dA
\end{aligned}
$$

First, we express our double integral as a repeated integral:

$$
\iint\limits_{D} (4 - 3y^2 + 2x) \:\textrm{d}A = \int_{0}^2 \left[ \int_{0}^1 (4 - 3y^2 + 2x) \:\textrm{d}x \right] \textrm{d}y
$$

Now, we evaluate the inner integral by integrating with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}[∫_{0}^{1}(4 - 3y^{2} + 2x)dx]dy &= ∫_{0}^{2}[4x - 3y^{2}x + x^{2}]_{0}^{1}dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}[(4(1) - 3y^{2}(1) + (1)^{2}) - (4(0) - 3y^{2}(0) - (0)^{2})]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}(5 - 3y^{2})dy
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we integrate with respect to $y{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}(5 - 3y^{2})dy &= [5y - y^{3}]_{0}^{2} \\
&= (5(2) - (2)^{3}) - (5(0) - (0)^{3}) \\
&= (10 - 8) - 0 \\
&= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the charge of the plate is $Q=2$.

<a id="computing-mass-or-charge-over-a-type-i-or-type-ii-region"></a>
## Computing Mass or Charge Over a Type I or Type II Region

**Example:** A triangular plate occupies the region $D$ enclosed between the lines $x=1,y=0$ and $y=x$, as shown above. The plate's mass density function $\lambda$ is given by $\lambda(x,y) = xy^2$. Find the total mass of the triangular plate.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Density, Mass, and Charge of Plane Laminas/Images/f15db6d8baa8892b061cfaa81871fd0e.png>)

**Explanation**

The mass of the triangular plate occupying the region $D$ with mass density

$$
\lambda(x,y) = x^2y
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= ∬_(D)xy^{2}dA
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $D$ is a type I region. So, we can express the integral as follows:

$$
\iint \limits_{D} xy^2\,\mathrm{d}A = \int_{0}^1 \int_{0}^{x} xy^2\,\mathrm{d}y \, \mathrm{d}x
$$

First, we evaluate the inner integral by integrating with respect to $y$, treating $x$ as a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= ∫_{0}^{1}x[∫_{0}^{x}y^{2}dy]dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x[\frac{y^{3}}{3}]_{0}^{x}dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}x(\frac{x^{3}}{3} - 0)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{3}∫_{0}^{1}x^{4}dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we integrate with respect to $x{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{3}∫_{0}^{1}x^{4}dx &= \frac{1}{3}[\frac{x^{5}}{5}]_{0}^{1} \\
&= \frac{1}{3}(\frac{1}{5} - 0) \\
&= \frac{1}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total mass of the plate is

$$
\dfrac{1}{15}
$$

<a id="computing-mass-or-charge-using-polar-coordinates"></a>
## Computing Mass or Charge Using Polar Coordinates

**Example:** Find the total charge $Q$ of a copper plate that occupies the region $D$ shown above, and has charge density $\rho (x,y) = \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$.

![](<../Source/7.3.2. Density, Mass, and Charge of Plane Laminas/Images/c3d92d5e0c81c1871fad053bd3726a50.png>)

**Explanation**

First, notice that the region $D$ represents one-quarter of the disk of radius $3$ centered at

$$
\left(0,0\right)
$$

The total charge $Q$ of an object occupying the region $D$ with charge density

$$
\rho (x,y) = \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}
$$

is given by

$$
Q = \iint\limits_{D} \dfrac{4x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \,\textrm{d}A
$$

We need to change the coordinate system from Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates. To do this, we will use the change of variables formula in the form

$$
\iint\limits_{D} \rho(x,y) \ \mathrm d A = \iint\limits_{\Delta} \ \rho(r \cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \: r \ \mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta
$$

The following equations connect Cartesian and polar coordinates:

$$
{x = r\cos θ; y = r\sin θ
$$

With that in mind, let's now write the curve

$$
x^2+y^2=9
$$

in polar coordinates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + y^{2} &= 9 \\
(r\cos θ)^{2} + (r\sin θ)^{2} &= 9 \\
r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ) &= 9 \\
r^{2} &= 9 \\
r &= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the region can be expressed as

$$
\Delta = \Big\{(r,\theta) \:: \: 0 \leq r \leq 3, \: \pi \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{3\pi}{2} \Big\}
$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
∬_(D)\frac{4x}{\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}}dA &= ∬_(Δ)\frac{4r\cos θ}{\sqrt{r^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ}}rdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{π}^{3π/2}∫_{0}^{3}4r\cos θdrdθ \\
&= ∫_{π}^{3π/2}4\cos θ[∫_{0}^{3}rdr]dθ \\
&= ∫_{π}^{3π/2}4\cos θ[\frac{r^{2}}{2}]_{0}^{3}dθ \\
&= ∫_{π}^{3π/2}4\cos θ(\frac{9}{2} - 0)dθ \\
&= 18∫_{π}^{3π/2}\cos θdθ \\
&= 18[\sin θ]_{π}^{3π/2} \\
&= 18[- 1 - 0] \\
&=-18
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total charge of the quarter-disk is $Q =-18$.
