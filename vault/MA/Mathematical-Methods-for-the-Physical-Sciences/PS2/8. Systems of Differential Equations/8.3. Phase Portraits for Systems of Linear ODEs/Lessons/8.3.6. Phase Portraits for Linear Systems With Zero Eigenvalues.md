## Introduction

In this topic, we'll study phase portraits of linear systems
$\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$
when the $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$ has at least one *zero eigenvalue*.

A zero eigenvalue is special because

$$
e^{0\cdot t}=1
$$

so the corresponding component of a solution has *no exponential growth or decay*. Geometrically, this often creates a **line of equilibrium points** (infinitely many fixed points), instead of a single equilibrium at the origin.

Indeed, equilibrium points satisfy

$$
\mathbf{x}'=\mathbf{0}
$$

so for

$$
\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}
$$

we solve

$$
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
$$

That is, equilibrium points form the *null space* of $A$.

- If $\det(A) \neq 0$, then the null space is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ and the only equilibrium is the origin.
- If $\det(A)=0$, then the null space contains infinitely many points (typically a line through the origin in $2\times2$ cases).

A zero eigenvalue guarantees $\det(A)=0$, so in that case the null space is nontrivial.

Let's see a concrete example.

---

<a id="finding-equilibrium-points-of-a-system"></a>
## Finding Equilibrium Points of a System

**Example:** ${x^{′}(t) = 2x(t) + 2y(t); y^{′}(t) = 5x(t) + 5y(t)$
Find an equation of the line in the phase space that contains all equilibrium points of the system above.

**Explanation**

Writing our system in matrix form, we have
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 2 \\ 5 & 5\end{bmatrix}$
where $\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$

The equilibrium (fixed) points of a system

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t) = \mathbf{f}(x,y)
$$

are the points for which

$$
\mathbf{f}(x,y)=\mathbf{0}
$$

So, we get the following system of linear equations:

$$
{2x + 2y = 0; 5x + 5y = 0
$$

Notice that the second equation is a multiple of the first one. So, we can disregard the second equation.

From the first equation, we get

$$
2x+2y=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2y=-2x \quad\Rightarrow\quad y=-x
$$

Therefore, the equilibrium points are given by
$\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$

This determines the line with the equation $y=-x$.

<a id="systems-with-one-zero-eigenvalue"></a>
## Systems With One Zero Eigenvalue

Suppose a $2 \times 2$ system has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=0$ and

$$
\lambda_2\neq 0
$$

with eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

Then, the eigenvector direction for $\lambda_1=0$ gives a **line of equilibria**:

$$
\mathbf{x}_E = c_1 \mathbf{v}_1, \qquad c_1 \in \mathbb{R}
$$

The other eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ determines what happens off the equilibrium line:

- If $\lambda_2>0$, trajectories move *away* from the equilibrium line (*unstable* direction).
- If $\lambda_2<0$, trajectories move *toward* the equilibrium line (*stable* direction).

A typical solution has the form

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)=c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\mathbf{v}_2, \qquad c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}
$$

So trajectories are *parallel to $\mathbf{v}_2$,* shifted by the equilibrium vector $c_1\mathbf{v}_1$.

To illustrate, let's consider the following example

$$
{x^{′}(t) = x(t) - 2y(t); y^{′}(t) =-x(t) + 2y(t)
$$

where we know the eigenvalues of the system's matrix are $\lambda_1=0$ and

$$
\lambda_2=3
$$

with corresponding eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ respectively.

Let's sketch the phase portrait of the system.

The general solution of the system is
$\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$

- First, notice that $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ are the equilibrium solutions of the system since $\mathbf{x}'_E(t) = \mathbf{0}$. These solutions form the line with the equation $y=\dfrac{1}{2}x$ in the phase space, as shown below.

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/d9a0cb6aea063e7d938b9218221c580a.png>)

- Now, the line that passes through the origin and is parallel to $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ has the equation
$y= -x$
and the straight-line solutions of the form
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
lie on that line. Since $e^{3t} \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$, these solutions approach infinity as $t \to \infty$.

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/797029119444de0f10795c0a00694204.png>)

All other non-equilibrium solutions are parallel to the vector $\mathbf{v}_2$. So, the phase portrait of our system looks as follows:

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/f8bc061c5ebd1d33bfc1892a1daad387.png>)

## Identifying the Phase Portrait of a System With One Zero Eigenvalue

Using this information, sketch the phase portrait of the system.

**Explanation**

The general solution of the system is
$\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$

- First, notice that $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ are the equilibrium solutions of the system since $\mathbf{x}'_E(t) = \mathbf{0}$. These solutions form the line with the equation $y=\dfrac13x$ in the phase space, as shown below.

$y=-\dfrac12x$
and the straight-line solutions of the form
$\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
lie on that line. Since $e^{-5t} \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$, these solutions approach the origin as $t \to \infty$.

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/29bacb4b190583c38b6f6c1e63da2895.png>)

<a id="systems-with-repeated-zero-eigenvalue"></a>
## Systems With Repeated Zero Eigenvalue

Now, suppose a $2 \times 2$ system has a repeated zero eigenvalue $\lambda=0$ (multiplicity $2$). Here, the vector field carries no exponential growth or decay in any direction.
There are two subcases.

- If there are two independent eigenvectors (we have a zero matrix), then every direction behaves like an equilibrium direction, and solutions are constant:
$\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}(0)$.
So *every point is an equilibrium*, and the phase portrait is a field of zero vectors.
- If there is only one eigenvector (we have a singular nonzero matrix), then we use a generalized eigenvector $\mathbf{w}$ with $(A-0\cdot I)\mathbf{w}=A\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}$, and solutions look like
$\mathbf{x}(t)=c_1\mathbf{v}+c_2\bigl(t\,\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\bigr), \qquad c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.
Notice that here$\mathbf{x}_E=c_1\mathbf{v}$ still gives a *line of equilibrium points*, whileother trajectories align with the eigenspace structure, yielding a **shear flow** where solutions grow linearly with $t$ (algebraic growth).

Let's see a concrete example of the second case.

---

<a id="identifying-the-phase-portrait-of-a-system-with-repeated-zero-eigenvalue"></a>
## Identifying the Phase Portrait of a System With Repeated Zero Eigenvalue

**Example:** ${x^{′}(t) =-3x(t) + 9y(t); y^{′}(t) =-x(t) + 3y(t)$
Consider the system of linear differential equations above. The eigenvector corresponding to the only eigenvalue $\lambda=0$ of the system's matrix $A$ is $\mathbf{v} = [3, \ 1]^T$. The vector $\mathbf{w} = [-1, \ 0]^T$ is such that $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{w}= \mathbf{v}$. Sketch the phase portrait of the system.

**Explanation**

Since we have a repeated eigenvalue $\lambda=0$ (of multiplicity two) and only one corresponding eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$, the solution of the system is given by

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = c_1\mathbf{v} e^{\lambda t} + c_2 (\mathbf{v} te^{\lambda t} + \mathbf{w}e^{\lambda t} \,)
$$

where $\mathbf{w}$ is a solution to the equation

$$
(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{w}= \mathbf{v}
$$

In our case,
$\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
Thus, the general solution of the system is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix} &= c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}(\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}t + \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}) \\
&= c_{1}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + c_{2}\begin{bmatrix}3t - 1 \\ t\end{bmatrix}, c_{1}, c_{2} ∈ R
\end{aligned}
$$

First, notice that $\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ are equilibrium solutions of the system since

$$
\mathbf{x}'_E(t) = \mathbf{0}
$$

These solutions form the line with the equation

$$
y=\dfrac13x
$$

in the phase space, as shown below.

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/1d5dfee4e3ecc9de1abd7b5c988c3572.png>)

Now, we consider some general solutions of the form
$\begin{bmatrix}x(t) \\ y(t)\end{bmatrix}$
For example:

- When $c_1=1$ and $c_2=1$, we get the following curve:
${x = 3t + 2; y = t + 1$
Let's add it to the phase space:

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/b2eab0b163b394484f17ad33df6c1d82.png>)

- When $c_1=-1$ and $c_2=-1$, we get the following curve:
${x =-3t - 2; y =-t - 1$
Let's add it to the phase space:

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/7555ac8c4917b8f00d3dddd94a3a9e7c.png>)

The phase portrait of our system looks as follows:

![](<../Source/8.3.6. Phase Portraits for Linear Systems With Zero Eigenvalues/Images/9c4709214d5c0674d33a227c8b36885c.png>)
