## Introduction

Suppose we have a differential equation of the form

$$
y ' = f (a x + b y)
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are real constants. In this case, we can rewrite the equation as a separable differential equation using the substitution

$$
z= ax+by
$$

We then proceed to solve this separable equation.

To see how we apply this method, let's consider the following example:

$$
y' = (x+y)^2
$$

First, we make the substitution

$$
z = x+y
$$

Differentiating $z(x)$ using the chain rule, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = 1 + \,\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} - 1
$$

Therefore, the original equation can be written in terms of $z$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dz}{dx} - 1 &= z^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Expressing this equation in the form

$$
z'(x) = g(z)
$$

gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = z^2+ 1
$$

Notice that this differential equation is separable. So, we separate the variables and integrate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{z^{2} + 1}\frac{dz}{dx} &= 1 \\
∫\frac{1}{z^{2} + 1}dz &= ∫dx \\
\arctan z &= x + C
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we write the general solution to the equation in terms of $x$ using our original substitution, as follows:

$$
\arctan {(x+y)} = x + C
$$

## Reducing a First-Order ODE to a Simpler Form Using a Linear Substitution

**Example:** Consider the differential equation
$\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \ln(x + y)$.
Using a substitution of the form $z= x + y$, transform the differential equation into an equation of the form $z'(x) = f(z)$. What is the function $f(z)$?

**Explanation**

First, we make the substitution

$$
z = x + y
$$

Differentiating $z(x)$ using the chain rule, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = 1 + \dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} - 1
$$

Therefore, the original equation can be written in terms of $z$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dz}{dx} - 1 &= \ln z
\end{aligned}
$$

Expressing this equation in the form

$$
z'(x) = f(z)
$$

we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = \ln z+1
$$

Therefore,

$$
f(z) = \ln z + 1
$$

## Solving a First-Order ODE Using a Linear Substitution

**Example:** Consider the differential equation
$\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = x + y$.
The general solution to this equation can be expressed in the form
$g(x,y) = x+C$,
where $C$ is a constant. What is $g(x,y)$?

**Explanation**

First, we make the substitution

$$
z = x+y
$$

Differentiating $z(x)$ using the chain rule, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = 1 + \,\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} - 1
$$

Therefore, the original equation can be written in terms of $z$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dz}{dx} - 1 &= z
\end{aligned}
$$

Expressing this equation in the form

$$
z'(x) = f(z)
$$

gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}z}{\textrm{d}x} = z+1
$$

This differential equation is separable. So, we separate the variables and integrate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{z + 1}\frac{dz}{dx} &= 1 \\
∫\frac{1}{z + 1}dz &= ∫dx \\
\ln \mid z + 1 \mid &= x + C
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we write the equation in terms of $y$ and $x$ using our original substitution, as follows:

$$
\ln{\mid x+y+1 \mid} = x + C
$$

Therefore,

$$
g(x,y) = \ln{\mid x+y+1 \mid}
$$
