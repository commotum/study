## Introduction

A **first-order linear ordinary differential equation** is a differential equation in the variable $y(x)$ that can be expressed in the **general form**

$$
a(x)y'(x)+b(x)y(x)=f(x)
$$

where

- $a(x)$, $b(x)$, and $f(x)$ are functions of $x$ only, and
- $a(x)$ is not identically zero (i.e., it is not zero for all $x$).

For example, the differential equation

$$
xy' + e^x y=1
$$

is a first-order linear ODE with

$$
a(x) = x, b(x) = e^x
$$

and

$$
f(x) = 1
$$

On the other hand, the differential equation

$$
x(y')^2 + e^x \sin(y)=1
$$

is *not* a first-order linear ODE. Notice that it contains the terms $(y')^2$ and $\sin(y)$, and therefore cannot be presented in the general form.

---

<a id="the-standard-form-of-a-first-order-linear-ode"></a>
## The Standard Form of a First-Order Linear ODE

The general form of a first-order linear ODE is

$$
a(x)y'+b(x)y=f(x)
$$

Now, if we take the general form and divide both sides by $a(x)$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a(x))/(a(x))y^{′} + (b(x))/(a(x))y &= (f(x))/(a(x)) \\
y^{′} + (b(x))/(a(x))y &= (f(x))/(a(x))
\end{aligned}
$$

Remember that $a(x)$ is not identically zero, so we're allowed to perform this division.

Now, if we define the functions $P$ and $Q$ as

$$
P(x)=\dfrac{b(x)}{a(x)}, \qquad Q(x)=\dfrac{f(x)}{a(x)}
$$

then we can write our equation in the following **standard form**:

$$
y'+P(x)y =Q(x)
$$

For example, given the first-order linear ODE

$$
y' \sin x+ y e^x= \cos x
$$

the standard form of this equation can be found by dividing through by $\sin x$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^{′} + y(\frac{e^{x}}{\sin x}) &= \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \\
y^{′} + ye^{x}\csc x &= \cot x
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, we have

$$
P(x) = e^x \csc x, \qquad Q(x) = \cot x
$$

<a id="writing-first-order-linear-odes-in-standard-form"></a>
## Writing First-Order Linear ODEs in Standard Form

**Example:** Write the following first-order linear ODE in standard form.
$\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + y\sin x - \cos x =0$

**Explanation**

A first-order linear ODE is in standard form if it is written as

$$
\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

Converting the given equation to standard form, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dx} + y\sin x - \cos x &= 0 \\
\frac{dy}{dx} + y\sin x &= \cos x \\
\frac{dy}{dx} + (\sin x)_(⏟)_(P(x))y &= \cos x_(⏟)_(Q(x))
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, the equation is written in standard form with

$$
P(x)=\sin x
$$

and

$$
Q(x) = \cos x
$$

<a id="identifying-first-order-linear-odes"></a>
## Identifying First-Order Linear ODEs

**Example:** Determine whether the following equation is a first-order linear ODE:

$x^2\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + \sin(2x)y = e^x$.

**Explanation**

We attempt to write the equation in the standard form

$$
\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x) \,
$$

Dividing the equation through by $x^2$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} + \sin (2x)y &= e^{x} \\
\frac{dy}{dx} + (\sin (2x))/(x^{2})y &= \frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can see that this equation is now in standard form with

$$
P(x) = \dfrac{\sin{2x}}{x^2}
$$

and

$$
Q(x) = \dfrac{e^x}{x^2}
$$

Because the equation can be written in standard form, we conclude that it is a first-order linear ODE.

## Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous Equations

Recall that the standard form of a first-order linear differential equation is

$$
y' + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

Note that $Q(x)$ is called the **forcing function.**

A first-order linear ODE is **homogeneous** if the forcing function $Q(x) = 0$ for all $x$. In other words, the equation is homogeneous if it can be written in the form

$$
y' + P(x)y = 0
$$

A first-order linear ODE is **inhomogeneous** if it is not homogeneous.

Let's consider some examples:

- Suppose we have the equation
$y' - xy = 0$.
The equation matches the form $y' + P(x)y = 0$ with $P(x) = -x$. Therefore, this equation *is* homogeneous.
- Now, consider the equation
$y' - xy = 6$.
The presence of the constant term $Q(x) = 6$ means that the equation cannot be written in the form $y' + P(x)y = 0$. Therefore, this equation is *not* homogeneous (i.e., it is *inhomogeneous*).
- Finally, we consider the equation
$y' + xy = 2y$.
We move the term involving $y$ to the left-hand side so that all $y$-terms are together:
$y^{′} + xy|= 2y; y^{′} + xy - 2y|= 0; y^{′} + (x - 2)y|= 0$
This matches the homogeneous form $y' + P(x)y = 0$ with $P(x) = (x-2)$. Therefore, this equation *is* homogeneous.

<a id="identifying-homogeneous-and-inhomogeneous-odes"></a>
## Identifying Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous ODEs

**Example:** Which of the following first-order linear differential equations are inhomogeneous?

1. $y' + x^3 y = 0$
2. $y' + x^3 y = x^4$
3. $y' + x^3 y = y$

**Explanation**

A first-order linear differential equation is homogeneous if it can be written in the form

$$
y' + P(x)y = 0
$$

A first-order linear differential equation is inhomogeneous if it is not homogeneous, i.e., if it can be written as

$$
y' + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

where $Q$ is not identical to zero.

To determine which equations are inhomogeneous, we examine each one in turn.

- First, we consider the equation
$y' + x^3 y = 0$.
Notice that this has the form $y' + P(x)y = 0$. Therefore, this equation is homogeneous.
- Next, we consider the equation
$y' + x^3 y = x^4$.
Notice that this is written in the form $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ where $Q(x) = x^4$ is not identically zero. Therefore, this equation is inhomogeneous.
- Finally, we consider the equation
$y' + x^3 y = y$.
We collect the $y$-terms on the left side:
$y^{′} + x^{3}y|= y; y^{′} + x^{3}y - y|= 0; y^{′} + (x^{3} - 1)y|= 0$.
Since our equation can be written in the form $y' + P(x)y = 0$, the equation is homogeneous.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."
