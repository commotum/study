## Introduction

Recall that a first-order linear ODE can always be expressed in the standard form

$$
y'(x)+P(x)y(x) =Q(x)
$$

First-order linear ODEs are special because we can always find the general solution using an **integrating factor.**

An integrating factor is a function $I(x)$ that allows us to rewrite the left-hand side as an exact derivative after multiplying the equation by $I(x)$. The resulting equation can then be solved by integrating both sides.

To demonstrate, consider the following first-order linear ODE.

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + \frac{2}{x}\, y = 3
$$

As it stands, this differential equation cannot be integrated directly. However, if we multiply both sides of the equation by $I(x) = x^2$, we get

$$
x^2\cdot \frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + x^2\cdot \frac{2}{x}\, y = x^2\cdot 3
$$

which simplifies as follows:

$$
x^2 \frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + 2 x y = 3x^2 \qquad (\ast)
$$

Now, here's the neat part. Provided our integrating factor is correct, we can *always* write the left-hand side of the equation as the exact derivative

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d}{\textrm d x}\left(y\cdot I(x)\right)
$$

which in our case is

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d}{\textrm d x}\left(y \cdot x^2\right)
$$

So, our differential equation is

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}(y \cdot x^2) = 3x^2
$$

We can now find the general solution by integrating both sides with respect to $x{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(y \cdot x^{2}) &= 3x^{2} \\
∫\frac{d}{dx}(y \cdot x^{2})dx &= ∫3x^{2}dx \\
y \cdot x^{2} &= x^{3} + C \\
&= y = x + C x 2
\end{aligned}
$$

We can check that

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}(y \cdot x^2) = 3x^2
$$

is indeed equivalent to the original equation $(\ast)$ using the product rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(y \cdot x^{2}) &= 3x^{2} \\
\frac{d}{dx}(y) \cdot x^{2} + y \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^{2}) &= 3x^{2} \\
x^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} + y \cdot 2x &= 3x^{2} \\
x^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} + 2xy &= 3x^{2}✓
\end{aligned}
$$

By finding an appropriate integrating factor, we can solve any first-order linear ODE! So the question is, how do we find an integrating factor? Let's discuss this next.

---

<a id="the-integrating-factor-method"></a>
## The Integrating Factor Method

Given a first-order linear ODE, we can find an integrating factor and use it to solve the equation using the following procedure.

- **Step 1**: Write the equation in standard form:
$\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)$
- **Step 2**: Compute an integrating factor $I(x)$ using the definition
$I(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,\textrm{d}x} \,$.
- **Step 3**: Multiply both sides of the equation found in step 1 by $I(x)$:
$I(x)\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + I(x)P(x)y = I(x)Q(x)$
- **Step 4**: Rewrite the left side as
$\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\Bigl(y I(x)\Bigr)$
If steps 1 through 3 are done correctly, then we can always do this, and we can check that it's correct by differentiating. Our new equation is
$\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\Bigl(y I(x)\Bigr) = I(x)Q(x)$
- **Step 5**: Integrate both sides of the equation with respect to $x$ and solve for $y$:
$∫\frac{d}{dx}(yI(x))dx|= ∫I(x)Q(x)dx; yI(x)|= ∫I(x)Q(x)dx + C; y|= (1)/(I(x))(∫I(x)Q(x)dx + C)$

You might be wondering where the formula for $I(x)$ comes from; we'll discuss this at the end of the lesson. First, let's apply this method to a concrete example in the next slide.

## Transforming First-Order Linear ODEs Using Integrating Factors

**Example:** Consider the following first-order linear ODE for the function $y = y(x)$:
$\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + 2y = 2e^{-2x}$
Write the equation in the form $\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(y I(x) \right) = I(x)Q(x)$, where $I(x)$ is an integrating factor.

**Explanation**

This equation is in the standard form

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

with

$$
P(x) = 2
$$

and

$$
Q(x) = 2e^{-2x}
$$

We proceed to find an integrating factor $I(x)$ using the definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(x) &= e^{∫P(x)dx} \\
&= e^{∫2dx} \\
&= e^{2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we multiply our differential equation through by

$$
I(x) = e^{2x}
$$

as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{2x} \cdot (\frac{dy}{dx} + 2y) &= e^{2x} \cdot 2e^{-2x} \\
e^{2x} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} + 2e^{2x} \cdot y &= e^{2x} \cdot 2e^{-2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we rewrite the left hand side as

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(y I(x)\right){:}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(ye^{2x}) &= e^{2x} \cdot 2e^{-2x}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Solving First-Order Linear ODEs Using Integrating Factors

**Example:** Find the general solution of the following differential equation using the method of integrating factors.
$\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + \dfrac {3} {x} y = \dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}, \qquad x \gt 0$.

**Explanation**

This equation is in the standard form

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

with

$$
P(x) = \dfrac{3}{x}
$$

and

$$
Q(x) = \dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}
$$

We proceed to find an integrating factor $I(x)$ using the definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(x) &= e^{∫P(x)dx} \\
&= e^{∫(3/x)dx} \\
&= e^{3\ln x} \\
&= e^{\ln (x^{3})} \\
&= x 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we multiply our differential equation through by $I(x) = x^3$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{3} \cdot (\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{3}{x}y) &= x^{3} \cdot \frac{\cos x}{x^{2}} \\
x^{3} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} + 3x^{2} \cdot y &= x\cos x
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we rewrite the left hand side as

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(y\cdot I(x)\right)
$$

and carry out the integration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(yx^{3}) &= x\cos x \\
∫\frac{d}{dx}(yx^{3})dx &= ∫x\cos xdx \\
yx^{3} &= ∫x\cos xdx
\end{aligned}
$$

To solve this integral, we integrate by parts.

$$
\int uv'\,\textrm d x = uv - \int u' v\,\textrm d x
$$

Let

$$
u = x, u' = 1, v' = \cos x
$$

and $v = \sin x$. Then, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
yx^{3} &= ∫x\cos xdx \\
yx^{3} &= x\sin x - ∫\sin xdx \\
yx^{3} &= x\sin x + \cos x + C \\
y &= x sin ⁡ x + cos ⁡ x x 3 + C x 3
\end{aligned}
$$

## Solving First-Order Initial Value Problems Using Integrating Factors

**Example:** Solve the following initial value problem using the method of integrating factors.
$\dfrac {\textrm{d}y} {\textrm{d}x} + 3x^2y = 9x^2, \qquad y(0) = 1$

**Explanation**

This equation is in the standard form

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

with

$$
P(x) = 3x^2
$$

and

$$
Q(x) = 9x^2
$$

We compute an integrating factor using the definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(x) &= e^{∫P(x)dx} \\
&= e^{∫3x^{2}dx} \\
&= e^{x^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying both sides of the equation in standard form by

$$
I(x) = e^{x^3}
$$

yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{x^{3}} \cdot (\frac{dy}{dx} + 3x^{2}y) &= e^{x^{3}} \cdot 9x^{2} \\
e^{x^{3}} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} + 3x^{2}e^{x^{3}} \cdot y &= 9x^{2}e^{x^{3}} \\
\frac{d}{dx}(ye^{x^{3}}) &= 9x^{2}e^{x^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we integrate with respect to $x$, making use of the substitution $u = x^3{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{d}{dx}(ye^{x^{3}})dx &= ∫9x^{2}e^{x^{3}}dx \\
ye^{x^{3}} &= ∫3e^{u}du \\
ye^{x^{3}} &= 3e^{u} + C \\
ye^{x^{3}} &= 3e^{x^{3}} + C \\
y &= 3 + Ce^{-x^{3}}
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the general solution. To find the value of $C$ corresponding to the specific solution, we apply the initial condition $y(0) = 1$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y(0) &= 3 + Ce^{-0^{3}} \\
1 &= 3 + C \\
C &= -2
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the solution to the initial value problem is

$$
y = 3 - 2e^{-x^3}
$$

<a id="deriving-the-integrating-factor"></a>
## Deriving the Integrating Factor

We will now derive the formula for the integrating factor.

Consider the first-order linear equation in standard form

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + P(x)y = Q(x)
$$

Our goal is to rewrite the left-hand side as the derivative of a product.

To do that, we multiply both sides of the equation by some function $I(x)$ that we wish to determine.

$$
{\color{blue}I(x)\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + {\color{purple}I(x)P(x)}y} = I(x)Q(x)
$$

We want the left-hand side to match the derivative of $y\cdot I(x)$. But by the product rule,

$$
\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\Bigl(y\cdot I(x)\Bigr) = {\color{blue}I(x)\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}x} + {\color{purple}I'(x)}y}
$$

So, if we choose $I(x)$ so that

$$
I'(x) = I(x)P(x)
$$

then the left-hand side becomes

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\Bigl(y\cdot I(x)\Bigr)
$$

Dividing both sides of

$$
I'(x) = I(x)P(x)
$$

by $I(x)$ gives

$$
\frac{I'(x)}{I(x)} = P(x)
$$

Next, integrating both sides with respect to $x$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫(I^{′}(x))/(I(x))dx &= ∫P(x)dx \\
∫\frac{d}{dx}(\ln (I(x)))dx &= ∫P(x)dx \\
\ln (I(x)) &= ∫P(x)dx + C \\
I(x) &= e^{∫P(x)dx + C} \\
&= e^{C} \cdot e^{∫P(x)dx}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, because multiplying $I(x)$ by a nonzero constant does not change the method, we can choose $e^C = 1$ and define an integrating factor as

$$
I(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,\textrm{d}x}
$$
