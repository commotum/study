## Introduction

We know how to define the double integral of a function over a rectangular domain. Now, we'll learn how to define a double integral over a non-rectangular region.

Let's consider the non-rectangular region $D$ enclosed by the circle

$$
x^2+y^2=1
$$

in the first quadrant, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/da4c46a5901ac362a413078cffa326fd.png>)

We will define the following double integral over this non-rectangular region:
$\iint\limits_{D} (3-x^2-y^2) \, \textrm{d}A$

To do this, we will use the following trick. Let's enclose the region $D$ by a rectangle $R$ with sides parallel to the coordinates axes, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/6dfc82221f7753e2325902813d7f04da.png>)

Now, we extend our function to $R$ by setting a new function $f(x,y)$ that's equal to $0$ outside $D$ and equals $3-x^2-y^2$ on $D$:

$$
{3 - x^{2} - y^{2},\begin{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D
$$

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/afafca53a3a338cae729d628fea0b1db.png>)

Although $f(x,y)$ might not be continuous at the boundary of $D$, the double integral of $f(x,y)$ over the rectangular domain $R$ will exist.

Therefore, we define our double integral of $3-x^2-y^2$ over $D$ as

$$
\iint\limits_D (3-x^2-y^2) \, \textrm{d}A = \iint\limits_R f(x,y) \, \textrm{d}A
$$

Geometrically, the value of the double integral will give us the volume of the solid bounded by the surface $z=3-x^2-y^2$ over the quarter-circular domain $D$.

**Watch out!** The domain $R$ must not necessarily be the smallest possible rectangle that covers the region $D$. It can be any rectangular domain that covers $D$ completely.

## Determining a Valid Region to Define a Double Integral Over a Non-Rectangular Domain

**Example:** Consider the region $D$ shown above. If
${x^{2} - y^{2},\begin{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D$
which of the following could be the definition of the rectangular domain $R$?

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/f62ea4c35fcc7d18eac85d6d253ef727.png>)

1. $R=[-2,2] \times [-2,1]$
2. $R=[-2,1] \times [-2,2]$
3. $R=[-1,1] \times [-1,1]$

**Explanation**

Notice that if $R$ covers $D$ completely and we define

$$
{x^{2} - y^{2},\begin{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D
$$

then, we obtain

$$
\iint\limits_D (x^2-y^2) \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_R f(x,y) \: \text{d}A
$$

With that in mind, let's examine each of the given rectangles.

- Rectangles I and II cover the region $D$ completely.
- Rectangle III does not cover the region $D$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "I and II only."

## Identifying True Statements About Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains

**Example:** Let $D$ be a finite region enclosed by the curve $x^2+y^2=9$. If
${\cos (x - y),\begin{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D$,
which of the following statements are true?

1. $\displaystyle \iint\limits_{\large R} \cos(x-y) \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_{\large D} f(x,y) \: \text{d}A,\quad$ where $R=[-4,4] \times [-4,4]$
2. $\displaystyle \iint\limits_{\large D} \cos(x-y) \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_{\large R} f(x,y) \: \text{d}A,\quad$ where $R=[-2,2] \times [-2,2]$
3. $\displaystyle \iint\limits_{\large D} \cos(x-y) \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_{\large R} f(x,y) \: \text{d}A,\quad$ where $R=[-3,3] \times [-3,3]$

**Explanation**

First, let's draw our region $D$:

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/d90282a665fd8f2c55197c22b38a931a.png>)

Notice that if $R$ covers $D$ completely and we define

$$
{\cos (x - y),\begin{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D
$$

then, we obtain

$$
\iint\limits_{\large D} \cos(x-y) \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_{\large R} f(x,y) \: \text{d}A
$$

With that in mind, let's examine each of the given statements.

- Statement I is false. The integrals have not been stated correctly with respect to the domains $R$ and $D$ (they have been swapped).
- Statement II is false. The rectangle $R$ does not cover the region $D$.
- Statement III is true. The rectangle $R$ covers the region $D$ completely.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."

## Determining a Correct Function Used to Define a Double Integral Over a Non-Rectangular Domain

**Example:** Consider the region $D$ shown above. If $R=[-1,1] \times [0,1]$, and
$\iint\limits_D \mid x-y \mid \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_R f(x,y) \: \text{d}A$,
which of the following could be the definition of $f(x,y)$?

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/8e46da282ec5e52c3797796b84fba298.png>)

1. ${\begin{vmatrix}x - y &,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∖D; 0, \mid (x, y) ∈ D∖R$
2. ${0, \mid (x, y) ∈ R∖D;\begin{vmatrix}x - y &,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ D$
3. ${\begin{vmatrix}x - y &,\end{vmatrix}(x, y) ∈ R∩D; 0, \mid (x, y) ∉ D$

**Explanation**

First, let's draw our region $D$ and rectangle $R$:

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/8. Multiple Integrals/8.1. Double Integrals/Source/8.1.2. Double Integrals Over Non-Rectangular Domains/Images/f75faf2d7defb332a3de4e495f71609f.png>)

Notice that the rectangle $R$ contains the region $D$ completely.

Now, if we define $f(x,y)$ to be equal $\mid x-y \mid$ on $D$ and to be equal $0$ outside $D$, we obtain

$$
\iint\limits_D \mid x-y \mid \: \text{d}A = \iint\limits_R f(x,y) \: \text{d}A
$$

With that in mind, let's examine each of the given functions.

- We cannot use function I. This functions are not identically zero for all points in $R\,\backslash D$. For example, $(x,y) = (1,0) \in R\,\backslash\, D$, but the function is nonzero at this point.
- Functions II and III equal $\mid x-y \mid$ on $D$ and equal $0$ outside $D$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II and III only."
