## Introduction

Given two vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$, the set of all possible linear combinations of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ is

$$
U = \left\{x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 \:: \: x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R} \right\}
$$

The set $U$ is also called the **linear span** of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$, and is denoted by $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$. We say that $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ are the **generators** of $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$.

For example, let's find the linear span generated by the following vectors:
$\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

From the definition of linear span, we know that if $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$, then $\mathbf{b}$ must take the form

$$
\mathbf{b} = x_1\mathbf{a_1} + x_2\mathbf{a_2}
$$

for some $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb{R}$. So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= x_{1}a_{1} + x_{2}a_{2} \\
&= x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-x_{1} \\ x_{1} \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3x_{2} \\ 2x_{2} \\ -x_{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-x_{1} + 3x_{2} \\ x_{1} + 2x_{2} \\ -x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the linear span of $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$ is
$\begin{bmatrix}-x_{1} + 3x_{2} \\ x_{1} + 2x_{2} \\ -x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$

Geometrically, $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$ can be viewed as the plane $\Pi$ that passes through the origin and is parallel to the vectors $\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/3. Vector Spaces/3.1. Vectors in N-Dimensional Space/Source/3.1.3. Linear Span of Vectors in N-Dimensional Euclidean Space/Images/d3f235df369da8fd14146c5c7f90942a.png>)

## Determining Whether a Vector Lies in the Span of Given Vectors

**Example:** Determine if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We know that $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$ if there exist $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}a_{1} + x_{2}a_{2} &= b \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 3 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 2 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
{x_{1} + 5x_{2} = 8; x_{1} + 3x_{2} = 2; 2x_{1} + 2x_{2} =-5
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & -5\end{bmatrix} & R_{2} &:=R_{2} - R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & -2 & -6 \\ 2 & 2 & -5\end{bmatrix} & R_{3} &:=R_{3} - 2R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & -2 & -6 \\ 0 & -8 & -21\end{bmatrix} & R_{2} &:=-\frac{1}{2}R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & -8 & -21\end{bmatrix} & R_{3} &:=R_{3} + 8R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 5 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} & R_{2} & ↔R_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

The third row of the augmented matrix now corresponds to the equation

$$
0x_1 + 0x_2 = 3
$$

which has no solution.

So, there are no values of $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}a_{1} + x_{2}a_{2} &= b \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ -5 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we conclude that $\mathbf{b}$ does *not* lie in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$.

## Finding the Value of an Unknown Such That a Vector Lies in the Span of Given Vectors

**Example:** Find the values of $k$ such that the vector $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 5 \\ 7\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We know that $\mathbf{b} \in \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2} \}$ if there exist $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}a_{1} + x_{2}a_{2} &= b \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 5 \\ 7\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 4 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}13 \\ -5 \\ k\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
{x_{1} - 2x_{2} = 13; 5x_{1} + 4x_{2} =-5; 7x_{1} + 2x_{2} = k
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 5 & 4 & -5 \\ 7 & 2 & k\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2} &:=R_{2} - 5R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 14 & -70 \\ 7 & 2 & k\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} - 7R_{1}; ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 14 & -70 \\ 0 & 16 & k - 91\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2} &:=\frac{1}{14}R_{2} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 1 & -5 \\ 0 & 16 & k - 91\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} - 16R_{2}; ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 13 \\ 0 & 1 & -5 \\ 0 & 0 & k - 11\end{bmatrix}
$$

The third row of the augmented matrix now corresponds to the equation

$$
0x_1 + 0x_2 = k-11
$$

. Therefore, the system has a solution only if

$$
0 = k-11 \quad \Rightarrow \quad k=11
$$

## Geometric Interpretation of the Linear Span

The linear span of vectors can be interpreted geometrically as the points in space that can be reached by adding multiples of those vectors.

- The span of a single nonzero vector is a *line* through the origin. More precisely, if $\mathbf{a} \neq \mathbf{0}$, then $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a} \}$ determines the *line* with direction vector $\mathbf{a}$ that passes through the origin. For instance, if
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$
then $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a} \}$ represents the line $l$ *spanned* by $\mathbf{a}$:

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/3. Vector Spaces/3.1. Vectors in N-Dimensional Space/Source/3.1.3. Linear Span of Vectors in N-Dimensional Euclidean Space/Images/e4d8f0b95ef99c59e394dd6d15aca43c.png>)

- The span of two non-parallel vectors is a *plane* through the origin. More precisely, if $\mathbf{a} \not\parallel \mathbf{b}$ ($\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are not parallel), then $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \}$ determines the *plane* with direction vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ that passes through the origin. For instance, if
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$
then $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \}$ represents the plane $\Pi$ *spanned* by $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$:

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/3. Vector Spaces/3.1. Vectors in N-Dimensional Space/Source/3.1.3. Linear Span of Vectors in N-Dimensional Euclidean Space/Images/28e2bc61c16f485fee52121c7189bf94.png>)

## Determining Whether a Vector Lies on a Plane

**Example:** Determine if the vector $\mathbf{u}$ lies on the plane $\;\Pi \,:\, \mathbf{r}=s \mathbf{b} + t \mathbf{c}, \, t,s \in \mathbb{R}$, where $\mathbf{b}=\langle 1, 4, 5 \rangle, \mathbf{c} = \langle 2, 4, 6 \rangle$ and $\mathbf{u}= \langle 2, 10, 12 \rangle$.

**Explanation**

By definition, $\mathbf{u} \in \Pi$ if there exist $t$ and $s$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
sb + tc &= u \\
s\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 4 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 10 \\ 12\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have the following system of equations:

$$
{s + 2t = 2; 4s + 4t = 10; 5s + 6t = 12
$$

To solve the system above, we set up and row reduce an augmented matrix:

$$
∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 4 & 4 & 10 \\ 5 & 6 & 12\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2} &:=R_{2} - 4R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 5 & 6 & 12\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} - 5R_{1}; ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{3} \mid:=R_{3} - R_{2}; ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -4 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, we have the system

$$
{s + 2t = 2; - 4t = 2; 0 = 0
$$

From the second equation, we get

$$
-4t = 2 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad t = -\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

Substituting into the first equation, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
s + 2t &= 2 \\
s + 2(-\frac{1}{2}) &= 2 \\
s - 1 &= 2 \\
s &= 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Since we found a solution

$$
s=3, \, t=-\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

we conclude that $\mathbf{u}$ does indeed lie on the plane $\Pi$. In fact, we have

$$
\mathbf{u}=3\mathbf{b} - \dfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{c}
$$
