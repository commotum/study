## Introduction

Given a subspace $H$ with a basis

$$
\mathcal B = \{\mathbf b_1, \mathbf b_2 \}
$$

and a vector $\mathbf x \in H$, the **coordinates of the vector** $\mathbf{x}$ **relative to the basis** $\mathcal{B}$ are the coefficients ${\color{red} x_1}$ and ${\color{blue} x_2}$ such that

$$
{\color{red}x_1} \mathbf{b}_1+{\color{blue}x_2} \mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{x}
$$

We can write the vector $\mathbf x$ relative to the basis $\mathcal B$ using these coordinates, as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

To demonstrate, consider the basis

$$
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2 \}
$$

where

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Also let

$$
H = \textrm{span} \{\mathbf b_1, \mathbf b_2 \}
$$

and consider the vector $\begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

The vector $\mathbf x$ can be expressed as a unique linear combination of $\mathbf b_1$ and $\mathbf b_2$. That is to say, there exist *unique coefficients* $\color{red}x_1$ and $\color{blue}x_2$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}b_{1} + x_{2}b_{2} &= x \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's solve for these coefficients. From the above, we get the following system:

$$
{2x_{1} + x_{2} = 8; 2x_{2} = 4
$$

The unique solution of this system is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, we have that

$$
\mathbf{x} = {\color{red}3} \mathbf{b}_1+ {\color{blue}2} \mathbf{b}_2
$$

which can be visualized as shown below.

## Finding a Vector Given Its Coordinates Relative To a Given Basis

**Example:** Find the vector $\mathbf{x}$ if $\,\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2 \}$ is a basis of $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2 \}$, and
$\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We know that any vector in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2 \}$ has the form

$$
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

where $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the coordinates relative to the basis $\,\mathcal{B}$. Since we are told that $\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}$ we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= (-1)_(⏟)_(x_{1})\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + (3)_(⏟)_(x_{2})\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 4 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 0 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 4 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Remember, the order of the vectors in a basis is important.

Notice that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2 \\ -5\end{bmatrix}
$$

which means that

$$
\mathbf{x} = (-5) \cdot \mathbf{b}_1 + 2 \cdot \mathbf{b}_2
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Finding the Coordinates of a Vector Relative To a Given Basis Containing Two Vectors

**Example:** Find the coordinates of the vector $\mathbf{x}$ relative to the basis $\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2 \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We need to find $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}b_{1} + x_{2}b_{2} &= x \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 3 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ 5 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}-5 \\ -11 \\ -8\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The equation above gives the system with the augmented matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -5 \\ 3 & 5 & -11 \\ 4 & 0 & -8\end{bmatrix}$

Row-reducing the matrix using Gaussian elimination, we obtain the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -5 \\ 3 & 5 & -11 \\ 4 & 0 & -8\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2} &:=R_{2} + (-3)R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -5 \\ 0 & -4 & 4 \\ 4 & 0 & -8\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} + (-4)R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -5 \\ 0 & -4 & 4 \\ 0 & -12 & 12\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{3} \mid:=R_{3} + (-3)R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & -5 \\ 0 & -4 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The augmented matrix above corresponds to the following system of equations:

$$
{x_{1} + 3x_{2} =-5; - 4x_{2} = 4
$$

From the second equation, we get $x_2 =-1$. Substituting this into the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1 + 3 \left(-1 \right) = -5 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad x_1 = -2
$$

Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$

## Finding the Coordinates of a Vector Relative To a Given Basis Containing More Than Two Vectors

**Example:** Find the coordinates of the vector $\mathbf{x}$ relative to the basis $\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \mathbf{b}_3 \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

We need to find $x_1$, $x_2$, and $x_3$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}b_{1} + x_{2}b_{2} + x_{3}b_{3} &= x \\
x_{1}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} + x_{2}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -9 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} + x_{3}\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 7 \\ 6\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing the above system as an augmented matrix $M$ and reducing using Gaussian elimination, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -9 & -1 & 7 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 6\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{3} &:=R_{3} + (-1)R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -9 & -1 & 7 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 & 5\end{bmatrix} & R_{3}\end{vmatrix}:=R_{3} + (-\frac{1}{3})R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -9 & -1 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{4}{3} & \frac{8}{3}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The system is now

$$
{x_{1} + 3x_{2} = 1; - 9x_{2} - x_{3} = 7; \frac{4}{3}x_{3} = \frac{8}{3}
$$

From the third equation, we get $x_3 =2$. Substituting this into the second equation, we obtain

$$
-9x_2 - 2 = 7 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x_2 = -1
$$

and substituting $x_2=-1$ into the first equation, we get

$$
x_1+3(-1) = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\quad x_1 = 4
$$

Therefore, $\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

## The Standard Basis

Let's consider a set of vectors
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

Notice that the set $\mathcal{S}$ is linearly independent since the vector equation

$$
x_1\mathbf{e}_1+x_2\mathbf{e}_2+x_3\mathbf{e}_3=\mathbf{0}
$$

leads to the system

$$
{x_{1} = 0; x_{2} = 0; x_{3} = 0
$$

which has only the zero solution.

Also, notice that any vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be written as a linear combination of $\mathbf{e}_1$, $\mathbf{e}_2$, and $\mathbf{e}_3$. For instance,
$\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 0 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$

In particular, the equation above tells us that
$\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 0 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$
that is to say, the numbers $\color{red}-2$, $\color{red}0$, $\color{red}5$ are the coordinates of $\mathbf{v}$ relative to the basis $\mathcal{S}$.

The basis $\mathcal{S}$ is somewhat special, since the coordinates of a vector relative to this basis coincide with the components of the vector. We refer to $\mathcal{S}$ as the **standard basis**. Remember that in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ we even have special notations for the vectors of the standard basis:
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/3. Vector Spaces/3.3. Bases of N-Dimensional Space/Source/3.3.4. Expressing the Coordinates of a Vector in a Given Basis/Images/ecb33ab122ba13aaf1695b4f48701cdb.png>)

In general, the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ ⋮ \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
$$
