## Introduction

The characteristic equation of a square matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=0
$$

On the left-hand side of this equation, we have the expression $\det(A-\lambda I)$, which we call the **characteristic polynomial** of the matrix $A$.

For instance, let's find the characteristic polynomial of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}-8 & -9 \\ 6 & 7\end{bmatrix}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(λ) &= det(C - λI) \\
&=-8 - λ\begin{vmatrix}-9 \\ 6\end{vmatrix}7 - λ \\
&= (-8 - λ) \cdot (7 - λ) - (-9) \cdot 6 \\
&= λ^{2} + λ - 2
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-characteristic-polynomial-for-2x2-matrices"></a>
## The Characteristic Polynomial for 2x2 Matrices

What conclusions can we make regarding the characteristic polynomial of the general $2\times 2$ matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}
$$

First, let's find the characteristic equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
a - λ\begin{vmatrix}b \\ c\end{vmatrix}d - λ &= 0 \\
(a - λ)(d - λ) - bc &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - (a + d)λ + (ad - bc) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the constant term is the determinant of the matrix:

$$
\det(A) = ad-bc
$$

The coefficient of $\lambda$ is the sum of the diagonal entries of $A$ taken with a negative sign. The sum of the diagonal entries of a matrix $A$ is called the **trace** of $A$, and we denote it as

$$
\mathrm{tr}(A) = a+d
$$

So, for any $2\times 2$ matrix $A$, the characteristic polynomial is

$$
p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 -\mathrm{tr}(A)\lambda + \det(A)
$$

**Note:** This result can be extended to the general case of $n \times n$ matrices. If $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, then its characteristic polynomial takes the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(λ) &= det(A - λI) \\
&= (-1)^{n}λ^{n} + c_{n - 1}λ^{n - 1} + ⋯ + c_{1}λ + c_{0} \\
&= (-1)^{n}λ^{n} + (-1)^{n - 1}tr(A)_(⏟)_(c_{n - 1})λ^{n - 1} + ⋯ + c_{1}λ + det(A)_(⏟)_(c_{0})
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
c_{n - 1} = (-1)^{n - 1}tr(A)andc_{0} = det(A)
$$

<a id="calculating-the-sum-and-product-of-eigenvalues-of-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## Calculating the Sum and Product of Eigenvalues of a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Given that $\begin{bmatrix}9 & 1 \\ 2 & -6\end{bmatrix}$ find the sum of the eigenvalues of $A$.

**Explanation**

The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(λ) &= λ^{2} - tr(A)λ + det(A)
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, using Vieta's formulas, we have that the roots of $p(\lambda)$, i.e., the eigenvalues of $A$, satisfy

$$
{λ_{1} + λ_{2} = tr(A); λ_{1} \cdot λ_{2} = det(A)
$$

So, the sum of the eigenvalues of $A$ is equal to the trace of $A$.

Therefore,

$$
\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = \textrm{tr}(A)= 9+(-6)=3
$$

<a id="the-cayley-hamilton-theorem"></a>
## The Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

The **Cayley-Hamilton theorem** states the following:

> *Let $A$ be an $\,n \!\times\! n$ matrix whose characteristic polynomial is*
> $p(\lambda) = \textrm{det}(A-\lambda I) = (-1)^n\lambda^n + c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1\lambda + c_0$.
> *Then, the matrix $A$ is a solution of the characteristic equation $p(\lambda) = 0$.*

That is to say, if we substitute $A$ into the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda)$, taking $A^0 = I$, then we have

$$
p(A) = (-1)^nA^n + c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1A + c_0I = O
$$

where $O$ is the zero-matrix.

For example, according to this theorem, for the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}-8 & -9 \\ 6 & 7\end{bmatrix}$
whose characteristic polynomial is

$$
p(\lambda)=\lambda^2 +\lambda -2
$$

we have

$$
p(A) = A^2 +A -2I =O
$$

<a id="expressing-a-power-of-a-matrix-as-a-linear-combination-of-smaller-powers"></a>
## Expressing a Power of a Matrix as a Linear Combination of Smaller Powers

**Example:** Let $A$ be a $2\times 2$ matrix such that $\textrm{tr}(A)=-4$ and $\textrm{det}(A)=-18$. If $A^2 = xA + yI$ for constants $x$ and $y$, then find the value of $x+y$.

**Explanation**

The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is given by

$$
p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\textrm{tr}(A)\lambda+\textrm{det}(A)
$$

Therefore, using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(A) &= O \\
A^{2} - tr(A)A + det(A)I &= O \\
A^{2} &= tr(A)A - det(A)I \\
A^{2} &= -4A + 18I
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
x+y = -4+18 = 14
$$

<a id="expressing-the-inverse-of-a-matrix-as-a-linear-combination-of-its-powers"></a>
## Expressing the Inverse of a Matrix as a Linear Combination of Its Powers

**Example:** Given that $\begin{bmatrix}-8 & -9 \\ 6 & 7\end{bmatrix}$ and $A^{-1}=pI+qA$, find the value of $p+q$.

**Explanation**

Let's find the characteristic equation

$$
\textrm{det}(A-\lambda I)=0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}-8 - λ & -9 \\ 6 & 7 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(-8 - λ)(7 - λ) + 54 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} + λ - 2 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, we have that

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{2} + A - 2I &= O
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $A$ is invertible, we can multiply this equation by $A^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1}(A^{2} + A - 2I) &= O \\
A + I - 2A^{-1} &= O \\
A^{-1} &= \frac{1}{2}A + \frac{1}{2}I
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
p=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

$$
q=\dfrac{1}{2}
$$

and

$$
p+q=\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}=1
$$

<a id="using-the-cayley-hamilton-theorem-to-quickly-evaluate-a-matrix-polynomial"></a>
## Using the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem to Quickly Evaluate a Matrix Polynomial

If we want to evaluate a high-degree polynomial for a given matrix input, it can take a lot of time. For example, suppose we wanted to evaluate the polynomial

$$
f(x) = x^{10} - 5x^9 - 2x^8 + x + 2
$$

for the input $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ Computing the $10$th power of this matrix would be a lot of work!

Luckily, there is a way to speed up the process. The trick is to use Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which tells us that every matrix is a solution to its characteristic equation.

For the given matrix $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ the characteristic polynomial is

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(λ) &= det(A - λI) \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}1 - λ & 2 \\ 3 & 4 - λ\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (1 - λ)(4 - λ) - 6 \\
&= λ^{2} - 5λ - 2
\end{aligned}
$$

so the Cayley-Hamilton theorem tells us that

$$
p(A) = A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0
$$

Now, observe that the polynomial $f(x)$ can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= x^{10} - 5x^{9} - 2x^{8} + x + 2 \\
&= x^{8}(x^{2} - 5x - 2) + x + 2 \\
&= x^{8}p(x) + x + 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Then we have

$$
f(A) = A^8 p(A) + A + 2I
$$

and because $p(A) = 0$, we conclude that

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(A) &= A^{8} \cdot 0 + A + 2I \\
&= 0 + A + 2I \\
&= A + 2I
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, the computation is much faster:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(A) &= A + 2I \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 2 \\ 3 & 6\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note:** Other times, it may not be so obvious how to group the terms of $f(x)$ to reveal $p(x)$. In the general case, it's best to divide $f(x)$ by $p(x)$ using long division, and use the result

$$
f(x) = q(x) p(x) + r(x)
$$

where $q(x)$ is the quotient and $r(x)$ is the remainder. Let's see an example of this.

<a id="evaluating-a-matrix-polynomial-using-the-cayley-hamilton-theorem"></a>
## Evaluating a Matrix Polynomial Using the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

**Example:** Given that $\begin{bmatrix}4 & -5 \\ 3 & -4\end{bmatrix}$ and $f(x) = x^{12} -x^{10} +2x-1$, find $f(A)$.

**Explanation**

Let's start by finding the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(λ) &= det(A - λI) \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}4 - λ & -5 \\ 3 & -4 - λ\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (4 - λ)(-4 - λ) + 15 \\
&= λ^{2} - 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, let's divide $f(x)$ by $p(x)$ using long division:

|  | $\:\:x^{10}$ |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $x^2-1\:$ | $\!\require{enclose}\enclose{longdiv}{x^{12} \,-\, x^{10} + 2x - 1 {\phantom{\mid}}}$ |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|  | $x^{12}$ | $-$ | $x^{10}$ |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  | $2x$ | $-$ | $1$ |

Therefore, we can write $f(x)$ as

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= q(x)p(x) + r(x) \\
&= x^{10}(x^{2} - 1) + (2x - 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
q(x) = x^{10}
$$

Replacing $x$ with $A$, we have

$$
f(A) = q(A)p(A)+(2A-I)
$$

According to the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, we have $p(A)=O$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(A) &= q(A)p(A) + (2A - I) \\
&= q(A) \cdot O + (2A - I) \\
&= 2A - I \\
&= 2\begin{bmatrix}4 & -5 \\ 3 & -4\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}8 & -10 \\ 6 & -8\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}7 & -10 \\ 6 & -9\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
