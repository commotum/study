## Introduction

A matrix is said to be **nilpotent** if some positive power of the matrix is the zero matrix. For example, the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

is nilpotent because its second power is the zero matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= O
\end{aligned}
$$

To state the definition more precisely: a matrix with the property $B^k=O$ for a number $k>0$ is known as a **nilpotent** matrix, and the lowest number $k$ such that $B^k=O$ is called the **index.**

Therefore, our matrix $B$ is a nilpotent matrix of index $2$.

<a id="finding-a-power-of-a-nilpotent-matrix"></a>
## Finding a Power of a Nilpotent Matrix

**Example:** Find $B^2$ given that $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 9 \\ -1 & -3\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Computing $B^2$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^{2} &= B \cdot B \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 9 \\ -1 & -3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & 9 \\ -1 & -3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= O
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that, since $B^2=O$, the matrix $B$ is a nilpotent matrix of index $2$.

## Idempotent Matrices

A matrix is said to be **idempotent**, when multiplied by itself, yields itself.

For example, the matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & -6 \\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}
$$

is idempotent because $B^2 = B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -6 \\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -6 \\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 \cdot 3 + (-6) \cdot 1 & 3 \cdot (-6) + (-6) \cdot (-2) \\ 1 \cdot 3 + (-2) \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot (-6) + (-2) \cdot (-2)\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -6 \\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= B
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-a-power-of-an-idempotent-matrix"></a>
## Finding a Power of an Idempotent Matrix

**Example:** Find $C^5$ given that
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we compute $C^2$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C^{2} &= C \cdot C \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
&= C
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $C^2=C$, so $C$ is an idempotent matrix.

We can use this fact to compute $C^5$ quickly, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C^{5} &= C^{2} \cdot C^{2} \cdot C \\
&= C \cdot C \cdot C \\
&= C^{2} \cdot C \\
&= C \cdot C \\
&= C^{2} \\
&= C
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac{1}{2} & 0 & \frac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$
