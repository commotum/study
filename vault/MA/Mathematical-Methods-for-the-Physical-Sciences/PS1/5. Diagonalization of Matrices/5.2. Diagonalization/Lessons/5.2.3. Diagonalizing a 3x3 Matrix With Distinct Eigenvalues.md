## Introduction

An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is said to be diagonalizable if there exists a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ such that $A=PDP^{-1}$.

For example, the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & -4 \\ 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$ is diagonalizable because
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & -4 \\ 2 & -3\end{bmatrix}$

In general, we have the following theorem:

> *An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if there exists a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that consists of eigenvectors of $A$.*

Since eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are linearly independent, we can also conclude the following fact:

> *If an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$*distinct*eigenvalues (that is, $n$ simple roots of the corresponding characteristic equation), then there exists a basis of eigenvectors. Thus, the matrix is diagonalizable.*

Let's show you how to use the diagonalization theorem with an example.

Suppose that

$$
\lambda_1={\color{blue}-2}
$$

$$
\lambda_2={\color{red}1}
$$

$$
\lambda_3=-3
$$

are eigenvalues of a $3\times 3$ matrix $A$, and that
$\begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$
are the respective eigenvectors. To find an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that

$$
PDP^{-1} = A
$$

we do the following:.

First, we construct the diagonal matrix $D$ by writing the eigenvalues in the main diagonal:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, to construct the matrix $P$, we simply write the respective eigenvectors as columns:

- the $1$st column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_1={\color{blue}-2}$,
- the $2$nd column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_2={\color{red}1}$, and
- the $3$rd column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_3=-3$.

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-3 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Note:** If the matrix $A$ does not have $n$ distinct eigenvalues, it is not always possible to diagonalize it. However, the algorithm to diagonalize any diagonalizable $n\!\times\! n$ matrix is the same as the $3\!\times\! 3$ case!

## Diagonalizing a Matrix Given Its Eigenvalues and Corresponding Eigenvectors

**Example:** Consider the matrix $A$ for which $\lambda_1=-1$, $\lambda_2=1$, $\lambda_3=0$ are eigenvalues, and
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
are the respective eigenvectors. What are the values of $a,b,c$ given that
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & b & 0 \\ a & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Since the eigenvalues of the matrix are

$$
\lambda_1={\color{blue}-1}
$$

$$
\lambda_2={\color{red}1}
$$

$$
\lambda_3=0
$$

we construct the diagonal matrix $D$ by writing the eigenvalues in the main diagonal:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, to construct the matrix $P$, we simply write the respective eigenvectors as columns:

- the $1$st column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_1={\color{blue}-1}$,
- the $2$nd column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_2={\color{red}1}$, and
- the $3$rd column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_3=0$.

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally, we obtain

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

In conclusion, we get $a=0$, $b=-1$, and $c=-1$.

<a id="identifying-whether-a-given-matrix-is-diagonalizable-over-the-real-numbers"></a>
## Identifying Whether a Given Matrix Is Diagonalizable Over the Real Numbers

**Example:** Consider the matrix $A$, given by
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 6 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$
Determine whether $A=PDP^{-1}$ for some invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$, where $P$ and $D$ are both real. In the affirmative case, find a possible diagonal matrix $D$.

**Explanation**

First, we need to determine whether $D$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$.

We start by computing the eigenvalues of $A$. To do that, we solve the characteristic equation, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}2 - λ & 6 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 - λ & 0 \\ 1 & -4 & 4 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(4 - λ)\begin{vmatrix}2 - λ & 6 \\ 2 & 1 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(4 - λ)([λ^{2} - 3λ + 2] - 12) &= 0 \\
(4 - λ)(λ^{2} - 3λ - 10) &= 0 \\
(4 - λ)(λ - 5)(λ + 2) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the eigenvalues of $A$ are

$$
\lambda_1=4
$$

$$
\lambda_2=5
$$

and

$$
\lambda_3=-2
$$

Since all of the eigenvalues are simple roots of the characteristic polynomial, $A$ is diagonalizable.

We construct a diagonal matrix $D$ by writing the eigenvalues in the main diagonal. Therefore, one possible matrix $D$ is
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$

Note that the diagonal matrix $D$ is unique up to the ordering of the eigenvalues. Therefore, another possible diagonal matrix is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}
$$

## Diagonalizing a Matrix Given Part of the Diagonalization

**Example:** Consider the following matrices:
$\begin{bmatrix}-8 & -4 & 6 \\ -9 & -7 & 9 \\ -16 & -10 & 14\end{bmatrix}$
Given that $D=P^{-1}AP$, find the first column of the matrix $P$.

**Explanation**

From the diagonal matrix $D$, we deduce that the eigenvalues of $A$ are

$\qquad$

$$
\lambda_1=-2, \quad
$$

$$
\lambda_2=-1, \quad
$$

$$
\lambda_3=2
$$

So, the first column of $P$ corresponds to the eigenvalue

$$
\lambda_1=-2
$$

Now, we need to find an eigenvector that corresponds to

$$
\lambda_1=-2
$$

Computing $A-(-2)I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - λ_{1}I &= \begin{bmatrix}-8 & -4 & 6 \\ -9 & -7 & 9 \\ -16 & -10 & 14\end{bmatrix} - (-2)\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-6 & -4 & 6 \\ -9 & -5 & 9 \\ -16 & -10 & 16\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we have a system of linear equations with the augmented matrix $M$ that we reduce to row echelon form using Gaussian elimination, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}-6 & -4 & 6 & 0 \\ -9 & -5 & 9 & 0 \\ -16 & -10 & 16 & 0\end{bmatrix}\begin{vmatrix}R_{2}:=R_{2} + (-\frac{3}{2})R_{1} \\ R_{3}:=R_{3} + (-\frac{8}{3})R_{1} \\ ∼\begin{bmatrix}-6 & -4 & 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{2}{3} & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}R_{3}:=R_{3} + (-\frac{2}{3})R_{2} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}-6 & -4 & 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has two pivot columns ($1$st and $2$nd). Thus, $x_3$ is a free variable. From the second equation, we obtain $x_2=0$. Substituting this into the first equation, we get $x_1=x_3$.
Hence, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_{3} \\ 0 \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, setting $x_3=1$, we get the eigenvector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally, $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$

## Diagonalizing a Matrix

**Example:** $\begin{bmatrix}0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$
The matrix $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=2$, $\lambda_2=1$, and $\lambda_3=0$.
Find a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ such that $D=P^{-1}AP$.

**Explanation**

We apply the diagonalization algorithm.

**Step 1.** Notice that we are already given the eigenvalues of the matrix:

$\qquad$

$$
\lambda_1=2,\quad
$$

$$
\lambda_2=1, \quad
$$

$$
\lambda_3=0
$$

**Step 2.** Now, we need to find an eigenvector basis of $A$. Applying our usual method, we get the following.

- For $\lambda_1=2$, we get
$A - 2I|= \begin{bmatrix}-2 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-2I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives us the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_2=1$, we get
$A - I|= \begin{bmatrix}-1 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives us the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_3=0$, we get
$A - 0I|= A = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.
Seeking a non-zero solution of $(A-0I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives us the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3.** We construct the diagonal matrix $D$ and the invertible matrix $P$ in the usual way, and this gives us the following:
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
