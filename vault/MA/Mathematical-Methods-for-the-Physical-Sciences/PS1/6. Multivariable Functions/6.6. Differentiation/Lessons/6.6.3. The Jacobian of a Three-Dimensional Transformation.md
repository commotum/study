<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction

Similar to transformations in the plane, we can also consider transformations of three-dimensional regions.

For example, the transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{bmatrix}
$$

maps a rectangular prism in Cartesian $uvw$-space onto one-eighth of a sphere in Cartesian $xyz$-space, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/73ce0ea62a7269301b1cd8a4f5eda03f.png>)

In general, if a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}
$$

is a $C^1$ transformation, meaning that it and its first derivatives are continuous, then the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf{T}$ is defined as

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} & \frac{∂x}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v} & \frac{∂y}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u} & \frac{∂z}{∂v} & \frac{∂z}{∂w}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, to compute the Jacobian determinant of our transformation above, we first calculate the required partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} &= \sin v\cos w & \frac{∂x}{∂v}|= u\cos v\cos w & \frac{∂x}{∂w}|=-u\sin v\sin w \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}|= \sin v\sin w & \frac{∂y}{∂v}|= u\cos v\sin w & \frac{∂y}{∂w}|= u\sin v\cos w \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u}|= \cos v & \frac{∂z}{∂v}|=-u\sin v & \frac{∂z}{∂w}|= 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\sin v\cos w & u\cos v\cos w & -u\sin v\sin w \\ \sin v\sin w & u\cos v\sin w & u\sin v\cos w \\ \cos v & -u\sin v & 0\end{vmatrix} \\
&= \cos v\begin{vmatrix}u\cos v\cos w & -u\sin v\sin w \\ u\cos v\sin w & u\sin v\cos w\end{vmatrix}+ u\sin v\begin{vmatrix}\sin v\cos w & -u\sin v\sin w \\ \sin v\sin w & u\sin v\cos w\end{vmatrix} \\
&= \cos v \cdot u^{2}\cos v\sin v \cdot (\cos^{2} w + \sin^{2} w) + u\sin v \cdot u\sin^{2} v \cdot (\cos^{2} w + \sin^{2} w) \\
&= u^{2}\cos^{2} v\sin v + u^{2}\sin^{2} v\sin v \\
&= u^{2}\sin v \cdot (\cos^{2} v + \sin^{2} v) \\
&= u^{2}\sin v
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's get some practice at computing Jacobians of three-dimensional transformations.

<a id="intuition-behind-the-jacobian"></a>
## Intuition Behind the Jacobian

The Jacobian determinant of a spatial transformation can be interpreted similarly to the Jacobian of a plane transformation.

As before, the Jacobian describes *local* effects, not global ones.

Given that $J(u_0,v_0,w_0)$ denotes the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf{T}$ evaluated at $(u_0, v_0, w_0)$, let's describe $\mathbf T$'s effect on a *tiny* rectangular prism in Cartesian $uvw$-space whose bottom, back left corner is at the point $(u_0,v_0,w_0)$.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/43ebb9ef1188a265ea3d23204eb8441c.png>)

It can be shown that, provided that our prism is very small and $(u_0,v_0, w_0)$ is not a critical point of $\mathbf T$, the image of our prism can be approximated by a parallelepiped.

The Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ evaluated at $(u_0,v_0,w_0)$ describes the following key features between our tiny rectangular prism and its (approximate) image:

- The local *volume* scale factor of $\mathbf T$. Suppose the length, width, and height of our rectangular prism are $\delta u$, $\delta v$, and $\delta w$, so its volume is $\delta V = \delta u \delta v \delta w$. Then, it can be shown that the approximate parallelepiped in $xyz$-space has a volume equal to $\left|J(u_0,v_0,w_0)\right| \cdot \delta V$, as depicted below.![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/5046763d3cb48583b185eac74bb0e852.png>)
- Whether the transformation is *locally* orientation-preserving or *locally* orientation-reversing.If $J(u_0,v_0,w_0) > 0$, the transformation is (locally) orientation-preserving. Geometrically, this means that the transformation maps a triple of vectors onto a triple with the same orientation (right-handed $\to$ right-handed or left-handed $\to$ left-handed).![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/b68cfe728cde8a377a4ab0678b0172ff.png>)If $J(u_0,v_0, w_0) < 0$, the transformation is (locally) orientation-reversing.Geometrically, this means that the transformation maps a triple of vectors onto a triple with the opposite orientation (right-handed $\to$ left-handed or left-handed $\to$ right-handed).![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/2a00089a9c4cc879ab7a2a82e2519c1e.png>)
- Whether the transformation is *locally* invertible or *locally* non-invertible.If $J(u_0,v_0,w_0) \neq 0$, the transformation is (locally) invertible.Geometrically, this means that the transformation maps our tiny rectangular prism (a three-dimensional region) onto a three-dimensional region (i.e., a parallelepiped).![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/2dd21cf504714308ba61b5f50729c650.png>)If $J(u_0,v_0,w_0)= 0$, the transformation is (locally) non-invertible.Geometrically, this means that the transformation maps our tiny rectangular prism (a three-dimensional region) onto a two-dimensional region (a parallelogram), a one-dimensional region (a line segment), or a zero-dimensional region (a point).![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.6. Differentiation/Source/6.6.3. The Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation/Images/73aeb6d2d89e357a27761e8cdc0822fa.png>)

<a id="finding-the-jacobian-of-a-three-dimensional-transformation"></a>
## Finding the Jacobian of a Three-Dimensional Transformation

**Example:** Find the Jacobian determinant of the transformation $\mathbf{T}$ that maps the Cartesian $uvw$-space onto the Cartesian $xyz$-space by means of the equations
$x = 2u-v,\qquad y = u+3v+w,\qquad z = u+2v-w$.

**Explanation**

For a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}
$$

the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$, denoted

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)}
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} & \frac{∂x}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v} & \frac{∂y}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u} & \frac{∂z}{∂v} & \frac{∂z}{∂w}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

First, we find the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} &= 2 & \frac{∂x}{∂v}|=-1 & \frac{∂x}{∂w}|= 0 \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}|= 1 & \frac{∂y}{∂v}|= 3 & \frac{∂y}{∂w}|= 1 \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u}|= 1 & \frac{∂z}{∂v}|= 2 & \frac{∂z}{∂w}|=-1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}2 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -1\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2\begin{vmatrix}3 & 1 \\ 2 & -1\end{vmatrix}- (-1) \cdot\begin{vmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2(-3 - 2) + (-1 - 1) \\
&=-10 - 2 \\
&=-12
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-critical-points-of-a-transformation"></a>
## The Critical Points of a Transformation

The **critical points** of a transformation $\mathbf T: \mathbb R^3\longrightarrow\mathbb R^3$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}
$$

are the points in Cartesian $uvw$-space where the Jacobian is zero:

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)}=0
$$

Similar to the two-dimensional case, the transformation $\mathbf T$ is locally non-invertible at the critical points.

Finally, provided that $\mathbf{T}$ is invertible, the connection between the Jacobians of $\mathbf T$ and $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ is as follows:

$$
\dfrac{\partial (u, v, w)}{\partial (x, y, z)} = \left(\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)} \right)^{-1}
$$

<a id="determining-the-critical-points-of-a-transformation"></a>
## Determining the Critical Points of a Transformation

**Example:** Find the locations of all of the critical points of the transformation $\mathbf{T}$, where $\mathbf T$ is defined as

$\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

For a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}
$$

the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$, denoted

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)}
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} & \frac{∂x}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v} & \frac{∂y}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u} & \frac{∂z}{∂v} & \frac{∂z}{∂w}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, we have

$$
x(u,v,w) =w-u^3, \qquad y(u,v,w) = 2uv, \qquad z(u,v,w) = u^2+v
$$

First, we find the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} &= -3u^{2} & \frac{∂x}{∂v}|= 0 & \frac{∂x}{∂w}|= 1 \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}|= 2v & \frac{∂y}{∂v}|= 2u & \frac{∂y}{∂w}|= 0 \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u}|= 2u & \frac{∂z}{∂v}|= 1 & \frac{∂z}{∂w}|= 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}-3u^{2} & 0 & 1 \\ 2v & 2u & 0 \\ 2u & 1 & 0\end{vmatrix} \\
&=-3u^{2}\begin{vmatrix}2u & 0 \\ 1 & 0\end{vmatrix}+ 1\begin{vmatrix}2v & 2u \\ 2u & 1\end{vmatrix} \\
&=-3u^{2}(0 - 0) + 1(2v - 4u^{2}) \\
&= 2(v - 2u^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

The function has critical points when

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)} =0
$$

So, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= 0 \\
2(v - 2u^{2}) &= 0 \\
v - 2u^{2} &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The equation $v-2u^2 = 0$ defines the parabolic cylinder $v=2u^2$.

<a id="finding-the-jacobian-of-an-inverse-transformation"></a>
## Finding the Jacobian of an Inverse Transformation

**Example:** Consider the transformation $\mathbf{T}:(u,v,w) \to (x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w))$. The inverse transformation $\mathbf T^{-1}(x,y,z)\to(u(x,y,z), v(x,y,z), w(x,y,z))$ is given by
$\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{bmatrix}$

Find the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$.

*Hint: You can assume that $x>0$, $y \ne 0$.*

**Explanation**

For a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v \\ w\end{bmatrix}
$$

the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$, denoted

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)}
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} & \frac{∂x}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v} & \frac{∂y}{∂w} \\ \frac{∂z}{∂u} & \frac{∂z}{∂v} & \frac{∂z}{∂w}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In this case, we're only given $\mathbf T^{-1}$. So, we compute the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ as follows:

- First, we compute $\dfrac{\partial (u, v, w)}{\partial (x, y, z)}$, the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T^{-1}$.
- Then, we will compute the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ using the relation
$\dfrac{\partial (x, y, z)}{\partial (u, v, w)} = \left(\dfrac{\partial (u, v, w)}{\partial (x, y, z)} \right)^{-1}$.

Here, we have

$$
u(x,y,z) = x^2, \qquad v(x,y,z) = xy, \qquad w(x,y,z) = yz
$$

First, we find the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂u}{∂x} &= 2x & \frac{∂u}{∂y}|= 0 & \frac{∂u}{∂z}|= 0 \\ \frac{∂v}{∂x}|= y & \frac{∂v}{∂y}|= x & \frac{∂v}{∂z}|= 0 \\ \frac{∂w}{∂x}|= 0 & \frac{∂w}{∂y}|= z & \frac{∂w}{∂z}|= y\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(u, v, w))/(∂(x, y, z)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂u}{∂x} & \frac{∂u}{∂y} & \frac{∂u}{∂z} \\ \frac{∂v}{∂x} & \frac{∂v}{∂y} & \frac{∂v}{∂z} \\ \frac{∂w}{∂x} & \frac{∂w}{∂y} & \frac{∂w}{∂z}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}2x & 0 & 0 \\ y & x & 0 \\ 0 & z & y\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2x\begin{vmatrix}x & 0 \\ z & y\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2x(xy - 0) \\
&= 2x^{2}y
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing down

$$
\dfrac{\partial (u, v, w)}{\partial (x, y, z)}
$$

in terms of $u$, $v$, and $w$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x^{2}y &= 2 \cdot x \cdot (xy) \\
&= 2 \cdot \sqrt{u} \cdot v \\
&= 2v\sqrt{u}
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we take the reciprocal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y, z))/(∂(u, v, w)) &= ((∂(u, v, w))/(∂(x, y, z)))^{-1} \\
&= \frac{1}{2v\sqrt{u}}
\end{aligned}
$$
