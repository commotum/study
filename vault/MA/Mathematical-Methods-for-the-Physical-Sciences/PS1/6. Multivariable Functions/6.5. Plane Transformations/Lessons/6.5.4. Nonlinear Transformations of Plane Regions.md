## Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to find the images of simple objects in the plane under the action of nonlinear transformations.

Consider the square region $\Delta$ and it's boundary $\partial \Delta$ in the $uv$-plane, where $\Delta$ is given by

$$
\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}
$$

A sketch of this region, specified with a counterclockwise orientation, is shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/4825ed533a17decb33af33fa894ae4a4.png>)

Since $\partial \Delta$ is specified with an orientation, we refer to it as a **directed boundary.**

Now, consider the transformation $\mathbf T:\Bbb R^2 \to \Bbb R^2$, defined by

$$
\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to \big(v(u^2+1), \: u \big)
$$

where

$$
x = v(u^2+1), \qquad y = u
$$

Our task is to find the image of $\Delta$ under the action of $\mathbf T$.

First, note the following:

- It's easy to check that $\mathbf T$ has no critical points in the *interior* of $\Delta$ (we'll discuss why this matters shortly).
- Since $\Delta$'s interior contains no critical points, we only need to find the image of its boundary $\partial \Delta$.
- To find the image of $\partial \Delta$, we parametrize each *directed* edge and map them individually onto the $xy$-plane.

Let's now map each directed edge of $\partial \Delta$ to the $xy$-plane:

- Consider the image of the side $S_1$, where we have $v = 0$ and $0 \leq u \leq 1$. Substituting $v = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (v(u^{2} + 1), u); = (0(u^{2} + 1), u); = (0, u), \mid 0 \le u \le 1$.
So, the image of $S_1$ can be parametrized by
$x = 0, \qquad y= u, \qquad 0 \leq u \leq 1$.
Writing these parametric equations in the usual notation, we have
$x = 0, \qquad y= t, \qquad 0 \leq t \leq 1$.
These are parametric equations of a line segment. Moreover, the endpoints of $S_1$ are mapped to the $xy$-plane as follows:
$T: (0, 0) → (0 \cdot (0^{2} + 1), 0) = (0, 0); T: (1, 0) → (0 \cdot (1^{2} + 1), 1) = (0, 1)$
Therefore, the image of $S_1$ is a line segment traversed from the point $(0,0)$ to $(0,1)$.![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/c5fe4bde19404aabc7bffc4a390fd5f0.png>)

- Consider the image of the side $S_2$. Here, we have $u = 1$ and $0 \leq v \leq 1$. Substituting $u = 1$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (v(u^{2} + 1), u); = (v(1^{2} + 1), 1); = (2v, 1), \mid 0 \le v \le 1$.
This horizontal line segment is traversed from the point $(0,1)$ to the point $(2,1)$ in the $xy$-plane.![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/b45a16c32a9ecc401ee2d05604d69e97.png>)
- Consider the image of the side $S_3$. Here, we have $v = 1$ and $1 \geq u \geq 0$ (we write the inequality for $u$ in this way to show that $S_3$ is traversed *from* the point where $u=1$ *to* the point where $u=0$).Substituting $v = 1$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (v(u^{2} + 1), u); = (1(u^{2} + 1), u); = (u^{2} + 1, u), \mid 1 \ge u \ge 0$.
So, the image of $S_3$ can be parametrized by
$x = u^2+1, \qquad y= u, \qquad 1 \geq u \geq 0$.
Writing these in the usual notation, we have
$x = t^2+1, \qquad y= t, \qquad 0 \leq t \leq 1$.
These are parametric equations of a parabolic arc. Notice that if we eliminate $t$ from the equation, we get
$y^2 = x-1$
which is a right-opening parabola whose vertex is at $(1,0)$. Moreover, the endpoints of $S_3$ are mapped to the $xy$-plane as follows:
$T: (1, 1) → (1 \cdot (1^{2} + 1), 1) = (2, 1); T: (0, 1) → (1 \cdot (0^{2} + 1), 0) = (1, 0)$
Therefore, the image of $S_3$ is an arc of a right-opening parabola, traversed from the point $(2,1)$ to $(1,0)$.![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/eef124c093337eae98885a45972721d4.png>)

- Consider the image of the side $S_4$. Here, we have $u = 0$ and $1 \geq v \geq 0$. Substituting $u = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (v(u^{2} + 1), u); = (v(0^{2} + 1), 0); = (v, 0), \mid 1 \ge v \ge 0$.
This is a horizontal line segment traversed from the point $(1,0)$ to the point $(0,0)$ in the $xy$-plane.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/3102a49736f031a00ee9628e806b8599.png>)

And that's it! We have successfully computed the image of $\Delta$ in the $xy$-plane.

It's highly recommended that you redo this calculation yourself. Make sure you can construct the image of $\Delta$, including its orientation (note that the image of $\Delta$ is traversed *clockwise*).

## Finding Part of an Image

**Example:** Consider the square region $\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane, given by
$\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}$,

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/301e42965375d20de3dae4fc94252bcb.png>)

and the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by
$\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to (x(u,v),y(u,v))$,
where

$x = u - v^2, \qquad y = 2v$.

The image of the side $S_4$ under the action of $\mathbf T$ is given by
$x = \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{x(t)}, \qquad y=2t, \qquad 1 \geq t \geq 0$.

Find the function $x(t)$.

**Explanation**

To map the entire region $\Delta$ onto the $xy$-plane, we consider the transformation of each side. However, in this case, we consider only the side $S_4$.

For the side $S_4$, we have $u = 0$ and

$$
1 \geq v \geq 0
$$

Substituting $u = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x, y) &= T(u, v) \\
&= (u - v^{2}, 2v) \\
&= (0 - v^{2}, 2v) \\
&= (-v^{2}, 2v)
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the image of $S_4$ can be parametrized by

$$
x = -v^2, \qquad y=2v, \qquad 1 \geq v \geq 0
$$

Writing this in the usual notation, we have

$$
x(t) = \bbox[2pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}-t^2}, \qquad y(t)=2t, \qquad 1 \geq t \geq 0
$$

## Cases When a Square’s Side Is Mapped to a Segment

**Example:** Consider the square region $\Delta$ and its **directed** boundary $\partial\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane, where $\Delta$ is given by
$\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}$,

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/eb83daf1d6411acc9b374a6918e705d5.png>)

and the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by
$\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to (x(u,v),y(u,v))$,
where

$x = 2u-3v, \qquad y=v^2-3u$.

The image of the side $S_3$ under the action of $\mathbf{T}$ is a **directed** line segment. Find the endpoints of this segment, stated in the order that they are traversed.

**Explanation**

To map the entire region $\Delta$ onto the $xy$-plane, we consider the transformation of each side. However, in this case, we consider only the side $S_3$.

For the side $S_3$, we have $v=1$ and

$$
1 \geq u \geq 0
$$

Substituting $v = 1$ into our transformation equations, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x, y) &= T(u, v) \\
&= (2u - 3v, v^{2} - 3u) \\
&= (2u - 3(1), 1^{2} - 3u) \\
&= (2u - 3, 1 - 3u)
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the image of $S_3$ can be parametrized by

$$
x=2u-3, \qquad y=1-3u, \qquad 1 \geq u \geq 0
$$

Writing this in the usual notation, we have

$$
x(t) = 2t-3, \qquad y(t) = 1-3t, \qquad 1 \geq t \geq 0
$$

This is a line segment with endpoints $(-1,-2)$ and $(-3,1)$ in the $xy$-plane.

## Cases When a Square’s Side Is Mapped to a Parabola

**Example:** Consider the square region $\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane, given by
$\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}$,

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/f5018f5963c09ade7244ff19220fbce9.png>)

and the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by
$\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to (x(u,v),y(u,v))$,
where
$x = (u + v)^2, \qquad y = \dfrac{u}{v+1}$.

Which of the following is the image of the side $S_1$ under the action of $\mathbf{T}$?

**Explanation**

To map the entire region $\Delta$ onto the $xy$-plane, we consider the transformation of each side. However, in this case, we consider only the side $S_1$.

For the side $S_1$, we have $v = 0$ and

$$
0 \leq u \leq 1
$$

Substituting $v = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(x, y) &= T(u, v) \\
&= ((u + v)^{2}, \frac{u}{v + 1}) \\
&= ((u + 0)^{2}, \frac{u}{0 + 1}) \\
&= (u^{2}, u)
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the image of $S_1$ can be parametrized by

$$
x = u^2, \qquad y = u, \qquad 0 \leq u \leq 1
$$

Writing this in the usual notation, we have

$$
x(t) = t^2, \qquad y(t) = t, \qquad 0 \leq t \leq 1
$$

These are parametric equations of a parabolic arc. Notice that if we eliminate $t$ from the equations, we get
$y^2=x$
which is a right-opening parabola whose vertex is at $(0,0)$.

Moreover, the endpoints of $S_1$ are mapped to the $xy$-plane as follows:

$$
T: (0, 0) → ((0 + 0)^{2}, \frac{0}{0 + 1}) = (0, 0); T: (1, 0) → ((1 + 0)^{2}, \frac{1}{0 + 1}) = (1, 1)
$$

Therefore, the image of $S_1$ is an arc of a right-opening parabola, traversed from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

<a id="the-significance-of-critical-points"></a>
## The Significance of Critical Points

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/9372545326ea374437f1999d8112814d.png>)

Earlier, we found that the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by

$$
\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to \big(v(u^2+1), \: u \big) = \big(u^2v+v, \: u \big)
$$

maps the square region $\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane to the region $D$ in the $xy$-plane shown above.

We deduced the image of $\Delta$ by mapping each edge of its boundary. For this method to work, we need to check that $\mathbf T$ has no critical points in the *interior* of $\Delta$.

To check for critical points, we start by computing the Jacobian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the partial derivatives of $x(u,v)$, we have

$$
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial u}(u^2v+v) = 2uv, \qquad \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial v}(u^2v+v) = u^2+1
$$

and for the partial derivatives of $y(u,v)$, we have

$$
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial u}(u) = 1, \qquad \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial v}(u) = 0
$$

Substituting our partial derivatives into our expression for the Jacobian, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}2uv & u^{2} + 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{vmatrix} \\
&= - (1 + u^{2})
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, note the following:

- The Jacobian is negative everywhere in the $(u,v)$ plane. So, $\mathbf T$ has no critical points inside $\Delta$.
- Since the Jacobian is negative everywhere, the transformation $\mathbf T$ is orientation-reversing everywhere (note, for example, that $\Delta$ and $D$ have opposite orientation).

To apply this method, we require that $\mathbf T$ has no critical points in the *interior* of $\Delta$. This method works even if the *boundary* of $\Delta$ contains critical points. Let's explore this a little more.

---

<a id="critical-points-on-the-boundary-of-the-domain"></a>
## Critical Points on the Boundary of the Domain

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/1cd138206929fcf68399e666df5a24e2.png>)

Let's again consider our square region $\Delta{:}$

$$
\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}
$$

Now consider the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by

$$
\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to \big(uv, \: u^2+uv) \big)
$$

The Jacobian matrix determinant of $\mathbf{T}$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}v & u \\ 2u + v & u\end{vmatrix} \\
&=-2u^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting the Jacobian equal to zero, we get

$$
-2u^2 = 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = 0
$$

Therefore, the critical points of $\mathbf{T}$ lie on the line $u = 0$. This coincides with the side $S_4$ of our square.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/efee422ce927aa8fc070a8cd6d42328e.png>)

Now, since there are no critical points in the interior of $\Delta$, we can find its image in the usual way. However, the side $S_4$ may collapse to a single point. So, let's go ahead and finish this example.

<a id="finding-the-image-of-a-square"></a>
## Finding the Image of a Square

**Example:** Consider the square region $\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane, given by
$\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}$

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/bb3f4499d045cb59b5d13942268c59c2.png>)

and the transformation $\mathbf T$, given by
$\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to (x(u,v),y(u,v))$,
where

$x = uv, \qquad y = u(u+v)$.

Find the image of $\Delta$ under the action of $\mathbf{T}$.

**Explanation**

We know that $\mathbf T$ has no critical points in the interior of $\Delta$. So, to map the entire region $\Delta$ onto the $xy$-plane, we transform each side of $\Delta$ separately:

- Consider the image of the side $S_1$, where we have $v = 0$ and $0 \leq u \leq 1$. Substituting $v = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (uv, u(u + v)); = (u(0), u(u + 0)); = (0, u^{2}), \mid 0 \le u \le 1$.
This is a vertical line segment traversed from the point $(0,0)$ to the point $(0,1)$ in the $xy$-plane.
- Consider the image of the side $S_2$. Here, we have $u = 1$ and $0 \leq v \leq 1$. Substituting $u = 1$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (uv, u(u + v)); = (1(v), 1(1 + v)); = (v, 1 + v), \mid 0 \le v \le 1$.
This is a line segment traversed from the point $(0,1)$ to the point $(1,2)$ in the $xy$-plane.
- Consider the image of the side $S_3$. Here, we have $v = 1$ and $1 \geq u \geq 0$. Substituting $v = 1$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (uv, u(u + v)); = (u(1), u(u + 1)); = (u, u^{2} + u), \mid 1 \ge u \ge 0$.
This is an upward-opening parabola traversed from the point $(1,2)$ to the point $(0,0)$ in the $xy$-plane.
- Consider the image of the side $S_4$. Here, we have $u = 0$ and $1 \geq v \geq 0$. Substituting $u = 0$ into our transformation equations, we obtain
$(x, y)|= T(u, v); = (uv, u(u + v)); = (0v, 0(0 + v)); = (0, 0), \mid 1 \ge v \ge 0$.
This is the point $(0,0)$ in the $xy$-plane.

Drawing the images of the sides of our square, we obtain the following image of $\Delta{:}$

<a id="critical-points-inside-the-domain"></a>
## Critical Points Inside the Domain

Until now, we've specified that our transformation $\mathbf T$ should not contain critical points in the interior of the preimage $\Delta$. So let's briefly discuss what to do when $\Delta$ contains critical points.

Once again, let's consider the region

$$
\Delta = \big\{(u,v) \:: \: 0 \leq u \leq 1, \: 0\leq v \leq 1 \big\}
$$

in the $uv$-plane, shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/78fb54bcd124bcf899d41c09320ed8b2.png>)

We now consider the image of $\Delta$ under the action of $\mathbf T$, given by

$$
\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to \big(uv, \: u^2+v^2 \big)
$$

Let's start by finding the critical points. First, we compute the Jacobian:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}v & u \\ 2u & 2v\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2v^{2} - 2u^{2} \\
&= 2(v + u)(v - u)
\end{aligned}
$$

The critical points correspond to the solutions of

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)} = 0
$$

Therefore, the critical points are given by

$$
2(v+u)(v-u) = 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=\pm v
$$

Therefore, the critical points of $\mathbf T$ that lie *inside* $\Delta$ are located on the line $u = v$.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/21534dea8ae6fc7e23de8546ed2dddf1.png>)

To find the image of $\Delta$ in this case, we divide $\Delta$ into two regions $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_2$ along the critical line (as shown below) and compute their images separately.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/8965d64c1ed18452efa040c141b53910.png>)

Let's compute the images of these two regions:

- Computing the image of $\Delta_1$'s boundary, we find that $\mathbf{T}(S_1)$ is a vertical line segment traversed from $(0,0)$ to $(0,1)$,$\mathbf{T}(S_2)$ is an upward-opening parabola traversed from $(0,1)$ to $(1,2)$, and $\mathbf{T}(C_1)$ is a line segment traversed from $(1,2)$ to $(0,0)$.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/bff9ac3c6d0ec0967fb69a4aa60f2656.png>)

- Similarly, computing the image of $\Delta_2$'s boundary, we find that $\mathbf{T}(C_2)$, a line segment traversed from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$, $\mathbf{T}(S_3)$, an upward-opening parabola traversed from $(1,2)$ to $(0,1)$, and$\mathbf{T}(S_4)$, a vertical line segment traversed from $(0,1)$ to $(0,0)$.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/7e3ac25b6d0306c63cadd6c51ed4b6f0.png>)

Superimposing the two results, we obtain the following image of $\Delta{:}$

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/c90d7fc2453f0dab501438094c85a045.png>)

Note that:

- As $(u,v)$ runs over $\Delta$ once, its image $D$ is traced out twice.
- The boundary of $D$ that runs from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ is the image of the critical line $u=v$, which is *not* a bounding curve of our original region $\Delta$.

<a id="the-jacobian-as-a-local-area-scale-factor"></a>
## The Jacobian as a Local Area Scale Factor

We'll now justify why the Jacobian determinant gives the (local) area scale factor of a transformation.

Consider a rectangular region $\Delta$ in the $uv$-plane of length $\delta u$, width $\delta v$, whose bottom-left corner is at the point $(u_0,v_0)$, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/b789e2c5190d52e665504a63dcd9dd64.png>)

Furthermore, we assume the following:

- $\mathbf T: \mathbb R^2\rightarrow \mathbb R^2$ is a $C^1$ transformation throughout $\Delta$, and contains no critical points.
- The image of $\Delta$ under the action of $\mathbf T$ is the region $D$.
- The image of $(u_0,v_0)$ is $(x_0,y_0)$.
- The image of $S_1$ is $S_1'$, and its endpoints are $(x_0,y_0)$ and $A$.
- The image of $S_2$ is $S_2'$, and its endpoints are $(x_0,y_0)$ and $B$.

Since $S_1'$ is the image of $S_1$, it can be parametrized by varying $u$ only. Similarly, since $S_2'$ is the image of $S_2$, it can be parametrized by varying $v$ only. So, the position vectors of $A$ and $B$, respectively, are given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) \\ y(u_{0} + δu, v_{0})\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, since $\delta u$ is small, $S_1'$ can be approximated by a line segment. Similarly, since $\delta v$ is small, $S_2'$ can also be approximated by a line segment. Let's construct two vectors $\mathbf{s}_1$ and $\mathbf{s}_2$ that span our two segments, as shown below.

![](<MA/Mathematical-Methods-for-the-Physical-Sciences/PS1/6. Multivariable Functions/6.5. Plane Transformations/Source/6.5.4. Nonlinear Transformations of Plane Regions/Images/b181aa7c6b828d235bcf9ba160df1ae8.png>)

We can find the vector $\mathbf s_1$ by subtracting the position vectors of its endpoints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{1} = a - \begin{bmatrix}x_{0} \\ y_{0}\end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}x(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) \\ y(u_{0} + δu, v_{0})\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}x(u_{0}, v_{0}) \\ y(u_{0}, v_{0})\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}x(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) - x(u_{0}, v_{0}) \\ y(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) - y(u_{0}, v_{0})\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, recall that when $\delta u$ is small, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & _{(u_{0}, v_{0})} & \approx (x(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) - x(u_{0}, v_{0}))/(δu) \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & _{(u_{0}, v_{0})} & \approx (y(u_{0} + δu, v_{0}) - y(u_{0}, v_{0}))/(δu)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, the vector $\mathbf s_1$ can be approximated as follows:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using similar arguments, we have the following approximation for $\mathbf s_2{:}$

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{bmatrix}
$$

The area of $D$ is approximately equal to the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf s_1$ and $\mathbf s_2$. Therefore,

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; s_{1} \mid s_{2}; \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

Substituting our approximations for $\mathbf s_1$ and $\mathbf s_2$, we arrive at the desired result:

$$
Area(D)\begin{vmatrix}\approx & det\begin{bmatrix}δu \cdot \frac{∂x}{∂u} & δv \cdot \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ δu \cdot \frac{∂y}{∂u} & δv \cdot \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{bmatrix}\end{vmatrix}_{(u_{0}, v_{0})}; =\begin{vmatrix}δuδv \cdot (\frac{∂x}{∂u}\frac{∂y}{∂v} - \frac{∂x}{∂v}\frac{∂y}{∂u}) & _{(u_{0}, v_{0})} \\ = δuδv \cdot & (∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v))\end{vmatrix}_{(u_{0}, v_{0})}
$$
