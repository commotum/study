## Introduction

There are several important properties of the variance of a random variable that we can use to simplify expressions.

Firstly, the variance of a constant is always zero. That is, for any constant $a$, we have

$$
\textrm{Var}[a] = 0
$$

Intuitively, this makes sense. A constant does not vary, so the mean deviation from its expected value should be zero.

---

<a id="computing-the-variance-of-a-constant"></a>
## Computing the Variance of a Constant

**Example:** Calculate $\textrm{Var}[4k^2-1]$ where $k$ is a constant.

**Explanation**

Recall that for any constant $a$, we have

$$
\textrm{Var}[a] = 0
$$

Therefore, since $k$ is a constant, $4k^2-1$ is also a constant, and we have

$$
\textrm{Var}[4k^2-1] = 0
$$

<a id="the-variance-of-a-scaled-random-variable"></a>
## The Variance of a Scaled Random Variable

Suppose that $X$ is a random variable and $a$ is a constant. Then,

$$
\textrm{Var}[aX] = a^2\textrm{Var}[X]
$$

So, for example, if we know that

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = 2
$$

then

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[3X] &= 3^{2} \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 9 \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 9 \cdot 2 \\
&= 18
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[- 4X] &= (-4)^{2} \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 16 \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 16 \cdot 2 \\
&= 32
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note:** To understand why we have to square the constant when we factor it outside the variance, it's helpful to remember that the variance is defined as the expected *squared* deviation from the mean:

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm E [ (X - \textrm E[X])^2 ]
$$

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[aX] &= E[(aX - E[aX])^{2}] \\
&= E[(aX - aE[X])^{2}] \\
&= E[a^{2}(X - E[X])^{2}] \\
&= a^{2}E[(X - E[X])^{2}] \\
&= a^{2}Var[X]
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-the-variance-of-a-scaled-random-variable"></a>
## Computing the Variance of a Scaled Random Variable

**Example:** The probability distribution of the random variable $X$ is given below. Given that $\textrm E[X] = 2.3$, calculate $\textrm{Var}[-10X]$.

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $f(x)$ | $0.2$ | $0.3$ | $0.5$ |

**Explanation**

Recall that for any random variable $X$ and constant $a$, we have

$$
\textrm{Var}[aX] = a^2\cdot\textrm{Var}[X]
$$

In our case, $a=-10$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[- 10X] &= (-10)^{2} \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 100 \cdot Var[X]
\end{aligned}
$$

We can compute the variance of $X$ using the following formula:

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm E[X^2] - \textrm E[X]^2
$$

Additionally, we're given that

$$
\textrm E [X] =2.3
$$

We start by computing $\textrm E[X^2]$. Summing up the products of each value of $X^2$ and its associated probability, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X^{2}] &= 1^{2} \cdot f(1) + 2^{2} \cdot f(2) + 3^{2} \cdot f(3) \\
&= 1(0.2) + 4(0.3) + 9(0.5) \\
&= 5.9
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[X] &= E[X^{2}] - E[X]^{2} \\
&= 5.9 - (2.3)^{2} \\
&= 0.61
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[- 10X] &= 100 \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 100 \cdot 0.61 \\
&= 61
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-variance-of-a-transformed-random-variable"></a>
## The Variance of a Transformed Random Variable

Suppose that $X$ is a random variable and $a$ and $b$ are constants. It can be shown that

$$
\textrm{Var}[aX+b] = a^2\textrm{Var}[X]
$$

A proof of this result is given at the end of this lesson.

Therefore, when a random variable is scaled by a factor of $a$ and shifted by a constant $b$:

- the variance is scaled by a factor of $a^2$ as before, and
- the variance is unaffected by the shift.

The fact that shifting a random variable leaves the variance unaltered makes intuitive sense. Shifting all values of $X$ by a fixed amount does not change how far apart those values are from each other.

For example, if we know that

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = 3
$$

then

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[2X + 1] &= 2^{2} \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 2^{2} \cdot 3 \\
&= 4 \cdot 3 \\
&= 12
\end{aligned}
$$

---

<a id="computing-the-variance-of-a-transformed-random-variable"></a>
## Computing the Variance of a Transformed Random Variable

**Example:** If $\textrm{Var}[X] = 10$, calculate $\textrm{Var}[1-2X]$.

**Explanation**

Recall that for any random variable $X$ and constants $a$ and $b$, we have

$$
\textrm{Var}[aX+b] = a^2\cdot\textrm{Var}[X]
$$

In our case, $a=-2$ and $b=1$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[1 - 2X] &= (-2)^{2} \cdot Var[X] \\
&= 4 \cdot 10 \\
&= 40
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="proof-of-the-general-formula"></a>
## Proof of the General Formula

We wish to prove the following result:

$$
\textrm{Var}[aX+b] = a^2\textrm{Var}[X]
$$

Recall that, by definition,

$$
\textrm{Var}[X] = \textrm E [ (X - \textrm E[X])^2 ]
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Var[aX + b] &= E[(aX + b - E[aX + b])^{2}]
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the properties of expected value, we can simplify this as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[(aX + b - E[aX + b])^{2}] &= E[(aX + b - aE[X] - b)^{2}] \\
&= E[(aX - aE[X])^{2}] \\
&= E[(a(X - E[X]))^{2}] \\
&= E[a^{2}(X - E[X])^{2}] \\
&= a^{2}E[(X - E[X])^{2}] \\
&= a^{2}Var[X]
\end{aligned}
$$
