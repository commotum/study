## Introduction

We can use proof by contradiction to prove the following properties about rational and irrational numbers:

- The sum of a rational and an irrational number is always irrational.
- The product of a nonzero rational number and an irrational number is always irrational.
- The quotient of an irrational number and a nonzero rational number is always irrational.

In this lesson, we'll learn how to prove these properties in particular cases.

For example, assume we've proved that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. Let's use this result to prove that $2\sqrt{3}$ is also irrational.

First, we write down the formal statement $P$ that we wish to prove:

$$
\phantom{\lnot}\:\: P{:} \quad 2\sqrt{3} \: \textrm{is irrational}
$$

To prove that $P$ is true, we assume that $\lnot P$ is true and show this leads to a contradiction.

$$
\lnot P{:} \quad 2\sqrt{3} \: \textrm{is rational}
$$

We begin our proof as follows:

> *Assume, for a contradiction, that $2\sqrt{3}$ is rational.*

Since we're assuming $2\sqrt{3}$ is rational, we can express it as the ratio of two nonzero integers:

> *Then $2\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{a}{b}$ for some non-zero integers $a$ and $b$.*

The idea is to rewrite this equation to give a radical $(\sqrt 3)$ on one side and a fraction on the other. This will provide us with a contradiction since $\sqrt 3$ is irrational and, therefore, cannot equal a ratio of two integers.

> *So, we have*
> $2\sqrt{3}|= \frac{a}{b}; \sqrt{3}|= (a)/(2b_(⏟)_(c))$.

Now, if we define the integer $c=2b$ (highlighted above), we have that

$$
\sqrt{3}=\dfrac{a}{c}
$$

which shows that $\sqrt{3}$ is rational.

> *Let $c=2b$. Since $2$ and $b$ are integers, we have that $c$ is an integer.*
> *Therefore, we have*
> $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{c}$,
> *which is rational.*
> *But this is a contradiction since $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.*

Since we have a contradiction, our original assumption must be incorrect. Hence, we write our conclusion as follows:

> *Therefore, $2\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.*

Now that we've figured out the details, let's state the full proof.

<a id="stating-the-full-proof"></a>
## Stating the Full Proof

Proposition:

> *Given that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational, prove that $2\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.*

Proof:

> *Assume, for a contradiction, that $2\sqrt{3}$ is rational.*
> *Then $2\sqrt{3} = \dfrac{a}{b}$ for some non-zero integers $a$ and $b$.*
> *So, we have*
> $2\sqrt{3}|= \frac{a}{b}; \sqrt{3}|= (a)/(2b_(⏟)_(c))$.
> *Let $c=2b$. Since $2$ and $b$ are integers, we have that $c$ is an integer.*
> *Therefore, we have*
> $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{c}$,
> *which is rational.*
> *But this is a contradiction since $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.*
> *Therefore, $2\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.*

The whole proof rests on the assumption that $\sqrt 3$ is irrational. You may ask, "But how do we know that $\sqrt 3$ is irrational?"

The irrationality of $\sqrt 3$ can be proved using proof by contradiction! However, the proof methodology is slightly different, so we'll save that discussion for a separate lesson.

## Proving Irrationality of Sums and Differences of Radicals

We can use a similar argument to show that a sum or difference of radicals is irrational.

For example, suppose we wish to show that the following expression is irrational:

$$
\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2
$$

We assume for a contradiction that this is *rational.*

$$
\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2 = \dfrac{a}{b}
$$

To isolate a single radical on one side, we first square both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{2})^{2} &= (\frac{a}{b})^{2} \\
(\sqrt{3})^{2} - 2 \cdot \sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{2} + (\sqrt{2})^{2} &= \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}} \\
3 - 2 \cdot \sqrt{3 \cdot 2} + 2 &= \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}} \\
5 - 2 \cdot \sqrt{6} &= \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that we reduced the original equation containing the radicals $\sqrt 2$ and $\sqrt 3$ to an equation containing the single radical

$$
\sqrt{2\cdot 3} = \sqrt 6
$$

Isolating the radical, we get:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-2 \cdot \sqrt{6} &= \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}} - 5 \\
2 \cdot \sqrt{6} &= 5 - \frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}} \\
\sqrt{6} &= \frac{5}{2} - \frac{a^{2}}{2b^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting the terms on the right-hand side over a common denominator, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{6} &= \frac{5b^{2}}{2b^{2}} - \frac{a^{2}}{2b^{2}} \\
&= \frac{5b^{2} - a^{2}}{2b^{2}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $a$ and $b$ are integers, it follows that

$$
c = 5b^2-a^2
$$

and $d = 2b^2$ are integers. Therefore, we have

$$
\sqrt 6 = \dfrac{c}{d}
$$

Now, it can be shown that $\sqrt 6$ is irrational. So, we have reached a contradiction! Therefore, we conclude that $\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2$ is irrational.

## Proving Irrationality of Sums and Differences

**Example:** Prove by contradiction that $3-\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. You may assume without proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

**Explanation**

Let's write down the formal statement $P$ that we wish to prove:

$$
P{:} \quad 3-\sqrt{2} \: \textrm{is irrational}
$$

To prove that $P$ is true, we assume that $\lnot P$ is true and show this leads to a contradiction.

$$
\lnot P{:} \quad 3-\sqrt{2} \: \textrm{is rational}
$$

We begin our proof as follows:

> Assume, for a contradiction, that $3-\sqrt{2}$ is rational.

Since we're assuming $3-\sqrt{2}$ is rational, we can express it as the ratio of two non-zero integers:

> Then $3-\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{a}{b}$ for some non-zero integers $a$ and $b$.

The idea is to rearrange the equation, leaving a root on one side and a fraction on the other.

> So, we have
> $3 - \sqrt{2}|= \frac{a}{b}; - \sqrt{2}|= \frac{a}{b} - 3; \sqrt{2}|= 3 - \frac{a}{b}; \sqrt{2}|= \frac{3b}{b} - \frac{a}{b}; \sqrt{2}|= \frac{3b - a}{b}^(⏞)^(c)$

Now, if we define $c=3b-a$ (highlighted above), we have that

$$
\sqrt{2}=\dfrac{c}{b}
$$

which shows that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational.

> Let $c=3b-a$. Since $a$ and $b$ are integers, we have that $c$ is an integer.
> Therefore, we have
> $\sqrt{2} = \frac{c}{b}$,
> which is rational.
> But this is a contradiction since $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

Since we have a contradiction, our original assumption must be incorrect. Hence, we write our conclusion as follows:

> Therefore, $3-\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
