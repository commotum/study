## Introduction

Recall that two sets $A$ and $B$ have the *same cardinality* if there exists a bijection of $A$ onto $B$. If no such bijection exists, the sets are said to have *unequal cardinalities*.

In this lesson, we'll discuss how bijections can be used to determine whether two real intervals have the same cardinality.

To begin, consider the intervals $A$ and $B$, defined below:

$$
A= (0,1), \qquad B= (0,2)
$$

In terms of their *lengths*, the second interval is twice as long as the first. But what about their cardinalities?

Since the length of $B$ is twice that of $A$, our intuition might tell us that $B$ should have twice the cardinality of $A$. However, this is *not* the case! You might be surprised to learn that $A$ and $B$ have the same cardinality!

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

To show why this is true, we need to construct a bijection that maps $A$ onto $B$.

First, notice that the function $y=x$ bijectively (i.e., both injectively and surjectively) maps the interval $(0,1)$ onto the interval $(0,1)$.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/2a710d1af0c2f61cc5efdbfe41b76549.png>)

However, we need a function that maps $(0,1)$ onto $(0,2)$ instead of onto $(0,1)$. To construct one, we can stretch our function by a factor of $2$ along the $y$-axis. This can be done by multiplying the function by $2$.

So, we consider the function $f: A \to B$ that maps the underlying sets as follows:
$f: x \to 2x$

Notice that $f$ bijectively maps $(0,1)$ onto $(0,2)$.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/20474fb8701b2b188ad3568cfa91448c.png>)

Therefore,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

i.e., the sets have the same cardinality.

## Constructing a Bijection Between Two Open Segments

**Example:** Do the sets below have the same cardinality, and why?
$A= (0,2), \qquad B= (1,9)$

**Explanation**

Sets $A$ and $B$ have the *same cardinality* if there exists a bijection of $A$ onto $B$.

Recall that the function $y=x$ bijectively (i.e., both injectively and surjectively) maps the interval $(0,2)$ onto the interval $(0,2)$.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/c7938562916c2ce4a75c51a5cac00596.png>)

However, we need a function that maps $(0,2)$ of length $2$ onto $(1,9)$ of length $8$. To construct one, we proceed as follows:

- First, we stretch our function by a factor of $4$ along the $y$-axis, i.e, multiply the function by $4$.
- Next, we shift the result $1$ units up, i.e., add $1$ to the result of the previous transformation.

So, we consider the function $f: (0,2) \to (1,9)$ that maps the underlying sets as follows:

$$
f: x \to 4x + 1
$$

Notice that $f$ bijectively maps $(0,2)$ onto $(1,9)$.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/715f3019154866d72ab119872547ae09.png>)

Therefore,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

i.e., the sets have the same cardinality.

## The Exponential and Logarithm Functions

As we've seen, transformations of the function

$$
f(x) = x
$$

help to construct bijections that map finite intervals to finite intervals. Let's now consider some functions we can use to map infinite intervals to infinite intervals.

- The function $y=e^x$ bijectively (i.e., both injectively and surjectively) maps the interval $(-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R}$ onto the interval $(0,\infty)$.![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/4ef1775847a3992d968871cf73b11c43.png>)

- On the other hand, $y=\ln x$ bijectively maps the interval $(0,\infty)$ onto the interval $(-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R}$.![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/01502f33da09c0a60b98a083bc7b0a75.png>)

In particular, this means that

$$
\big\begin{vmatrix}(0,\infty) \big & = \big & (-\infty,\infty) \big & = & \mathbb R\end{vmatrix}
$$

Similar to what we've seen previously, we can perform transformations on these elementary functions to construct bijections that map between different intervals. Let's see some examples.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/e193af4837e024a55927f6ecafe6dad0.png>)

$$

i.e., the sets have the same cardinality.

## Tangent, Cotangent, and Arctangent

Finally, let's consider functions we can use to map an (open) segment to the real line and vice-versa.

- The function $y = \tan x$ bijectively (i.e., both injectively and surjectively) maps the interval $\left(-\dfrac{\pi}2,\dfrac{\pi}2\right)$ onto the interval $(-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R}$.![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/3113f6fa698eefe3d448916a92ff6256.png>)
- Similarly, the function $y = \cot x$ bijectively maps the interval $\left(0, \pi\right)$ onto the interval $(-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R}$.![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/ef266706bfe1a0cab015b8517e201bbb.png>)
- Finally, the function $y = \arctan x$ bijectively maps the interval $\left(-\infty, \infty \right)$ onto the interval $\left(-\dfrac\pi2,\dfrac\pi2\right)$.![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/7fe93146a30cff62ac0536be6800e251.png>)

In particular, this means that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigg\begin{vmatrix}\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2} \right) \bigg & =\big & (0,\pi)\big &= \big & (-\infty, \infty) \big & = \big & \mathbb R\big\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Constructing a Bijection Between an Open Segment and the Real Line

**Example:** Do the sets below have the same cardinality, and why?

$A= (-3,3), \qquad B= \mathbb{R}$

**Explanation**

Sets $A$ and $B$ have the *same cardinality* if there exists a bijection between $A$ and $B$.

Recall that the function $y = \tan x$ bijectively (i.e., both injectively and surjectively) maps the interval

$$
\left(-\dfrac{\pi}2,\dfrac{\pi}2\right)
$$

onto the interval

$$
(-\infty,\infty) = \mathbb{R}
$$

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/5a3ce8802d2cde8f5f1201efa1962bf3.png>)

However, we need a function that maps from $(-3,3)$ onto $\mathbb R$ instead of

$$
\left(-\dfrac{\pi}2,\dfrac{\pi}2\right)
$$

onto $\mathbb R$. To construct one, we can expand our tangent curve by a factor of

$$
\dfrac{6}{\pi}
$$

along the $x$-axis. This can be done by multiplying the argument of the function by

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

So, we consider the function $f: A \to B$ that maps the underlying sets as follows:

$$
f: x \to \tan \left(\dfrac{\pi x}{6}\right)
$$

Notice that $f$ bijectively maps $(-3,3)$ onto $\mathbb{R}$.

![](<../Source/5.3.2. Continuous Infinite Sets With Equal Cardinality/Images/5842f1d30cc6a5095a2b5f0c0a0dea87.png>)

Therefore,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}A & = & B\end{vmatrix}
$$

i.e., the sets have the same cardinality.
