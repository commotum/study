## Introduction

We often want to measure the strength of the (linear) dependence between two random variables. One measure that we use for this purpose is covariance.

The **covariance** between two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is defined as

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] = \textrm{E}\Big[\left(X - \textrm{E}[X]\right)\left(Y - \textrm{E}[Y]\right) \Big]
$$

Let's break this formula down a bit:

- $\left(X - \textrm{E}[X]\right)$ is the deviation of $X$ from its mean
- $\left(Y - \textrm{E}[Y]\right)$ is the deviation of $Y$ from its mean

Therefore, the covariance measures the expected value of the product of these deviations.

The covariance can also be expressed as

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] =\sum_{(x,y)\in S}(x - \textrm E[X])(y - \textrm E[Y])f(x,y)
$$

where $f(x,y)$ is the joint PMF of $X$ and $Y$, and $S$ is the joint support.

Let's sketch a few diagrams to get a feel for what the covariance tells us.

- A *positive* covariance means that if $X$ increases, then $Y$ is likely to increase. We say that $X$ and $Y$ are **positively correlated**.![](<../Source/11.4.1. The Covariance of Two Random Variables/Images/f32b6656ee95ac0e7d0ee9926c11409f.png>)
- A *negative* covariance means that if $X$ increases, then $Y$ is likely to decrease. We say that $X$ and $Y$ are **negatively correlated**.![](<../Source/11.4.1. The Covariance of Two Random Variables/Images/69563f85dd1ce9b30334f86da2b462ce.png>)

Note the following:

- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then
$\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] = 0$.
The reverse implication does not always hold! For example, two random variables can have zero covariance yet be dependent. This is because covariance measures *linear* dependence only.
- The covariance of a random variable with itself is its variance:
$Cov[X, X]|= E[(X - E[X])(X - E[X])]; = E[(X - E[X])^{2}]; = Var[X]$

- Aside from indicating whether two random variables are positively or negatively correlated, the covariance *by itself* does not tell us very much. For example, if
$\textrm{Cov}[X_1, Y_1] > \textrm{Cov}[X_2, Y_2] > 0$
this does not necessarily mean that the dependence between $X_1$ and $Y_1$ is somehow "stronger" than the dependence between $X_2$ and $Y_2$. This is because the covariance does not take the variance of the individual random variables into account. In a future lesson, we'll use the covariance to define the so-called **correlation coefficient,** which *does* consider the variance of the individual random variables and so *can* be used to compare the relative strength of the linear dependence between two pairs of random variables.

## Stating the Covariance Using the Definition

**Example:** The joint probability mass function $f(x,y)$ for the discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint support $S=S_X\times S_Y$ is given below.

| $f$ | $Y=2$ | $Y=5$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $X=1$ | $0.15$ | $0.25$ |
| $X=4$ | $0.45$ | $0.15$ |

Find an expression for the covariance in sigma notation.

**Explanation**

The covariance of two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint support $S$ is defined as

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] = \sum_{(x,y)\in S}(x - \textrm E[X])(y - \textrm E[Y])f(x,y)
$$

First, let's find the marginal mass functions for $X$ and $Y$. These are given by the row and column totals, respectively.

| $f$ | $Y=2$ | $Y=5$ | $f_X$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $X=1$ | $0.15$ | $0.25$ | $0.4$ |
| $X=4$ | $0.45$ | $0.15$ | $0.6$ |
| $f_Y$ | $0.6$ | $0.4$ | $1$ |

We now compute $\textrm{E}[X]$ and $\textrm{E}[Y]$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= ∑_(x ∈ S_{X})x \cdot f_{X}(x) \\
&= 1 \cdot 0.4 + 4 \cdot 0.6 \\
&= 0.4 + 2.4 \\
&= 2.8 \\
E[Y] &= ∑_(y ∈ S_{Y})y \cdot f_{Y}(y) \\
&= 2 \cdot 0.6 + 5 \cdot 0.4 \\
&= 1.2 + 2 \\
&= 3.2
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the formula for the covariance, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)(x - E[X])(y - E[Y])f(x, y) \\
&= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)(x - 2.8)(y - 3.2)f(x, y)
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="calculating-covariance-using-the-definition"></a>
## Calculating Covariance Using the Definition

**Example:** The joint probability mass function $f(x,y)$ for the discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given below.

| $f$ | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $X=1$ | $0.1$ | $0.5$ |
| $X=2$ | $0$ | $0.4$ |

Calculate the covariance of $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

The covariance of two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint support $S$ is defined as

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] = \sum_{(x,y)\in S}(x - \textrm E[X])(y - \textrm E[Y])f(x,y)
$$

First, let's find the marginal mass functions for $X$ and $Y$. These are given by the row and column totals, respectively.

| $f$ | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ | $f_X$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $X=1$ | $0.1$ | $0.5$ | $0.6$ |
| $X=2$ | $0$ | $0.4$ | $0.4$ |
| $f_Y$ | $0.1$ | $0.9$ | $1$ |

We now compute $\textrm{E}[X]$ and $\textrm{E}[Y]$, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= ∑_(x ∈ S_{X})x \cdot f_{X}(x) \\
&= 1 \cdot 0.6 + 2 \cdot 0.4 \\
&= 0.6 + 0.8 \\
&= 1.4 \\
E[Y] &= ∑_(y ∈ S_{Y})y \cdot f_{Y}(y) \\
&= 0 \cdot 0.1 + 1 \cdot 0.9 \\
&= 0 + 0.9 \\
&= 0.9
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the formula for the covariance, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)(x - E[X])(y - E[Y])f(x, y) \\
&= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)(x - 1.4)(y - 0.9)f(x, y) \\
&= (1 - 1.4)(0 - 0.9)f(1, 0) \\
&= + (1 - 1.4)(1 - 0.9)f(1, 1) \\
&= + (2 - 1.4)(0 - 0.9)f(2, 0) \\
&= + (2 - 1.4)(1 - 0.9)f(2, 1) \\
&= (-0.4)(-0.9)(0.1) \\
&= + (-0.4)(0.1)(0.5) \\
&= + (0.6)(-0.9)(0) \\
&= + (0.6)(0.1)(0.4) \\
&= 0.04
\end{aligned}
$$

## A Formula for Quickly Computing Covariance

You may have noticed that computing the covariance using the definition is quite a lot of work. Is there a formula that makes it easier?

Indeed, there is. It can be shown that

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] =\textrm E [XY] - \textrm E [X]\cdot \textrm E [Y]
$$

We'll prove this formula at the end of the lesson. But for now, let's get some practice at applying it.

<a id="finding-the-covariance-using-the-shortcut-formula"></a>
## Finding the Covariance Using the Shortcut Formula

**Example:** The joint probability mass function $f(x,y)$ for the discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is given below.

| $f$ | $Y=2$ | $Y=3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $X=1$ | $0.2$ | $0.2$ |
| $X=4$ | $0.4$ | $0.2$ |

If $\textrm{E} [X]=2.8$ and $\textrm{E}[Y] = 2.4$, calculate the covariance of $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

The covariance of $X$ and $Y$ can be calculated using the formula

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{E} [XY] - \textrm{E}[X]\cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

We use the rule of the lazy statistician to find $\textrm{E}[XY]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[XY] &= ∑_((x, y) ∈ S)xy \cdot f(x, y) \\
&= (1)(2)(0.2) + (1)(3)(0.2) + (4)(2)(0.4) + (4)(3)(0.2) \\
&= 6.6
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, using the covariance formula, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= 6.6 - (2.8)(2.4) \\
&=-0.12
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="covariance-for-continuous-random-variables"></a>
## Covariance for Continuous Random Variables

If $X$ and $Y$ are continuous random variables, the covariance between $X$ and $Y$ is given by

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] =\iint_\limits{\mathbb R^2}(x - \textrm E[X])(y - \textrm E[Y])f(x,y) \, \textrm d x\textrm d y
$$

As is the case with discrete random variables, it's often easier to compute the covariance using the following formula:

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = \textrm{E} [XY] - \textrm{E}[X]\cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

Suppose we're given that the random variables $X$ and $Y$ have the joint probability density function

$$
{\frac{1}{2}(x + \frac{y}{2}),\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 2, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

To compute the covariance of this distribution, we first use the rule of the lazy statistician to find $\textrm{E}[XY]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[XY] &= ∫_{0}^{2}∫_{0}^{1}xyf(x, y)dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}∫_{0}^{1}xy \cdot \frac{1}{2}(x + \frac{y}{2})dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2}y∫_{0}^{1}(x^{2} + \frac{1}{2}xy)dxdy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2}y[\frac{1}{3}x^{3} + \frac{1}{4}x^{2}y]_{x = 0}^{x = 1}dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2}y[\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4}y]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{2}(\frac{1}{6}y + \frac{1}{8}y^{2})dy \\
&= [\frac{1}{12}y^{2} + \frac{1}{24}y^{3}]_{y = 0}^{y = 2} \\
&= \frac{1}{12} \cdot 2^{2} + \frac{1}{24} \cdot 2^{3} \\
&= \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} \\
&= \frac{2}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Using methods discussed in previous lessons, we can compute $\textrm{E}[X]$ and $\textrm{E}[Y]{:}$

$$
\textrm{E}[X] = \dfrac{7}{12}, \qquad \textrm{E}[Y] = \dfrac76
$$

Finally, using the covariance formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= \frac{2}{3} - (\frac{7}{12})(\frac{7}{6}) \\
&=-\frac{1}{72}
\end{aligned}
$$

Remember that if two random variables are independent then their covariance equals zero. Let's see an example.

## Finding the Covariance Given a Joint Probability Density Function

**Example:** The random variables $X$ and $Y$ have the joint probability density function

${\frac{3}{5}\sqrt{x}y,\begin{vmatrix}0 \le x \le 1, 2 \le y \le 3, \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$.

Find the covariance of $X$ and $Y$.

**Explanation**

The joint support of $X$ and $Y$ is given by the rectangle

$$
S = [0,1] \times [2,3]
$$

Moreover, we can write

$$
f(x,y) = g(x) \cdot h(y)
$$

where

$$
g(x) =\dfrac{3}{5} \sqrt{x}
$$

and $h(y) =y$.

Therefore, $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and consequently

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X,Y] = 0
$$

<a id="proof-of-the-covariance-formula"></a>
## Proof of the Covariance Formula

Let's now prove the following formula:

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] =\textrm E [XY] - \textrm E[X]\cdot \textrm E[Y]
$$

We start with the definition of covariance, given by

$$
\textrm{Cov}[X, Y] =\textrm E\Big[\left(X- \textrm E[X]\right)\left(Y-\textrm E[Y]\right)\Big]
$$

Expanding the product in the brackets, we get

$$
Cov[X, Y] = E[XY - X \cdot E[Y] - E[X] \cdot Y + E[X] \cdot E[Y]]
$$

Now, recall that if $a$ and $b$ are constants, then

$$
\textrm E[a]=a, \qquad \textrm{E}[aX + bY] = a\cdot \textrm{E}[X] + b \cdot \textrm{E}[Y]
$$

Since $\textrm{E}[X]$ and are $\textrm{E}[Y]$ are both constants, the covariance formula simplifies as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Cov[X, Y] &= E[XY - X \cdot E[Y] - E[X] \cdot Y + E[X] \cdot E[Y]] \\
&= E[XY] - E[X \cdot E[Y]] - E[E[X] \cdot Y] + E[E[X] \cdot E[Y]] \\
&= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] - E[X] \cdot E[Y] + E[X] \cdot E[Y] \\
&= E[XY] - E[X] \cdot E[Y]
\end{aligned}
$$
