## Introduction

Suppose the random variable $X\sim B(n,p)$ follows a binomial distribution.

There are certain situations where working with binomial random variables becomes difficult. For example, when the parameter $n$ is large, computing exact probabilities can become problematic due to the large numbers involved.

Under certain conditions, we can approximate the *discrete* random variable $X$ using a *continuous normal* random variable $Y$, given by

$$
Y \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are calculated using the formulas for the mean and variance of the binomial random variable $X$:

$$
\mu = np, \qquad \sigma^2 = np(1-p)
$$

To determine whether a given binomial distribution can be approximated using a normal distribution, we use the following rule of thumb:

- $np > 5$, and
- $n(1-p) > 5$.

To get a feel for how this works, let's consider a few examples of binomial distributions and their corresponding normal approximations:

- First, consider the case where $n=5$ and $p=0.2$. In this case, it's straightforward to show that
$\mu = {\color{blue}{1}},\qquad \sigma^2 = {\color{red}{0.8}}$,
and
$np = 1 < 5,\qquad n(1-p) = 4 < 5$.
Since $np < 5$, the normal distribution $N({\color{blue}{1}},{\color{red}{0.8}})$ is *not* a good approximation to $B(5, 0.2)$. The diagram below compares the two distributions. The yellow rectangles correspond to the values of the PMF of $B(5, 0.2)$, and the smooth blue curve is the PDF of the corresponding normal distribution.![](<../Source/11.5.1. Normal Approximations of Binomial Distributions/Images/8cfafc32409fd0181c8b2844e067b8d0.png>)
- Next, we consider the case where $n=5$ and $p=0.5$. In this case, it's straightforward to show that
$\mu = {\color{blue}{2.5}},\qquad \sigma^2 = {\color{red}{1.25}}$,
and
$np = n(1-p) = 2.5 < 5$.
Since $np = n(1-p) < 5$, the normal distribution $N({\color{blue}{2.5}},{\color{red}{1.25}})$ is *not* a good approximation to $B(5, 0.5)$.![](<../Source/11.5.1. Normal Approximations of Binomial Distributions/Images/1507bae644c7c2fe2de1006667ee03bc.png>)
- Finally, we consider the case where $n=20$ and $p=0.5$. In this case, it's straightforward to show that
$\mu = {\color{blue}{10}},\qquad \sigma^2 = {\color{red}{5}}$,
and
$np = n(1-p) = 10 > 5$.
Since $np > 5$ and $n(1-p) > 5$, the normal distribution $N({\color{blue}{10}},{\color{red}{5}})$ *is* a good approximation to $B(20, 0.5)$.![](<../Source/11.5.1. Normal Approximations of Binomial Distributions/Images/007aafc33b4905b45a14b08e9e700d2a.png>)

The fact that we can approximate binomial random variables as normal when $np > 5$ and $n(1-p) > 5$ is a consequence of the so-called **central limit theorem** (or CLT). We'll discuss this in more detail at the end of the lesson.

## Identifying Situations Where a Normal Approximation is Appropriate

**Example:** Which of the following probabilities could be approximated using the normal approximation of the binomial distribution?

1. The probability that a basketball player scores at least $2$ baskets in a series of $3$ free throws if the probability of scoring a basket on each free throw is $0.55$.
2. The probability of getting $35$ even numbers on $60$ rolls of a $6$-sided die.
3. The probability that a fair spinner with three regions, red, green, and blue, lands on green in $5$ out of $12$ spins.

**Explanation**

Given a binomial random variable $X \sim B(n,p)$, if

- $np > 5$, and
- $n(1-p) > 5$,

we can approximate $X$ as a normal random variable $Y$ with mean $\mu = np$ and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p)
$$

that is,

$$
Y \sim N(np, np(1-p))
$$

Let's now examine each situation.

- In situation I, we have $n=3$ and $p=0.55$. Therefore,
$np|= 3 \cdot 0.55; = 1.65; ≯5.\cdot$
So, we *cannot* use the normal approximation of the binomial distribution.

- In situation II, we have $n=60$ and $p=\dfrac12$. Therefore,
$np|= 60 \cdot 0.5; = 30; > 5✓; n(1 - p)|= 60 \cdot (1 - 0.5); = 30; > 5.✓$
So, we can use the normal approximation of the binomial distribution.
- In situation III, we have $n=12$ and $p=\dfrac13$. Therefore,
$np|= 12 \cdot \frac{1}{3}; = 4; ≯5.\cdot$
So, we *cannot* use the normal approximation of the binomial distribution.

Therefore, the correct answer is "II only."

<a id="using-the-normal-approximation-to-the-binomial-distribution"></a>
## Using the Normal Approximation to the Binomial Distribution

Suppose we have a random variable $X \sim B(50,0.6)$. Let's estimate the value of

$$
P(X=33)
$$

using the normal approximation.

Given a binomial random variable $X \sim B(n,p)$, if

- $np > 5$, and
- $n(1-p) > 5$,

we can approximate $X$ as a normal random variable $Y$ with mean $\mu = np$ and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p)
$$

that is,

$$
Y \sim N(np, np(1-p))
$$

Here, we have $n=50$ and $p = 0.6$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
np &= 50 \cdot 0.6 \\
&= 30 \\
&> 5✓ \\
n(1 - p) &= 50 \cdot (1 - 0.6) \\
&= 20 \\
&> 5.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we can approximate our binomial random variable $X$ with a normal random variable $Y$ with mean

$$
\mu = 50\cdot0.6 = 30
$$

and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = 50\cdot0.6\cdot(1-0.6)=12
$$

That is, we can approximate $X$ by

$$
Y \sim N(30,12)
$$

Now, since $X$ is discrete and $Y$ is continuous, we need to apply a continuity correction. The required continuity correction in this case is

$$
P(X = 33) \approx P(32.5 \leq Y \leq 33.5)
$$

To compute this probability, we transform to a standard normal random variable by $z$-scoring:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(32.5 \le Y \le 33.5) &= P(\frac{32.5 - 30}{\sqrt{12}} \le Z \le \frac{33.5 - 30}{\sqrt{12}}) \\
&\approx P(0.72 \le Z \le 1.01) \\
&= P(Z \le 1.01) - P(Z < 0.72) \\
&= P(Z \le 1.01) - P(Z \le 0.72) \\
&= Φ(1.01) - Φ(0.72)
\end{aligned}
$$

Using a table of values for the CDF of the standard normal distribution, we find that

$$
\Phi(1.01) = 0.8438, \qquad \Phi(0.72) = 0.7642
$$

So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(32.5 \le Y \le 33.5) &= Φ(1.01) - Φ(0.72) \\
&= 0.8438 - 0.7642 \\
&= 0.0796
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
P(X = 33) \approx 0.0796
$$

<a id="a-note-on-rounding-errors"></a>
## A Note on Rounding Errors

In the last example, we approximated the binomial random variable

$$
X \sim B(50,0.6)
$$

using a normal random variable

$$
Y \sim N(30,12)
$$

and we arrived at the approximation

$$
P(X=33) \approx P(32.5 \leq Y \leq 33.5) \approx 0.0796
$$

If you were to use a software package to evaluate this approximation, you might get an answer like

$$
P(X = 33) \approx P(32.5 \leq Y \leq 33.5) \approx 0.0791
$$

Notice that there is a difference of around $0.0005$ between the two answers.

There is a reason for this discrepancy. When we calculated that

$$
P(X = 33) \approx P(32.5 \leq Y \leq 33.5) \approx 0.0796
$$

we rounded our $z$-values two decimal places and then used a lookup table to find $\Phi(z)$ for those rounded values. As a result, we introduced some small rounding errors.

Typically, the rounding errors are small, so we will continue to work with a table of values. However, it's worth bearing this in mind if you're using a software package or calculator to compute $\Phi(z)$.

## Using a Normal Approximation to Approximate a Binomial Probability

**Example:** In a city, $44\%$ of the people have blood type A. Using the normal approximation to the binomial distribution, approximate the probability that if $150$ people are picked at random, then $70$ of them will have blood type A. Give your answer to three decimal places.

*Hint: The data below is taken from the table of values of the cumulative distribution function $\Phi(z)$ for the standard normal distribution.*

| $z$ | $\, 0.04 \,$ | $\, 0.05 \,$ | $\, 0.06 \,$ | $\, 0.07 \,$ | $\, 0.08 \,$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\, 0.5 \,$ | $\, 0.7054 \,$ | $\, 0.7088 \,$ | $\, 0.7123 \,$ | $\, 0.7157 \,$ | $\, 0.7190 \,$ |
| $\, 0.6 \,$ | $\, 0.7389 \,$ | $\, 0.7422 \,$ | $\, 0.7454 \,$ | $\, 0.7486 \,$ | $\, 0.7517 \,$ |
| $\, 0.7 \,$ | $\, 0.7704 \,$ | $\, 0.7734 \,$ | $\, 0.7764 \,$ | $\, 0.7794 \,$ | $\, 0.7823 \,$ |

**Explanation**

Given a binomial random variable $X \sim B(n,p)$, if

- $np > 5$, and
- $n(1-p) > 5$,

we can approximate $X$ as a normal random variable $Y$ with mean $\mu = np$ and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p)
$$

that is,

$$
Y \sim N(np, np(1-p))
$$

Here, we have $n=150$ and $p = 0.44$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
np &= 150 \cdot 0.44 \\
&= 66 \\
&> 5✓ \\
n(1 - p) &= 150 \cdot (1 - 0.44) \\
&= 84 \\
&> 5.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we can approximate our binomial random variable $X$ with a normal random variable $Y$ with mean

$$
\mu = np = (150)(0.44) = 66
$$

and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p) = (150)(0.44)(1-0.44) = 36.96
$$

That is, we can approximate $X$ by $Y \sim N(66, 36.96)$.

Now, we can approximate the desired probability

$$
P(X = 70)
$$

Using a continuity correction, we have

$$
P(X =70) \approx P(69.5 \leq Y \leq 70.5)
$$

To compute this probability, we transform to a standard normal random variable by $z$-scoring:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(69.5 \le Y \le 70.5) &= P(\frac{69.5 - 66}{\sqrt{36.96}} \le Z \le \frac{70.5 - 66}{\sqrt{36.96}}) \\
&\approx P(0.58 \le Z \le 0.74) \\
&= P(Z \le 0.74) - P(Z < 0.58) \\
&= P(Z \le 0.74) - P(Z \le 0.58) \\
&= Φ(0.74) - Φ(0.58) \\
&= 0.7704 - 0.7190 \\
&= 0.0514
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
P(X = 70)\approx 0.051
$$

Note: Using a software package for the approximation gives a probability of $0.0528$.

## Using a Normal Approximation to Approximate a Binomial Probability Over an Interval

**Example:** An archer practices firing an arrow at a target $50$ times. The archer has a $47\%$ chance of hitting the bull's eye on each shot. Using the normal approximation to the binomial distribution, estimate the probability that the archer hits the bull's eye more than $29$ times but less than $35$ times. Give your final answer to three decimal places.

*Hint: The data below is taken from the table of values of the cumulative distribution function $\Phi(z)$ for the standard normal distribution.*

| $z$$\, 0.00 \,$$\, 0.01 \,$$\, 0.02 \,$$\, 1.6 \,$ $\, 0.9452 \,$ $\, 0.9463 \,$ $\, 0.9474 \,$ $\, 1.7 \,$ $\, 0.9554 \,$ $\, 0.9564 \,$ $\, 0.9573 \,$ $\, 1.8 \,$ $\, 0.9641 \,$ $\, 0.9649 \,$ $\, 0.9656 \,$ |  | $z$$\, 0.00 \,$$\, 0.01 \,$$\, 0.02 \,$$\, 3.0 \,$ $\, 0.9987 \,$ $\, 0.9987 \,$ $\, 0.9987 \,$ $\, 3.1 \,$ $\, 0.9990 \,$ $\, 0.9991 \,$ $\, 0.9991 \,$ $\, 3.2 \,$ $\, 0.9993 \,$ $\, 0.9993 \,$ $\, 0.9994 \,$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |

**Explanation**

Given a binomial random variable $X \sim B(n,p)$, if

- $np > 5$, and
- $n(1-p) > 5$,

we can approximate $X$ as a normal random variable $Y$ with mean $\mu = np$ and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p)
$$

that is,

$$
Y \sim N(np, np(1-p))
$$

Here, we have $n=50$ and $p = 0.47$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
np &= 50 \cdot 0.47 \\
&= 23.5 \\
&> 5✓ \\
n(1 - p) &= 50 \cdot (1 - 0.47) \\
&= 26.5 \\
&> 5.✓
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we can approximate our binomial random variable $X$ with a normal random variable $Y$ with mean

$$
\mu = np = (50)(0.47) = 23.5
$$

and variance

$$
\sigma^2 = np(1-p) = (50)(0.47)(1-0.47) = 12.455
$$

That is, we can approximate $X$ by $Y \sim N(23.5, 12.455)$.

Now, we can estimate the desired probability $P(29 < X < 35)$. Using a continuity correction, we have

$$
P(29 < X < 35) = P(30 \leq X \leq 34) \approx P(29.5 \leq Y \leq 34.5)
$$

To compute this probability, we transform to a standard normal random variable by $z$-scoring:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(29.5 \le Y < 34.5) &= P(\frac{29.5 - 23.5}{\sqrt{12.455}} \le Z \le \frac{34.5 - 23.5}{\sqrt{12.455}}) \\
&\approx P(1.70 \le Z \le 3.12) \\
&= P(Z \le 3.12) - P(Z < 1.70) \\
&= P(Z \le 3.12) - P(Z \le 1.70) \\
&= Φ(3.12) - Φ(1.70) \\
&= 0.9991 - 0.9554 \\
&= 0.0437
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
P(29 < X < 35) \approx 0.044
$$

**Note:** Using a software package for the approximation gives a probability of $0.0436$.

## The Normal Approximation as a Consequence of the Central Limit Theorem

According to the so-called **central limit theorem**, if $X_1, X_2, \ldots X_n$ are independent and identically distributed (I.I.D) random variables, then

$$
X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \approx N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)
$$

where $n$ is "sufficiently large," and

$$
\textrm{E}[X_i] = \mu, \qquad \textrm{Var}[X_i] = \sigma^2
$$

Now, if $X\sim B(n,p)$, then $X$ can be thought of as a sum of $n$ I.I.D Bernoulli random variables

$$
X_i\sim\textrm{Bernoulli}(p)
$$

where the PMF of each $X_i$ is given by

$$
{p,\begin{vmatrix}x = 1 \\ 1 - p,\end{vmatrix}x = 0; 0, \mid otherwise
$$

It can be shown that

$$
\textrm{E}[X_i] = \mu = p, \qquad \textrm{Var}[X_i] = \sigma^2 = p(1-p)
$$

Therefore, by substituting our values of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ into our normal approximation for $X$, we get

$$
X \approx N\left(np, np(1-p)\right)
$$

The "sufficiently large" condition on $n$ is taken care of by stipulating that $np > 5$ and $n(1-p) > 5$:

- When $p\approx 0.5$, then $n\approx 10$ is sufficiently large for the normal approximation to be applied.
- However, if $p$ is closer to zero or one, then the distribution of $X$ is not symmetric, and consequently, we need $n$ to be much larger for the central limit theorem to be valid.
