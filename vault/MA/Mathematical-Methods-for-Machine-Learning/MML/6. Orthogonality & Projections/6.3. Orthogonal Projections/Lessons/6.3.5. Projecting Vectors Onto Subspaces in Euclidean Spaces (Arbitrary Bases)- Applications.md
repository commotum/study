## Introduction

One use for finding the orthogonal projection of a vector onto a subspace is to find the shortest distance between the vector and the subspace.

Given a vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a subspace

$$
S = \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}
$$

the **distance between $\mathbf{x}$ and $S$** is given by

$$
\Vert \mathbf{x} - \textrm{proj}_{S}\mathbf{x} \Vert
$$

as shown in the diagram below.

![](<../Source/6.3.5. Projecting Vectors Onto Subspaces in Euclidean Spaces (Arbitrary Bases)- Applications/Images/459145fa2f16c9ee207677b449ffe98f.png>)

Let's find the distance between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace spanned by the linearly independent vectors $\{\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_2 \}$, where

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the columns of a matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

In this case, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; a_{1} \mid a_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$
Therefore, we get the following projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 4 & 9\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-4 \\ -10\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}9 & -4 \\ -4 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-4 \\ -10\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} = x^(^)
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, using the distance formula, the distance between $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace $S$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, x^(^)) &= ‖x - x^(^)‖ \\
&= \sqrt{(x_{1} - x^(^)_{1})^{2} + (x_{2} - x^(^)_{2})^{2} + (x_{3} - x^(^)_{3})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(0 - (-2))^{2} + (2 - 2)^{2} + (4 - 2)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{4 + 0 + 4} \\
&= \sqrt{8} \\
&= 2\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note:** Recall that the vector $\textbf{x}-\textrm{proj}_{S}\,\mathbf{x}$, whose norm $\| \textbf{x}-\textrm{proj}_{S}\,\mathbf{x} \|$ represents the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to $S$, is sometimes called the **vector rejection of $\mathbf{x}$ from $S$.**

## Calculating the Distance Between a Vector and a Subspace

**Example:** Find the distance between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace spanned by the linearly independent vectors $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the columns of a matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

In our case, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; a_{1} \mid a_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$

Therefore, we get the following projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 5 \\ -1 \\ 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 & 8 \\ 8 & 18\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 16\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}9 & -4 \\ -4 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 16\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-7 \\ 4\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ 5 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = x^(^)
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, the distance between $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace $S$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(x, x^(^)) &= ‖x - x^(^)‖ \\
&= \sqrt{(x_{1} - x^(^)_{1})^{2} + (x_{2} - x^(^)_{2})^{2} + (x_{3} - x^(^)_{3})^{2} + (x_{4} - x^(^)_{4})^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{(-3 - (-3))^{2} + (5 - 5)^{2} + (-1 - 1)^{2} + (3 - 1)^{2}} \\
&= \sqrt{0 + 0 + 4 + 4} \\
&= \sqrt{8} \\
&= 2\sqrt{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Angle Between a Vector and a Subspace

As well as finding the distance between a vector and a subspace, we can also use the vector's orthogonal projection onto the subspace to find the angle between the vector and the subspace.

Given a vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a subspace

$$
S = \textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}
$$

the **acute angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $S$** is the acute angle formed by $\mathbf{x}$ and its orthogonal projection $\textrm{proj}_S \mathbf{x}$, as shown below:

![](<../Source/6.3.5. Projecting Vectors Onto Subspaces in Euclidean Spaces (Arbitrary Bases)- Applications/Images/896ccca71bfaaec52b467ebfa538c2a7.png>)

Let's return to our previous example, where we had

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Previously, we found that the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ into the subspace spanned by $\{\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2 \}$ is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2 \\ 2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

We can compute the cosine of the angle between $\mathbf x$ and $\textrm{proj}_S \mathbf x$ using the dot product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos θ &= \frac{x \cdot proj_{S}x}{\mid \mid x \mid \mid \cdot \mid \mid proj_{S}x \mid \mid} \\
&= (0 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 2)/(\sqrt{0^{2} + 2^{2} + 4^{2}}\sqrt{(-2)^{2} + 2^{2} + 2^{2}}) \\
&= \frac{12}{\sqrt{20}\sqrt{12}} \\
&= \frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}{2\sqrt{5} \cdot 2\sqrt{3}} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\theta = \arccos\left(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt5}\right) \approx 39.23^{\circ}
$$

which is already an acute angle.

## Calculating the Acute Angle Between a Vector and a Subspace

**Example:** Find the acute angle between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace spanned by the linearly independent vectors $\{\mathbf{a}_1,\mathbf{a}_2\}$, where
$\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -3 \\ -3\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the subspace spanned by the columns of a matrix $A$ is given by

$$
\textrm{proj}_{S} \: \mathbf{x} = A(A^T\!A)^{-1}\!A^T\,\mathbf{x}
$$

In our case, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; a_{1} \mid a_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$
Therefore, we get the following projection:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3 \\ -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}3 & -3 & -3 \\ 3 & -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3 \\ -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}3 & -3 & -3 \\ 3 & -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3 \\ -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}27 & 9 \\ 9 & 27\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3 \\ -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{72}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-3 \\ -3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{12}\begin{bmatrix}3 & 3 \\ -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The acute angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $S$ is the acute angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\textrm{proj}_S \mathbf{x}$. Equivalently, it is the acute angle between $\mathbf{x}$ and any non-zero vector that is parallel to $\textrm{proj}_S \mathbf{x}$. So, to make calculations easier, we can find the angle between the vectors

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Computing the cosine using the dot product, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos θ &= (0 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0)/(\sqrt{0^{2} + 1^{2} + 0^{2}}\sqrt{(-1)^{2} + 1^{2} + 0^{2}}) \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{1} \cdot \sqrt{2}} \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\theta = \arccos\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}2\right)= \dfrac\pi 4
$$

which is already an acute angle, as desired.

## Finding the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector Onto the Solution Space of a Linear System

**Example:** Given the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the system of linear equations below, find the projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the solution space of the system.
$\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 2 \\ 14\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we need to find a basis for the solution space of the system.

Consider the augmented matrix $M$ of the system, which we reduce to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & -4 & 6 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} - 2R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, there is $1$ pivot column (the $1$st one). Thus, $x_2$, and $x_3$ are free variables. Now, from the first equation, we get

$$
x_1 = 2x_2-3x_3
$$

Therefore, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}2x_{2} - 3x_{3} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$

Consequently,

$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
is a basis of the solution space of the system.

Now, we let $A$ be a matrix whose columns equal the elements of $\mathcal B$, and we find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the solution space of the system as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{S}x &= A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}x \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot (\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix})^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 2 \\ 14\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}5 & -6 \\ -6 & 10\end{bmatrix}^{-1} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{14}\begin{bmatrix}10 & 6 \\ 6 & 5\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}10 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
