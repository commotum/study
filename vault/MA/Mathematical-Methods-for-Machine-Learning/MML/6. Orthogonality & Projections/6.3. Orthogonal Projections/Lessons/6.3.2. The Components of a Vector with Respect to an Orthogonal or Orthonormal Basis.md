## Introduction

Given an orthogonal basis

$$
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_n\}
$$

of

$$
S=\textrm{Span}\{\mathcal{B}\}
$$

there is an easy way to find the components of a vector $\mathbf{x}\in S$ with respect to this basis. The vector $\mathbf{x}$ with respect to the basis $\mathcal{B}$ is
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix}$

Furthermore, if the basis is *orthonormal*, then we know that

$$
\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{q}_i=1
$$

. So, the formula for each component is
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ ⋮ \\ x_{n}\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="finding-a-component-of-a-vector-with-respect-to-a-given-orthogonal-basis"></a>
## Finding a Component of a Vector With Respect to a Given Orthogonal Basis

**Example:** Given the orthogonal basis $\mathcal{B}$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ and the vector $\mathbf{x}$ below, find the value of $x_2$, the second component of $[\mathbf{x}]_{\mathcal{B}}$.
$\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of $\mathcal{B}$ as $\mathbf{q}_1,\, \mathbf{q}_2,\,\mathbf{q}_3$.

Since $\mathcal{B}$ is an orthogonal basis, we have that $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$ where

$$
x_i = \dfrac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{q}_i}{\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_i}
$$

Computing $x_2$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{2} &= \frac{x \cdot q_{2}}{q_{2} \cdot q_{2}} \\
&= (4 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 1 + (-2) \cdot (-4))/(1^{2} + 1^{2} + (-4)^{2}) \\
&= \frac{4 + 6 + 8}{1 + 1 + 16} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating the Components of a Vector With Respect to a Given Orthonormal Basis

**Example:** Given the **orthonormal** basis $\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \\ -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix}$ of $\mathbb R^2$, find $[\mathbf{x}]_{\mathcal{B}}$ if $\begin{bmatrix}-2\sqrt{5} \\ 3\sqrt{5}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's denote the vectors of $\mathcal{B}$ as $\mathbf{q}_1,\, \mathbf{q}_2$.

Since $\mathcal{B}$ is an orthonormal basis, we have that $\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$ where

$$
x_i = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{q}_i
$$

Computing the components of $\mathbf{x}$, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1} &= \begin{bmatrix}-2\sqrt{5} \\ 3\sqrt{5}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \\ -\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix} =-2 - 6 =-8 \\
x_{2} &= \begin{bmatrix}-2\sqrt{5} \\ 3\sqrt{5}\end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix}\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\end{bmatrix} =-4 + 3 =-1
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we have $\begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="the-orthogonal-decomposition-theorem"></a>
## The Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem

Given any vector $\mathbf{x}$ and any subspace $W$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can always write $\mathbf{x}$ as a sum of a vector from $W$ and a vector from $W^{\perp}$. This fact is known as the **orthogonal decomposition theorem.** We can state it formally as follows:

> *Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then each $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ can be written uniquely in the form
> $\mathbf{x}= \mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z}$
> where $\mathbf{y} \in W$ and $\mathbf{z} \in W^{\perp}$.*

> *Moreover, since $\mathbf y$ is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf x$ onto the subspace $W$, if $\{\mathbf{u}_1\,\dots,\mathbf{u}_p\}$ is any orthogonal basis of $W$, then*
> $y|= proj_{W}x; = proj_{u_{1}}x + proj_{u_{2}}x + ⋯ + proj_{u_{p}}x; = \frac{x \cdot u_{1}}{u_{1} \cdot u_{1}}u_{1} + \frac{x \cdot u_{2}}{u_{2} \cdot u_{2}}u_{2} + ⋯ + \frac{x \cdot u_{p}}{u_{p} \cdot u_{p}}u_{p}$.
