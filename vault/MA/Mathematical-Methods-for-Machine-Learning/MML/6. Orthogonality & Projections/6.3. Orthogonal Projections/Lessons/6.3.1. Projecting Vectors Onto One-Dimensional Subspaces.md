## Introduction

The **orthogonal projection** of a vector $\mathbf{x}$ onto a span $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ is the vector in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ that is "closest" to $\mathbf{x}$. Let's work an example to clarify what we mean by "closest."

Consider the following two vectors:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}5 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Geometrically, $\mathbf{x}$ can be written as the sum of a vector $k{\mathbf{a}}$ that lies in $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ and
another vector $\mathbf{z}$ that belongs to the orthogonal complement of $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$, as illustrated in the picture below.

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Projecting Vectors Onto One-Dimensional Subspaces/Images/15f9e2cdbaf7812381f739a8602f4674.png>)

The vector $k\mathbf a$ is called the **orthogonal projection** of $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$.

Symbolically, we can write the vector $\mathbf{x}$ as the sum

$$
\mathbf{x} = k \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{z}
$$

for some $k \in \mathbb{R}$ and ${\mathbf{z}} \perp \mathbf{a}$.

To compute the orthogonal projection $k \mathbf{a}$, we can first find the dot product of both sides of the equation above with $\mathbf{a}$ to get

$$
\begin{aligned}
x \cdot a &= (ka + z) \cdot a \\
x \cdot a &= k(a \cdot a) + z \cdot a
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{z}$, we have

$$
\mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{a} = 0
$$

As a result,

$$
\begin{aligned}
x \cdot a &= k(a \cdot a) \\
k &= \frac{x \cdot a}{a \cdot a} \\
k &= (5 \cdot 3 + (-1) \cdot (-6) + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 6)/(3^{2} + (-6)^{2} + 6^{2}) \\
k &= \frac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the orthogonal projection is

$$
ka = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -6 \\ 0 \\ 6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -2 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Note:** There is a shortcut! Instead of going through this process each time, we can compute the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ using the following formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{a}x &= \frac{a \cdot x}{a \cdot a}a
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-orthogonal-projection-of-a-vector-onto-a-one-dimensional-subspace"></a>
## Finding the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector onto a One-Dimensional Subspace

**Example:** Let $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 9 \\ -1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ Find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\text{Span}\{\mathbf{b}\}$.

**Explanation**

The orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{b}\}$ is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}a &= \frac{b \cdot a}{b \cdot b}b \\
&= (2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 9 + 0 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot (-1))/(2^{2} + 1^{2} + 0^{2} + 1^{2})b \\
&= \frac{4 + 9 + 0 - 1}{4 + 1 + 0 + 1}b \\
&= \frac{12}{6}b \\
&= 2b \\
&= 2\begin{bmatrix}2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 2 \\ 0 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-distance-between-a-vector-and-a-one-dimensional-subspace"></a>
## The Distance  Between a Vector and a One-Dimensional Subspace

We define the **distance** between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the vector space $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ to be
$\Vert \mathbf{x} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{x} \Vert$.

We can also define the **angle** $\theta$ between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the subspace $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ to be the acute angle between the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and its orthogonal projection $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\,\mathbf{x}$, as shown in the diagram below.

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Projecting Vectors Onto One-Dimensional Subspaces/Images/b9a288e6c87399d2d3fa6d772b9e4895.png>)

Finally, we now can represent the vector $\mathbf{x}$ as a sum of two orthogonal vectors

$$
\mathbf{x} = \underbrace{\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{x}}_{\large \mathbf{y}} + \underbrace{(\mathbf{x}-\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{x})}_{\large \mathbf{z}}
$$

where $\mathbf{y} \in \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ and $\mathbf{z} \perp \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$.

**Note:** The vector $\textbf{x}-\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{x}$, whose norm $\| \textbf{x}-\textrm{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{x} \|$ represents the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{a}$, is sometimes called the **vector rejection of $\mathbf{x}$ from $\mathbf{a}$.**

<a id="calculating-the-distance-between-a-vector-and-a-one-dimensional-subspace"></a>
## Calculating the Distance  Between a Vector and a One-Dimensional Subspace

**Example:** Let $\begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -4 \\ 2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}$ If $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{z}$, where $\mathbf{y} \in \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ and $\mathbf{z} \perp \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$, then find $\mathbf{z}$.

**Explanation**

A diagram of the situation is provided below:

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Projecting Vectors Onto One-Dimensional Subspaces/Images/1a8d9c2a40956965eaef1293fc216d15.png>)

First, we find the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\textrm{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{a}x &= \frac{a \cdot x}{a \cdot a}a \\
&= (1 \cdot 4 + (-1) \cdot (-4) + 0 \cdot 2 + (-1) \cdot 2)/(1^{2} + (-1)^{2} + 0^{2} + (-1)^{2})a \\
&= \frac{4 + 4 + 0 - 2}{1 + 1 + 0 + 1}a \\
&= \frac{6}{3}a \\
&= 2a \\
&= 2\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2 \\ 0 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, we get the following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= proj_{a}x = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2 \\ 0 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} \\
z &= x - proj_{a}x = \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ -4 \\ 2 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2 \\ 0 \\ -2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2 \\ -2 \\ 2 \\ 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that $\mathbf{y} \in \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$ and $\mathbf{z} \perp \text{Span}\{\mathbf{a}\}$, as shown in the picture above.

<a id="finding-the-orthogonal-projection-of-a-vector-onto-the-solution-space-of-a-system-of-equations"></a>
## Finding the Orthogonal Projection of a Vector onto the Solution Space of a System of Equations

**Example:** Given the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and the system of linear equations below, find the projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the solution space of the system.
$\begin{bmatrix}-10 \\ 20 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we need to find the basis of the solution space of the system.

Consider the augmented matrix $M$ of the system, which we reduce to row echelon form, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 4 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} \mid:=R_{2} - R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the reduced matrix above, there are $2$ pivot columns ($1$st and $2$nd). Thus, $x_3$ is a free variable. Now, from the second equation, we get $x_2=4x_3$.
Substituting this into the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1+4x_3+4x_3= 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x_1=-8x_3
$$

So, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}-8x_{3} \\ 4x_{3} \\ x_{3}\end{bmatrix}$
Consequently, $\begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 4 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ is a basis of the solution space of the system.

Finally, we find the projection of $\mathbf{x}$ onto the solution space of the system:

$$
\begin{aligned}
proj_{b}x &= \frac{x \cdot b}{b \cdot b}b \\
&= ((-10) \cdot (-8) + 20 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 1)/((-8)^{2} + 4^{2} + 1^{2})b \\
&= \frac{80 + 80 + 2}{64 + 16 + 1}b \\
&= \frac{162}{81}b \\
&= 2b \\
&= 2\begin{bmatrix}-8 \\ 4 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}-16 \\ 8 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
