## Introduction

**The chi-square distribution** with $k$ **degrees of freedom**, denoted $\chi^2(k)$, is the distribution of a random variable that is the sum of the squares of $k$ independent standard normal random variables.

Thus, if $Z_1, Z_2, \ldots, Z_k$ are mutually independent, where $Z_i\sim N(0,1)$, then
$Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\ldots+Z_k^2\sim \chi^2(k)$.

It can be shown that $X \sim \chi^2(k)$ has the following probability density function:

$$
{(1)/(2^{k/2}Γ(\frac{k}{2}))x^{k/2 - 1}e^{-x/2},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Note that $\Gamma$ is the gamma function.

The diagram below shows $f(x)$ for various degrees of freedom $k$.

![](<../Source/10.6.5. The Chi-Square Distribution/Images/7b9c194e8c3685f91c5b11f6a6f2053f.png>)

The chi-square distribution is widely used in inferential statistics. In particular, it is used in **goodness of fit** tests, where experimental data is compared against a hypothesized theoretical model. You will learn more about the applications of the chi-square distribution in future lessons.

## Computing a Probability for a Chi-Square Random Variable Using the PDF

**Example:** Given that $X\sim \chi^2(4)$, calculate $P\left(X < 5\right)$. Round your final answer to four decimal places.

*Hint: You may wish to make use of the following result:*

$\int_0^{5} x e^{-x/2} \, \textrm dx \approx 2.85081$

*The probability density function $\chi(k)$ is given by*

${(1)/(2^{k/2}Γ(\frac{k}{2}))x^{k/2 - 1}e^{-x/2},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise$.

**Explanation**

A chi-square random variable $X \sim \chi^2(k)$ with $k$ degrees of freedom has the following probability density function:

$$
{(1)/(2^{k/2}Γ(\frac{k}{2}))x^{k/2 - 1}e^{-x/2},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

We're given that $X\sim \chi^2(4)$. So, $X$ has the following probability density function:

$$
{(1)/(4Γ(2))xe^{-x/2},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Recalling that

$$
\Gamma(n) = (n-1)
$$

for any natural number

$$
n \geq 1
$$

we have

$$
\Gamma(2) = 1! = 1
$$

So, the probability distribution becomes

$$
{\frac{1}{4}xe^{-x/2},\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ 0,\end{vmatrix}otherwise
$$

Finally, we calculate

$$
P\left(X < 5\right)
$$

as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X < 5) &= ∫_{0}^{5}f(x)dx \\
&= ∫_{0}^{5}\frac{1}{4}xe^{-x/2}dx \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫_{0}^{5}xe^{-x/2}dx \\
&\approx \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2.85081 \\
&\approx 0.7127
\end{aligned}
$$

## Working With a Percentage Points Table

Let $X\sim\chi^2(3)$. Suppose we wish to know the specific value $x$ such that

$$
P(X\geq x) = 0.05
$$

Here,

$$
\alpha = 0.05
$$

is sometimes called the **significance level**. The required probability is represented by the shaded region below.

![](<../Source/10.6.5. The Chi-Square Distribution/Images/85033521377a1ae3b0c5eff3ae1d1361.png>)

We often resort to percentage points tables to look up our desired values rather than computing these probabilities by hand (which would be very difficult).

Some percentage points for the $\chi^2(3)$ distribution are given below. Each cell in the table gives a value $x$ such that

$$
P(X\geq x) = p
$$

for a particular value of $p$.

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.100$ | $0.050$ | $0.025$ | $0.010$ | $0.005$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $3$ | $\,6.251\,$ | $\,7.815\,$ | $\,9.348\,$ | $\,11.345\,$ | $\,12.838\,$ |

From the table, we have

$$
P(X \geq 7.815) = 0.05
$$

Therefore, $x = 7.815$.

## Using a Percentage Points Table To Find a Chi-Square Probability

**Example:** The table below gives the values of $x$ that satisfy $P(X\geq x) = p$, where $X\sim \chi^2(k)$. Given that the random variable $X \sim \chi^2(7)$, find the value of $x$ such that $P(X \geq x) = 0.99$.

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.995$ | $0.990$ | $0.975$ | $0.950$ | $0.900$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $5$ | $\,0.412\,$ | $\,0.554\,$ | $\,0.831\,$ | $\,1.145\,$ | $\,1.610\,$ |
| $6$ | $\,0.676\,$ | $\,0.872\,$ | $\,1.237\,$ | $\,1.635\,$ | $\,2.204\,$ |
| $7$ | $\,0.989\,$ | $\,1.239\,$ | $\,1.690\,$ | $\,2.167\,$ | $\,2.833\,$ |

**Explanation**

Since $X \sim \chi^2(7)$, we focus on the row of the table that corresponds to $k=7$.

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.995$ | $0.990$ | $0.975$ | $0.950$ | $0.900$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $7$ | $\,0.989\,$ | $\,1.239\,$ | $\,1.690\,$ | $\,2.167\,$ | $\,2.833\,$ |

From this row, we see that

$$
P(X \geq 1.239) = 0.990
$$

Therefore, our answer is $x=1.239$.

## Computing a Chi-Square Probability Using the Complement

**Example:** The table below gives the values of $x$ that satisfy $P(X\geq x) = p$, where $X\sim \chi^2(k)$. Given that $X \sim \chi^2(7)$, find the value of $x$ such that $P(X \leq x) = 0.005$.

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.995$ | $0.990$ | $0.975$ | $0.950$ | $0.900$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $5$ | $\,0.412\,$ | $\,0.554\,$ | $\,0.831\,$ | $\,1.145\,$ | $\,1.610\,$ |
| $6$ | $\,0.676\,$ | $\,0.872\,$ | $\,1.237\,$ | $\,1.635\,$ | $\,2.204\,$ |
| $7$ | $\,0.989\,$ | $\,1.239\,$ | $\,1.690\,$ | $\,2.167\,$ | $\,2.833\,$ |

**Explanation**

The table shows probabilities in the form $P(X > x)$. So first, we express the desired probability in this form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X > x) &= 1 - P(X \le x) \\
&= 1 - 0.005 \\
&= 0.995
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, since $X \sim \chi^2(7)$, we focus on the row of the table that corresponds to $k=7$.

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.995$ | $0.990$ | $0.975$ | $0.950$ | $0.900$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $7$ | $\,0.989\,$ | $\,1.239\,$ | $\,1.690\,$ | $\,2.167\,$ | $\,2.833\,$ |

From this row, we see that

$$
P(X > 0.989) = 0.995
$$

Therefore, our answer is $x=0.989$.

## Finding a Probability Involving Sums of Squared Standard Normal Variables

**Example:** Let the random variable $Y$ be defined as
$Y = Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\cdots + Z_{10}^2$
where $Z_i\sim N(0,1)$ for $1\leq i\leq 10$. Find the value $y$ that satisfies $P(Y\leq y) = 0.99$.

*Hint: The table below gives the values of $x$ that satisfy $P(X\geq x) = p$, where $X\sim \chi^2(k)$.*

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.010$ | $0.050$ | $0.100$ | $0.150$ | $0.200$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $8$ | $\,20.090\,$ | $\,15.507\,$ | $\,13.362\,$ | $\,12.027\,$ | $\,11.030\,$ |
| $9$ | $\,21.666\,$ | $\,16.919\,$ | $\,14.684\,$ | $\,13.288\,$ | $\,12.242\,$ |
| $10$ | $\,23.209\,$ | $\,18.307\,$ | $\,15.987\,$ | $\,14.534\,$ | $\,13.442\,$ |

**Explanation**

If $Z_i\sim N(0,1)$ for

$$
1\leq i \leq k
$$

are independent normal random variables, then the sum
$Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\cdots + Z_{k}^2$
follows a $\chi^2(k)$ distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.

So, in our case, we have

$$
Y = Z_1^2+Z_2^2+\cdots + Z_{10}^2 \sim \chi^2(10)
$$

First, we express the given probability in the form

$$
P(Y\geq y){:}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y \le y) &= 1 - P(Y \ge y) \\
0.99 &= 1 - P(Y \ge y) \\
P(Y \ge y) &= 0.01
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we wish to find the value $y$ such that

$$
P(Y\geq y) = 0.01
$$

This can be found from the given table:

| $k \setminus p$ | $0.010$ | $0.050$ | $0.100$ | $0.150$ | $0.200$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $8$ | $\,20.090\,$ | $\,15.507\,$ | $\,13.362\,$ | $\,12.027\,$ | $\,11.030\,$ |
| $9$ | $\,21.666\,$ | $\,16.919\,$ | $\,14.684\,$ | $\,13.288\,$ | $\,12.242\,$ |
| $10$ | $\,\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}23.209}\,$ | $\,18.307\,$ | $\,15.987\,$ | $\,14.534\,$ | $\,13.442\,$ |

So, we have

$$
P(Y\geq 23.209) = 0.01
$$

which means

$$
P(Y\leq 23.209) = 0.99
$$

Therefore, the value $y$ that satisfies

$$
P(Y\leq y) = 0.99
$$

is

$$
y=\bbox[3pt,Gainsboro]{\color{blue}23.209}
$$
