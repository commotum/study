## Introduction

Consider the quadric surface with the equation

$$
z = x^2 + y^2
$$

whose graph is shown below.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/2eedf88c4e37f9484211e572c42cd644.png>)

This surface is an example of an **elliptic paraboloid.**

The **vertex** of this elliptic paraboloid is located at the origin, and its **axis of symmetry** is the $z$-axis. Note that the axis of symmetry is the vertical line that passes through the vertex.

More generally, the equation of an elliptic paraboloid whose vertex is at the point $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ is given by

$$
z - z_0 = \dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are constants.

These surfaces are called *elliptic* *paraboloids* because their traces in the $xy$-plane are *ellipses*, while their traces in the $yz$- and $xz$-planes are *parabolas*. We'll discuss the traces of this surface in more detail shortly.

---

<a id="identifying-properties-of-an-elliptic-paraboloid"></a>
## Identifying Properties of an Elliptic Paraboloid

**Example:** Find the vertex and the axis of symmetry of the elliptic paraboloid $z= 4x^2 + 8(y + 2)^2$.

**Explanation**

The general equation of an elliptic paraboloid with vertex at a point $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is

$$
z - z_0 = \dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2}
$$

To find the vertex, we rewrite our equation as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z - 0 &= 4x^{2} + 8(y + 2)^{2} \\
z - 0 &= \frac{x^{2}}{1/4} + ((y + 2)^{2})/(1/8) \\
z - 0 &= ((x - 0)^{2})/(1/4) + ((y - (-2))^{2})/(1/8)
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us the equation of an elliptic paraboloid that has its vertex at $(0,-2,0)$. Also, from the equation, we conclude that its axis of symmetry is parallel to the $z$-axis.

The axis of symmetry should pass through the vertex of the paraboloid. Therefore, the equation of the axis of symmetry can be written as an intersection of two planes:

$$
{x = 0; y =-2
$$

<a id="hyperbolic-paraboloids"></a>
## Hyperbolic Paraboloids

Now consider the graph of the quadric surface

$$
z = x^2 -y^2
$$

whose graph is shown below.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/f9b5b1fd1c17b6177d5bd83b73efcbd8.png>)

This surface is an example of a **hyperbolic paraboloid** whose **saddle point** is at the origin.

More generally, a hyperbolic paraboloid whose saddle point is at $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ has the general equation

$$
z - z_0 = \dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2}- \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ constants.

These surfaces are called *hyperbolic* *paraboloids* because their traces in the $xy$-plane are *hyperbolas*, while their traces in the $yz$- and $xz$-planes are *parabolas*. We'll discuss the traces of this surface in more detail shortly.

The hyperbolic paraboloid has rotational symmetry of order $2$ about the $z$-axis. For this reason, we can refer to the $z$-axis as the **axis of symmetry** of the hyperbolic paraboloid.

---

<a id="identifying-properties-of-a-hyperbolic-paraboloid"></a>
## Identifying Properties of a Hyperbolic Paraboloid

**Example:** Find the saddle point of the hyperbolic paraboloid $z+1=\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{4} - (y-2) ^2$.

**Explanation**

The general equation of a hyperbolic paraboloid with a saddle point at $(x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is given by

$$
z - z_0 = \dfrac{(x-x_0)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(y-y_0)^2}{b^2}
$$

To find the saddle point, we rewrite our equation as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z + 1 &= ((x - 1)^{2})/(4) - (y - 2)^{2} \\
z - (-1) &= ((x - 1)^{2})/(4) - (y - 2)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us the equation of a hyperbolic paraboloid with a saddle point at $(1,2,-1)$.

<a id="alternative-orientations-of-the-axis-of-symmetry"></a>
## Alternative Orientations of the Axis of Symmetry

So far, we've encountered elliptic paraboloids whose axes of symmetry are parallel to the $z$-axis.
Let's now consider the cases when an elliptic paraboloid is oriented along the $x$- or the $y$-axes.

For an elliptic paraboloid, the other two cases are as follows.

- When the vertex is at the origin and the $y$ term is linear, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis:
$y = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{z^2}{c^2}$![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/42bd0bbe37a960f2b7f5ccd9e9ede76e.png>)
- When the vertex is at the origin and the $x$ term is linear, the axis of symmetry is the $x$-axis:
$x = \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} + \dfrac{z^2}{c^2}$![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/f524c5f0eeae3fa91e493880eb1ec985.png>)

Likewise, for a hyperbolic paraboloid, the other two cases are as follows:

- When the saddle point is at the origin and the $y$ term is linear, the axis of symmetry is the $y$-axis. The two corresponding equations are
$y = \dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{z^2}{c^2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad y = \dfrac{z^2}{c^2} - \dfrac{x^2}{a^2}$.
- When the saddle point is at the origin and the $x$ term is linear, the axis of symmetry is the $x$-axis. The two corresponding equations are
$x = \dfrac{z^2}{c^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad x = \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} - \dfrac{z^2}{c^2}$.

<a id="traces-of-elliptic-paraboloids"></a>
## Traces of Elliptic Paraboloids

The traces of an elliptic paraboloid are either ellipses, parabolas, a single point, or the empty set.

For example, let's find the traces with the coordinate planes for the elliptic paraboloid given by

$$
z=x^2+y^2
$$

- The trace of this surface in the $xy$-plane (when $z=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} + y^{2}|= 0$,
which corresponds to the origin $(0,0,0)$.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/011ffad0b50ce5f4a5720219b13bd1db.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $xz$-plane (when $y=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} + 0^{2}|= z; x^{2}|= z$,
which is a parabola in the $xz$-plane that has its vertex at the origin.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/50384f3c5af0a79fb8c056213cfc95d3.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $yz$-plane (when $x=0$) is given by the equation
$0^{2} + y^{2}|= z; y^{2}|= z$,
which is a parabola in the $yz$-plane that has its vertex at the origin.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/d6ead3dc517e82ab821189f8137b2eae.png>)

There are a few other cases we should note:

- The trace of our surface with the plane $z=-k$ for $k > 0$ is the empty set. This is because $z\geq 0$ everywhere on the elliptic paraboloid.
- The trace of our surface with the plane $z=k$ for $k > 0$ is an ellipse:
$x^2+y^2 = k$

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/078df5c1247d8ebeda4b0c788b901a4d.png>)

---

<a id="traces-of-hyperbolic-paraboloids"></a>
## Traces of Hyperbolic Paraboloids

The traces of a hyperbolic paraboloid are either hyperbolas, parabolas, or intersecting lines.

For example, let's find the traces with the coordinate planes for the hyperbolic paraboloid given by

$$
z=x^2-y^2
$$

- The trace of this surface in the $xy$-plane (when $z=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} - y^{2}|= 0; y^{2}|= x^{2}; y|= ± x$
which corresponds to two intersecting lines in the $xy$-plane.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/ee6bf19893dda6c98db96ba2625b4540.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $xz$-plane (when $y=0$) is given by the equation
$x^{2} - 0^{2}|= z; z|= x^{2}$,
which corresponds to the parabola in the $xz$-plane that has its vertex at the origin.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/1602f4c284e8eb06d90de3cc3c825361.png>)

- The trace of this surface in the $yz$-plane (when $x=0$) is given by the equation
$0^{2} - y^{2}|= z; z|=-y^{2}$,
which corresponds to the parabola in the $yz$-plane that has its vertex at the origin.

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/7ed879efe615366f75b69ec3ee9b3bdc.png>)

In this particular case, the **saddle point** is at the origin. It is a minimum point for the trace in the $xz$-plane but a maximum point for the trace in the $yz$-plane.

Finally, note that the trace of our surface with the plane

$$
z=k\neq 0
$$

is a hyperbola:
$x^2-y^2 = k$

![](<../Source/9.1.3. Paraboloids/Images/fbab75e39cc8f1d60373593eca4bd18b.png>)

<a id="finding-traces-of-paraboloids"></a>
## Finding Traces of Paraboloids

**Example:** The projection of the intersection between the hyperbolic paraboloid
$\dfrac{x^2}{4} - \dfrac{(y+5) ^2}{3} = z-1$
and the plane $x=2$ onto the $yz$-plane is a parabola. Find the vertex of this parabola.

**Explanation**

To find the intersection of the paraboloid and the plane $x=2$, we substitute $x=2$ into the equation of the paraboloid and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x^{2}}{4} - ((y + 5)^{2})/(3) &= z - 1 \\
\frac{2^{2}}{4} - ((y + 5)^{2})/(3) &= z - 1 \\
1 - ((y + 5)^{2})/(3) &= z - 1 \\
z &= -\frac{1}{3}(y + 5)^{2} + 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Projecting this onto the $yz$-plane, we get the equation of a parabola that has its vertex at $(-5,2)$.
