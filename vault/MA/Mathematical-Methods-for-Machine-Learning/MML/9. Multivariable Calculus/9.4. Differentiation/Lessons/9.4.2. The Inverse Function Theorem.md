## Introduction

Let's now state the so-called **inverse function theorem** for single-variable functions:

> *If $f: U \to \Bbb R$ is continuously differentiable on an open set $U\subseteq \mathbb R$, $f(a) = b$ and $f'(a)\neq 0$ for $a\in U$, then*
> - *$f$ is invertible in some neighborhood of $a$,*
> - *$f^{-1}$, the inverse of $f$, is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $b$, and*
> - *the derivative of $f^{-1}$ is given by*
> $\left(f^{-1}\right)'(b) = \dfrac{1}{f'(a)} =\dfrac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(b))}\,$.

To explain this concretely, consider the graph below:

![](<../Source/9.4.2. The Inverse Function Theorem/Images/2e4118d93f0c3090cbdf68dc967b5860.png>)

In this example, the function $f$ is continuously differentiable at $x=a$ and has a nonzero derivative. As a result, $f^{-1}$ exists in a neighborhood of $x=b$ and is continuously differentiable at this point.

On the other hand, if $f$ is continuously differentiable at $x=a$ but $f'(a) = 0$, then one of two things can happen:

- **Case 1**: The function $f$ does not have a local inverse at $a$.![](<../Source/9.4.2. The Inverse Function Theorem/Images/2cf8286334c183548e5cdb2595881fbf.png>)
In the example above, $f$ is not one-to-one in a neighborhood of $a$, so it's not invertible there.
- **Case 2**: The function $f$ has a local inverse at $a$, but its inverse is not continuously differentiable at $b$.![](<../Source/9.4.2. The Inverse Function Theorem/Images/e3de0e65978f9c53fb7f290a05785e31.png>)
In the example above, $f$ is invertible in a neighborhood of $a$, but $f^{-1}$ is not differentiable at $x=b$ since it has a vertical tangent at this point.

## Identifying True Statements Regarding the Inverse Function Theorem

**Example:** The function $f(x)$, whose graph is shown above, has a stationary point at $x=0$ and is continuously differentiable everywhere except at $x=-3$. Which of the following statements **must** be true according to the inverse function theorem?

![](<../Source/9.4.2. The Inverse Function Theorem/Images/af9f81048aa4f33fd0c1b0f79d35ca30.png>)

1. $f$ is invertible in some neighborhood of $x=-3$
2. $f^{-1}(y)$ is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $y=0$
3. The derivative of $f^{-1}(y)$ at $y=-1.1$ equals $\dfrac{1}{f'(2)}$

**Explanation**

The inverse function theorem for single-variable functions states the following:

> *If $f: U \to \Bbb R$ is continuously differentiable on an open set $U\subseteq \mathbb R$, $f(a) = b$ and $f'(a)\neq 0$ for $a\in U$, then*
> - *$f$ is invertible in some neighborhood of $a$,*
> - *$f^{-1}$, the inverse of $f$, is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of $b$, and*
> - *the derivative of $f^{-1}$ is given by*
> $\left(f^{-1}\right)'(b) = \dfrac{1}{f'(a)} =\dfrac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(b))}\,$.

With that in mind, let's examine our statements in turn.

- Statement I is false. Notice that $f$ is not continuous at $x = -3$. So, the theorem can't be applied in this situation.
- Statement II is also false. Notice that $y = 0 = f(0)$. However, the function $f$ does *not* satisfy the conditions for the inverse function theorem at $x = 0$ since $f'(0) = 0$. So, the theorem can't be applied in this situation.
- Statement III is true. Notice that $y = -1.1 = f(2)$, and the function $f$ satisfies the conditions for the inverse function theorem at $x = 2$. Indeed,$f$ is continuously differentiable on an open set containing $x=2$, for example, $(0,4)$, andthe derivative of $f$ is nonzero at $x = 2$, i.e., $f'(2) \neq 0$.Therefore, by the inverse function theorem, the derivative of $f^{-1}(y)$ at $y = -1.1$ equals $\dfrac{1}{f'(2)}$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."

## Critical Points of Transformations

Consider a transformation $\mathbf T$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}
$$

The points in the $(u,v)$ plane where $\mathbf T$ is *locally* non-invertible are known as **critical points**.

We've already seen that $\mathbf T$ is locally non-invertible when the Jacobian determinant is zero. Therefore, the critical points of $\mathbf T$ are the solutions to the equation

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)}=0
$$

Let's compute the critical points of the following transformation:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}
$$

First, we find the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} &= 4u & \frac{∂x}{∂v}|=-6v \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}|=-1 & \frac{∂y}{∂v}|= 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}4u & -6v \\ -1 & 1\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 4u \cdot 1 - (-6v) \cdot (-1) \\
&= 4u - 6v
\end{aligned}
$$

The function has critical points when the Jacobian equals zero. So, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= 0 \\
4u - 6v &= 0 \\
2u - 3v &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the critical points of $\mathbf{T}$ consist of those points that lie on the line $2u-3v=0$ in the $uv$-plane, shown below.

## Determining the Critical Points of a Transformation

**Example:** Find the locations of all of the critical points of the transformation $\mathbf{T}$, where $\mathbf T$ is defined as

$\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Given a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}
$$

the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$, denoted

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)}
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the locations of the critical points of $\mathbf T$, we need to solve

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)} = 0
$$

Here, we have

$$
x(u,v) = u^2-v^2
$$

and

$$
y(u,v) = u+v
$$

First, we find the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} &= 2u & \frac{∂x}{∂v}|=-2v \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u}|= 1 & \frac{∂y}{∂v}|= 1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}2u & -2v \\ 1 & 1\end{vmatrix} \\
&= 2(u + v)
\end{aligned}
$$

The function has critical points when

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)} =0
$$

So, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= 0 \\
2(u + v) &= 0 \\
u &= -v
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the critical points of $T$ consist of those points that lie on the line $u=-v$ in the $uv$-plane.

## The Inverse Function Theorem for Transformations

The inverse function theorem can be generalized to transformations too. We state it below:

> *Let $\mathbf T:U \to \Bbb R^2$ be a $C^1$ (continuously differentiable) transformation on an open set $U\subseteq \mathbb R^2$, where*
> $\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}$
> *Further, suppose that $(u_0,v_0)$ is **not** a critical point of $\mathbf T$.* Then,
> - *$\mathbf T$ is locally invertible at $(u_0,v_0)$,*
> - *$\mathbf T^{-1}$, the inverse of $\mathbf T$, is a $C^1$ function in a neighborhood of $(x_0,y_0)$, and*
> - *the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T^{-1}$, denoted $\dfrac{\partial (u, v)}{\partial (x, y)}$, is given by*
> $\dfrac{\partial (u, v)}{\partial (x, y)} = \left(\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)} \right)^{-1}$.

<a id="jacobian-determinants-of-inverse-transformations"></a>
## Jacobian Determinants of Inverse Transformations

**Example:** Consider all non-critical points of the transformation $\mathbf{T}:(u,v) \to (x(u,v),y(u,v))$. The inverse transformation $\mathbf T^{-1}(x,y)\to(u(x,y), v(x,y))$ is given by

$\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}$

Find the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ in terms of $u$ and $v$.

**Explanation**

Given a transformation

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u \\ v\end{bmatrix}
$$

the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$, denoted

$$
\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)}
$$

is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂x}{∂u} & \frac{∂x}{∂v} \\ \frac{∂y}{∂u} & \frac{∂y}{∂v}\end{vmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

In this case, we're only given $\mathbf T^{-1}$. So, we compute the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ as follows:

- First, we compute $\dfrac{\partial (u, v)}{\partial (x, y)}$, the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T^{-1}$.
- Then, we will compute the Jacobian determinant of $\mathbf T$ using the relation
$\dfrac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)} = \left(\dfrac{\partial (u, v)}{\partial (x, y)} \right)^{-1}$.

Here, we have

$$
u(x,y) = e^{x+y}
$$

and

$$
v(x,y) = e^{x-y}
$$

First, we compute the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{∂u}{∂x} &= e^{x + y} & \frac{∂u}{∂y}|= e^{x + y} \\ \frac{∂v}{∂x}|= e^{x - y} & \frac{∂v}{∂y}|=-e^{x - y}\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the corresponding Jacobian determinant is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(u, v))/(∂(x, y)) &= \begin{vmatrix}\frac{∂u}{∂x} & \frac{∂u}{∂y} \\ \frac{∂v}{∂x} & \frac{∂v}{∂y}\end{vmatrix} \\
&=\begin{vmatrix}e^{x + y} & e^{x + y} \\ e^{x - y} & -e^{x - y}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= e^{x + y} \cdot (-e^{x - y}) - e^{x + y} \cdot e^{x - y} \\
&=-e^{x + y} \cdot e^{x - y} - e^{x + y} \cdot e^{x - y} \\
&=-2 \cdot e^{x + y} \cdot e^{x - y} \\
&=-2uv
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we take the reciprocal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(∂(x, y))/(∂(u, v)) &= ((∂(u, v))/(∂(x, y)))^{-1} \\
&=-\frac{1}{2uv}
\end{aligned}
$$
