## Introduction

A square matrix $A$ is called **diagonalizable** if there exists a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ such that

$$
A = PDP^{-1}
$$

So, to **diagonalize** a matrix $A$, we need to find a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ that satisfy the equation above.

For example, let's diagonalize the matrix $A$, given by

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 & -6 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}
$$

To diagonalize a $2\times 2$ matrix, we follow a three-step **diagonalization algorithm.**

**Step 1:** Find the eigenvalues of the matrix.

We solve the characteristic equation

$$
\vert A - \lambda I \vert = 0
$$

For the given matrix, the eigenvalues are

$$
\color{red}\lambda_1 =2
$$

and

$$
\color{blue}\lambda_2 =3
$$

**Step 2:** Find a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of the matrix.

Following the usual method for computing eigenvectors, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 \\ -1\end{bmatrix}
$$

corresponding to the eigenvalues

$$
\color{red}\lambda_1 =2
$$

and

$$
\color{blue}\lambda_2 =3
$$

respectively.

Note that

$$
\left\{{\color{red}\mathbf{v_1}}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v_2}}\right\}
$$

is a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ since eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. This becomes important later.

**Step 3:** Construct the matrices $D$ and $P$.

Now that we have our eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors, we can construct the diagonal matrix $D$ and invertible matrix $P$.

The matrix $D$ has the eigenvalues of the matrix as its entries on the diagonal. For now, we can write them in any order. So, we let

$$
\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then, the columns of $P$ are the corresponding eigenvectors. We need to write each eigenvector in the *same* column as its respective eigenvalue in the matrix $D$. So, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A = PDP^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can check our solution by computing the product on the right-hand side:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix}6 & -6 \\ -2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 & -6 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix} \\
&= A✓
\end{aligned}
$$

## Diagonalizing a 2x2 Matrix Given Its Eigenvalues

**Example:** Consider the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ -5 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ You're given that $\lambda_1=4$ and $\lambda_2=3$ are the eigenvalues of $A$, and
$\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$ $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$ are the respective eigenvectors. Find the matrices $P$ and $D$ such that $PDP^{-1} = A$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix.

**Explanation**

Since the eigenvalues of the matrix are

$$
\lambda_1={\color{blue}4}
$$

and

$$
\lambda_2={\color{red}3}
$$

we construct the diagonal matrix $D$ by writing the eigenvalues in the main diagonal:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now, to construct the matrix $P$, we simply write the respective eigenvectors as columns:

- the $1$st column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_1={\color{blue}4}$, and
- the $2$nd column is the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda_2={\color{red}3}$.

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}
$$

<a id="diagonalizable-vs-non-diagonalizable-matrices"></a>
## Diagonalizable vs. Non-Diagonalizable Matrices

There is a theorem that states the following:

> *If $A$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix, then it is diagonalizable if and only if there exists a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ that consists of eigenvectors of $A$.*

We can break this down into two possible cases:

- If $A$ has two distinct eigenvalues, then it is indeed diagonalizable. This is because two distinct eigenvalues will give rise to two linearly independent eigenvectors (one for each eigenvalue). So, for a matrix $A$ with eigenvalues $\color{red}\lambda_1$ and $\color{blue}\lambda_2$ and corresponding eigenvectors $\color{red}\mathbf{v_1}$ and $\color{blue}\mathbf{v_2}$, we have
$[\mid \mid \mid; v_{1} \mid v_{2}; \mid \mid \mid]$
- If we get a double root $\lambda$ of the characteristic equation, the matrix will be diagonalizable *only if* we can find exactly two linearly independent eigenvectors corresponding to $\lambda$, which may or may not be possible. Another way of saying this is that the corresponding eigenspace $V_\lambda$ must be of dimension $2$.

<a id="identifying-whether-a-given-2x2-matrix-is-diagonalizable"></a>
## Identifying Whether a Given 2x2 Matrix Is Diagonalizable

**Example:** If the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & -5 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}$
is diagonalizable, find a corresponding diagonal matrix $D$.

**Explanation**

Since $A$ is triangular, the eigenvalues are the entries on the main diagonal.

So, the eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=3
$$

Since we get one repeated eigenvalue, the matrix might not be diagonalizable. To check this, we row-reduce the matrix $A-3I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 3I &= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -5 \\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}0 & -5 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has only $1$ pivot column. This implies that we will have only $2-1=1$ linearly independent eigenvector corresponding to the current eigenvalue. Therefore, $A$ is not diagonalizable.

<a id="the-connection-between-a-matrix-and-its-diagonal-form"></a>
## The Connection Between a Matrix and Its Diagonal Form

If $A$ is a diagonalizable matrix, then we can write

$$
A=PDP^{-1}
$$

We can write this equation in an equivalent form, making $D$ the subject.

First, we left-multiply both sides of the equation by $P^{-1}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= PDP^{-1} \\
P^{-1} \cdot A &= P^{-1} \cdot PDP^{-1} \\
P^{-1}A &= I_{2}DP^{-1} \\
P^{-1}A &= DP^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we right-multiply both sides of the equation by $P{:}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^{-1}A \cdot P &= DP^{-1} \cdot P \\
P^{-1}AP &= DI_{2} \\
P^{-1}AP &= D
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
A=PDP^{-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad D = P^{-1}A P
$$

<a id="diagonalizing-a-2x2-matrix-given-part-of-the-diagonalization"></a>
## Diagonalizing a 2x2 Matrix Given Part of the Diagonalization

**Example:** Consider the following matrices:
$\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$
Given that $D=P^{-1}AP$, find the value of $\dfrac{a}{b}$.

**Explanation**

From the diagonal matrix $\begin{bmatrix}5 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$ we deduce that the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_1=5$ and

$$
\lambda_2=-1
$$

So, the second column of $P$ corresponds to the eigenvalue

$$
\lambda_2=-1
$$

Now, we simply need to find an eigenvector that corresponds to

$$
\lambda_2=-1
$$

Computing $A-(-1)I$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - (-1)I &= \begin{bmatrix}1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

To solve the corresponding system of linear equations, we construct the augmented matrix $M$ and reduce it to its row echelon form using Gaussian elimination:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} = R_{2} + (-1)R_{1} \\
&= ∼\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced matrix above has one pivot column (the $1$st one). So, $x_2$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain

$$
x_1= -{2}x_2
$$

Therefore, the general solution is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}-2x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
$$

By setting $x_2=1$, we get the eigenvector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}a \\ b\end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally,

$$
\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{-2}{1} = -2
$$

<a id="diagonalizing-a-2x2-matrix"></a>
## Diagonalizing a 2x2 Matrix

**Example:** Given the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & -8 \\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$
find a diagonal matrix $D$ and an invertible matrix $P$ such that $D=P^{-1}AP$.

**Explanation**

We apply the diagonalization algorithm.

**Step 1.** We need to find the eigenvalues of the matrix. Since matrix $A$ is triangular, its eigenvalues are the entries on the main diagonal:

$$
\lambda_1=3, \quad\quad\lambda_2=-2
$$

**Step 2.** Now, we need to find an eigenvector basis of $A$.

- For $\lambda_1=3$, we have
$A - 3I|= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -8 \\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix} - 3\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -8 \\ 0 & -5\end{bmatrix}$.
To solve the corresponding system of linear equations, we construct the augmented matrix $M$ and reduce it to its row echelon form using Gaussian elimination:
$M|= \begin{bmatrix}0 & -8 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 & 0\end{bmatrix} \mid R_{2} = R_{2} + (-\frac{5}{8})R_{1}; ∼\begin{bmatrix}0 & -8 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$
The reduced matrix above has one pivot column (the $2$nd one). So, $x_1$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain $x_2=0$. Therefore, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}x_{1} \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
Setting $x_1=1$, we get the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}$
- For $\lambda_2=-2$, we have
$A - (-2)I|= \begin{bmatrix}3 & -8 \\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}; = \begin{bmatrix}5 & -8 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.
The above matrix corresponds to a homogeneous system of linear equations. Note, the matrix is already in row echelon form. There is one pivot column (the $1$st one). So, $x_2$ is a free variable. From the first equation, we obtain $x_1=\dfrac{8}{5}x_2$. Therefore, the general solution is
$\begin{bmatrix}\frac{8}{5}x_{2} \\ x_{2}\end{bmatrix}$
Setting $x_2=5$, we get the eigenvector $\begin{bmatrix}8 \\ 5\end{bmatrix}$

**Step 3.** We construct the diagonal matrix $D$ and the invertible matrix $P$ as follows:
$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2\end{bmatrix}$

<a id="uniqueness-of-the-diagonalization"></a>
## Uniqueness of the Diagonalization

Given a matrix $A$, the diagonalization

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; v_{1} \mid v_{2}; \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

is almost unique, but not quite.

When we construct the diagonal matrix $D$, we can put the eigenvalues in any order. So this matrix is unique up to permutations of the order of the eigenvalues. Note that by swapping the eigenvalues in $D$, we then have to swap the corresponding eigenvectors (columns) in $P{:}$

$$
[ \mid \mid \mid; v_{2} \mid v_{1}; \mid \mid \mid ]
$$

Also, since any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector as well, the eigenvector basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ can consist of these multiples instead. So the invertible matrix $P$ is unique up to multiples of its columns.

<a id="geometrical-interpretation-of-matrix-diagonalization"></a>
## Geometrical Interpretation of Matrix Diagonalization

Let $A$ be a diagonalizable $2 \times 2$ matrix. Therefore, we can write the matrix $A$ as

$$
A = P D P^{-1}
$$

where

- $P$ is an invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix whose columns are the *unit* eigenvectors ${\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2}$ corresponding to eigenvalues ${\color{red}\lambda_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\lambda_2}$, and
- $D$ is a diagonal matrix with ${\color{red}\lambda_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\lambda_2}$ on the main diagonal.

Now, suppose that $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^2$. Then, the image of $\mathbf x$ under the action of $A$ is the same as that of $\mathbf x$ under the action of the sequence $P^{-1} \to D \to P$. In fact, *every* vector in the plane is mapped to a new vector under this sequence.

Let's consider the effect of these equivalent actions on a unit circle, with the two unit eigenvectors (not necessarily orthogonal) forming a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

![](<../Source/5.2.1. Diagonalizing a 2x2 Matrix/Images/34719dc7612c5fe58a02f91bb0937432.png>)

Notice that multiplying $P$ on the right by the first and second vectors of the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$, we obtain the vectors ${\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2}$, respectively. Thus, $P$ represents a linear transformation of the plane that maps the standard basis $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{e}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{e}_2} \}$ onto our basis of eigenvectors $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \}$.

We can now describe the matrix decomposition $PDP^{-1}$ geometrically, as follows:

- The matrix $P^{-1}$ maps $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \}$ onto the standard basis $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{e}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{e}_2} \}$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- The diagonal matrix $D$ represents a scaling along the $x$- and $y$-axes by ${\color{red}\lambda_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\lambda_2}$, respectively.
- Finally, the matrix $P$ maps us back to the original space.

Note that since the eigenvectors ${\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2}$ are not orthogonal in general, the matrix $P^{-1}$ usually distorts the space. Therefore, the image of the unit circle under the action of $P^{-1}$ is not necessarily a circle.

This also can be interpreted in terms of changing the coordinates.

Since the columns of $P$ are the eigenvectors given in the standard basis, we have that $P$ is the change-of-coordinate matrix from the basis of eigenvectors $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \}$ to the standard basis $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{e}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{e}_2} \}$. Thus, its inverse, the matrix $P^{-1}$, is the change-of-coordinate matrix from the standard basis to our basis of eigenvectors.

We can now describe the matrix decomposition $PDP^{-1}$ in terms of changing the coordinates, as follows:

- The matrix $P^{-1}$ changes the coordinates of any vector of the plane from the standard basis $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{e}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{e}_2} \}$ to the basis of eigenvectors $\{{\color{red}\mathbf{v}_1}, {\color{blue}\mathbf{v}_2} \}$.
- The diagonal matrix $D$ scales the $1$st and $2$nd coordinates by ${\color{red}\lambda_1}$ and ${\color{blue}\lambda_2}$, respectively.
- Finally, the matrix $P$ changes the coordinates of the result back to the standard basis.
