## Introduction

Consider the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 5 \\ -1 & -4\end{bmatrix}$ How can we find its eigenvalues?

By definition, we need to find the value(s) of $\,\lambda\,$ such that

$$
\,A\mathbf{\color{blue}v} = \lambda \mathbf{\color{blue}v}\,
$$

is true for some *non-zero* vector $\mathbf{\color{blue}v}$. Let's re-write this equation as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av &= λv \\
Av - λv &= 0 \\
Av - λIv &= 0 \\
(A - λI)v &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The final equation above tells us that there should be at least one non-zero vector $\mathbf{\color{blue}v}$ that lies in $\textrm{Null}(A-\lambda I)$, the null space of the matrix $A-\lambda I$. As a result, the invertible matrix theorem tells us that the matrix $\, A-\lambda I \,$ must be singular, meaning

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = 0
$$

We've reduced the problem to finding the values of $\lambda$ that satisfy the equation

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = 0
$$

which is called the **characteristic equation** of the matrix $A$. So, let's solve it!

First, we write down the matrix $A-\lambda I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A- λI &= \begin{bmatrix}2 & 5 \\ -1 & -4\end{bmatrix}- λ\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \\
&= \begin{bmatrix}2- λ & 5 \\ -1 & -4- λ\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we solve the characteristic equation

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = 0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}2 - λ & 5 \\ -1 & -4 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)(-4 - λ) - 5 \cdot (-1) &= 0 \\
- 8 - 2λ + 4λ + λ^{2} + 5 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} + 2λ - 3 &= 0 \\
(λ + 3)(λ - 1) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda=-3$ and $\lambda=1$.

<a id="finding-the-characteristic-equation-of-a-matrix"></a>
## Finding the Characteristic Equation of a Matrix

**Example:** Find the characteristic equation of the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 \\ 3 & 2\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

First, we find the matrix $\, A-\lambda I$:
﻿﻿﻿$\begin{bmatrix}2 - λ & 4 \\ 3 & 2 - λ\end{bmatrix}$

Then, the characteristic equation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}2 - λ & 4 \\ 3 & 2 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(2 - λ)(2 - λ) - 4 \cdot 3 &= 0 \\
4 - 4λ + λ^{2} - 12 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 4λ - 8 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

## The Number of Real Eigenvalues of a 2x2 Matrix

The characteristic equation of any $2 \!\times\! 2$ matrix $A$ is always a quadratic equation, which implies that $A$ has at most $2$ eigenvalues. Here are the all possible cases:

- Two distinct real eigenvalues
- One repeated real eigenvalue (or, equivalently, two real eigenvalues that are the same)
- No real eigenvalues (or, equivalently, two complex eigenvalues)

---

<a id="computing-the-eigenvalues-of-a-matrix-with-two-distinct-eigenvalues"></a>
## Computing the Eigenvalues of a Matrix With Two Distinct Eigenvalues

**Example:** Find all real eigenvalues of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}10 & 3 \\ 6 & 7\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's start by writing the matrix $A - \lambda I$:
﻿﻿$\begin{bmatrix}10 - λ & 3 \\ 6 & 7 - λ\end{bmatrix}$

Now, let's find and solve the characteristic equation

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = 0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}10 - λ & 3 \\ 6 & 7 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(10 - λ)(7 - λ) - 3 \cdot 6 &= 0 \\
70 - 7λ - 10λ + λ^{2} - 18 &= 0 \\
λ^{2} - 17λ + 52 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the quadratic formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
λ &= (17 ± \sqrt{(-17)^{2} - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 52})/(2 \cdot 1) \\
&= \frac{17 ± \sqrt{81}}{2} \\
&= \frac{17 ± 9}{2} \\
&= 13, 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the eigenvalues are $\lambda=13$ and $\lambda=4$.

## Computing the Eigenvalues of a Matrix With At Most One Distinct Eigenvalue

**Example:** Find all real eigenvalues of the matrix
$\begin{bmatrix}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's start by writing the matrix $B-\lambda I$:
$\begin{bmatrix}4 - λ & 0 \\ 0 & 4 - λ\end{bmatrix}$

Now, let's find and solve the characteristic equation

$$
\det(B-\lambda I) = 0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(B - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}4 - λ & 0 \\ 0 & 4 - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(4 - λ)(4 - λ) - 0 \cdot 0 &= 0 \\
(λ - 4)^{2} &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the matrix $B$ has only one eigenvalue $\lambda=4$, which is a double root of the characteristic equation.

**Note:** We also say that $\lambda=4$ has an **algebraic multiplicity** of $2$.

## Calculating the Eigenvalues of a Triangular/Diagonal Matrix

**Example:** Find all real eigenvalues of the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 9 & -2\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}$

**Explanation**

Let's start by writing the matrix $A - \lambda I$:
﻿﻿﻿$\begin{bmatrix}3 - λ & 0 \\ 9 & -2\sqrt{3} - λ\end{bmatrix}$

Now, let's find and solve the characteristic equation

$$
\det (A - \lambda I) = 0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
det(A - λI) &= 0 \\
\begin{vmatrix}3 - λ & 0 \\ 9 & -2\sqrt{3} - λ\end{vmatrix} &= 0 \\
(3 - λ)(-2\sqrt{3} - λ) - 9 \cdot 0 &= 0 \\
(λ - 3)(λ + 2\sqrt{3}) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

So, we get the eigenvalues

$$
\lambda={\color{red}3}
$$

and

$$
\lambda={\color{blue}-2\sqrt{3}}
$$

If we look again at the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}3 & 0 \\ 9 & -2\sqrt{3}\end{bmatrix}$
we see that the eigenvalues are the entries on the main diagonal. This is true in general: the eigenvalues of a *triangular* or *diagonal* matrix are *always* given by the entries on the main diagonal!

**Watch out!** For a general matrix (which is neither triangular nor diagonal), this might not be the case!
