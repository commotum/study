## Introduction

Consider the graph of the absolute value function

$$
y= \mid x \mid
$$

below.

![](<../Source/2.4.2. Limits of Absolute Value Functions/Images/af350cf04e3707ab927ae4f7b97a9b25.png>)

From this graph, we see that as $x$ approaches $4$, the value of $y$ approaches

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid 4 &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\lim_{x\to 4} \begin{vmatrix}x & = & 4\end{vmatrix} = 4
$$

The absolute value is defined for all real $x$. As $x$ approaches any finite number $a$, the limit of the absolute value function can be obtained by directly substituting $x=a$ into the absolute value:

$$
\lim_{x\to a} \begin{vmatrix}x & = & a\end{vmatrix}
$$

Additionally, we can see from the graph that the limits at infinity are

$$
\lim_{x\to \infty} \begin{vmatrix}x & = \infty \qquad \textrm{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to -\infty} & x\end{vmatrix} = \infty
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-containing-an-absolute-value-by-direct-substitution"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit Containing an Absolute Value By Direct Substitution

**Example:** Find $\lim_\limits{x \to (-2)^+} \dfrac{15x}{\mid x^3+x \mid}$.

**Explanation**

Let's attempt to evaluate this limit by direct substitution. Substituting $x=-2$ into the limit, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → - 2^{+})\frac{15x}{\begin{vmatrix}x^{3} + x &} &= (15(-2))/(\mid (-2)^{3} + (-2)\end{vmatrix}) \\
&= \frac{-30}{\mid - 8 - 2\begin{vmatrix}} \\ = \frac{-30}{\mid - 10\end{vmatrix}} \\
&= \frac{-30}{10} \\
&=-3
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="the-definition-of-absolute-value"></a>
## The Definition of Absolute Value

Remember that the absolute value function can be defined in two different ways. First, we can define it as the following piecewise function:

$$
{x,\begin{vmatrix}x \ge 0 \\ -x,\end{vmatrix}x < 0
$$

Second, we can define it as the square root of a square:

$$
\mid x \mid = \sqrt{x^2}
$$

As we will see in the following examples, it is sometimes helpful to use all of these definitions together when evaluating limits.

---

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-containing-an-absolute-value-function-using-the-definition"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit Containing an Absolute Value Function Using the Definition

**Example:** Find $\lim_\limits{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{\sqrt{x^2}}{x}$.

**Explanation**

First, recall that

$$
\sqrt{x^2} = \mid x \mid
$$

Therefore, we can rewrite the limit as

$$
\lim_\limits{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{\mid x \mid}{x}
$$

Let's attempt to evaluate this limit by direct substitution. Substituting $x=0$ into the limit, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 0^{-})\frac{\begin{vmatrix}x &}{x} &= \frac{\mid 0\end{vmatrix}}{0} = \frac{0}{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is an indeterminate form.

Notice, however, that we only require the left-sided limit, i.e., $x\to0^{-}$. If $x < 0$, then

$$
\mid x \mid = -x
$$

Therefore, we can evaluate this limit as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 0^{-})(\frac{\mid x \mid}{x}) &= lim_(x → 0^{-})(\frac{-x}{x}) \\
&= lim_(x → 0^{-})(\frac{-x}{x}) \\
&= lim_(x → 0^{-})(-1) \\
&=-1
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we conclude that

$$
\lim_\limits{x \to 0^-} \dfrac{\sqrt{x^2}}{x} = -1
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-containing-a-radical-and-an-absolute-value-function"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit Containing a Radical and an Absolute Value Function

**Example:** Evaluate $\lim_\limits{x \rightarrow 1^+}\dfrac{\mid x-1 \mid}{\sqrt{x-1}}$.

**Explanation**

First, let's attempt to evaluate this limit by direct substitution. Substituting $x=1$ into the limit, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{\begin{vmatrix}x - 1 &}{\sqrt{x - 1}} &= \frac{\mid 1 - 1\end{vmatrix}}{\sqrt{1 - 1}} \\
&= \frac{\mid 0 \mid}{0} \\
&= \frac{0}{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is an indeterminate form.

Notice, however, that we only require the right-sided limit, i.e., $x\to1^{+}$. If $x > 1$, then

$$
\mid x-1 \mid = x-1
$$

Therefore, we can write this limit as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{\mid x - 1 \mid}{\sqrt{x - 1}} &= lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x - 1}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Direct substitution into the above still gives an indeterminate form

$$
\dfrac 0 0
$$

However, we can rationalize the expression and then evaluate the limit, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x - 1}} &= lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x - 1}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x - 1}}{\sqrt{x - 1}} \\
&= lim_(x → 1^{+})((x - 1)\sqrt{x - 1})/(x - 1) \\
&= lim_(x → 1^{+})((x - 1)\sqrt{x - 1})/(x - 1) \\
&= lim_(x → 1^{+})\frac{\sqrt{x - 1}}{1} \\
&= lim_(x → 1^{+})\sqrt{x - 1} \\
&= \sqrt{1 - 1} \\
&= \sqrt{0} \\
&= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-containing-a-quadratic-absolute-value-function"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit Containing a Quadratic Absolute Value Function

**Example:** Determine $\lim_\limits{x \to 2^-} \dfrac {\vert x^2 - 4 \vert} {x - 2}$.

**Explanation**

First, let's attempt to evaluate this limit by direct substitution. Substituting $x=2$ into the limit, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 2^{-})\frac{\begin{vmatrix}x^{2} - 4 &}{x - 2} &= \frac{\mid 2^{2} - 4\end{vmatrix}}{2 - 2} \\
&= \frac{\mid 4 - 4 \mid}{2 - 2} \\
&= \frac{0}{0}
\end{aligned}
$$

which is an indeterminate form.

To evaluate the limit, we factor the numerator and rewrite the limit, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned} \lim_{x \to 2^-} \dfrac {\vert x^2 - 4 \vert} {x - 2} &=\lim_{x \to 2^-} \dfrac {\vert (x+2)(x-2) \vert} {x - 2} \\[5pt] &=\lim_{x \to 2^-} \dfrac {\vert x+2\vert \cdot \vert x-2 \vert} {x - 2} \end{aligned}
$$

Now, since $x<2$, we have

- $\mid x+2 \mid = x+2$ and
- $\mid x-2 \mid = -(x-2)$.

Substituting the above into our limit and simplifying, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → 2^{-})\frac{\begin{vmatrix}x + 2 & \cdot & x - 2\end{vmatrix}}{x - 2} &= lim_(x → 2^{-})(- (x + 2)(x - 2))/(x - 2) \\
&= lim_(x → 2^{-})(- (x + 2)(x - 2))/(x - 2) \\
&= lim_(x → 2^{-}) - (x + 2) \\
&= - (2 + 2) \\
&=-4
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we conclude that

$$
\lim_\limits{x \to 2^-} \dfrac {\vert x^2 - 4 \vert} {x - 2} = -4
$$
