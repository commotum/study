## Introduction

Let's consider the differential equation

$$
y' = 2y(1-y)
$$

The slope field for this equation is shown below.

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/e011852a5c7a63fbe3129c27d532d9be.png>)

Let's see how much information we can gather about the differential equation by referring *only* to its slope field.

- First, note that the slope field has horizontal symmetry. Therefore, the associated differential equation must be of the form $y' = f(y)$ (i.e., it must be autonomous). Indeed, the equation $y' = 2y(1-y)$ is autonomous.
- Second, recall that $y=c$ is an equilibrium solution of an autonomous differential equation $y' = f(y)$ if $f(c) = 0$. Equilibrium solutions correspond to horizontal solution curves.In this case, there appear to be two equilibrium solutions, $y=0$, and $y=1$. Let's highlight these equilibrium solutions in our slope field.![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/b54d7067c6cac045633a0c799291c285.png>)
Note that $y=0$ and $y=1$ are indeed equilibrium solutions of $y' = 2y(1-y)$ since the right-hand side evaluates to zero on these lines.
- Third, the lines $y=0$ and $y=1$ split the plane into three regions. Let's sketch some solution curves, one for each region.![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/6224bf9662f0e85e1f720fdf55d2b24f.png>)
The slopes of the solution curves in each region are summarized in the table below.
Region$y < 0$$0 < y < 1$$y > 1$Slope$\searrow$$\nearrow$$\searrow$Sign$\color{red}-$$\color{blue}+$$\color{red}-$
The equation $y' = 2y(1-y)$ indeed exhibits this behavior. To see this a little more clearly, let $f(y) = 2y(1-y)$. A sketch of this function is shown below.![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/1fd1736e67e92587c25e9e4d8041d91f.png>)
Notice that the sign of $f(y)$ in the three regions agrees with the above table:$f(y)$ is positive for $0 < y < 1$, and $f(y)$ is negative for $y<0$ and $y >1$.

## Identifying an Equation That Generates a Slope Field Using Equilibrium Solutions

**Example:** Which of the following equations could have generated the slope field shown above?

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/ebef86d8bf088f7bfceaaed94999c6cd.png>)

1. $y' = y^2(y-1)$
2. $y' = y(y-1)(y-4)$
3. $y' = -(y-1)^2(y-4)$

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/fad2dd38b97e530e13888329b587e157.png>)

Notice that the slopes of the slope field are horizontal along the lines $y = 1$ and $y = 4$, as shown above. Therefore, the right-hand side of the differential equation should evaluate to zero on both of these lines.

With that in mind, let's check each equation:

- The equation $y' = y^2(y-1)$ does *not* give the required behavior. The right-hand side evaluates to zero at $y=0$ and $y=1$.
- The equation $y' = -y(y-1)(y-4)$ does *not* give the required behavior. The right-hand side evaluates to zero at $y=1$ and $y=4$, but it also evaluates to zero when $y=0$, which is not behavior exhibited by our slope field.
- The equation $y' =-(y-1)^2(y-4)$ gives the required behavior. The right-hand side evaluates to zero at $y=1$ and $y=4$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."

## Identifying an Equation That Generates a Slope Field Using the Sign of the Slopes

**Example:** The diagram shown above is the slope field for which of the following differential equations?

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/86ab6710d8a01fe1648d5b39a316ed5b.png>)

1. $y = -y^2(y+4)$
2. $y = y(y-4)^2$
3. $y = y(y+4)$

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/d4a4ac0f384c15b61fcaed3773ab2b76.png>)

Notice the following properties of the given slope field:

- First, the slopes of the slope field are horizontal along the lines $y=-4$ and $y=0$. Therefore, the right-hand side of the differential equation should equal zero on both of these lines.Of the given equations, the only equations that satisfy this condition are the following:
$y^{′}|=-y^{2}(y + 4); y^{′}|= y(y + 4)$
- Second, the lines $y=-4$ and $y=0$ divide the plane into $3$ regions. The sign of the slopes in each of the $3$ regions are easily deduced by considering the behavior of the solution curves in these regions. This information is summarized in the following table:
Region$y < -4$$-4 < y < 0$$y > 0$Slope$\nearrow$$\searrow$$\searrow$Sign$\color{blue}+$$\color{red}-$$\color{red}-$
Of the remaining options, the only one that gives this behavior is
$y' = -y^2(y+4)$.

Therefore, the correct answer is

$$
y' = -y^2(y+4)
$$

## Identifying an Equation That Generates a Slope Field Using the Sign of the Slopes and Factoring

**Example:** The diagram shown above is the slope field for which of the following differential equations?

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/68e980c93a3dde61e9b3777f266b0be6.png>)

1. $y' = y^3 - 4y^2 + 4y$
2. $y' = y^2 - 2y$
3. $y' = y^3 + y^2$

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/ba11bc7d92bbbefc467705ceeece8cea.png>)

Notice the following properties of the given slope field:

- First, the slope field is zero along the lines $y = 0$ and $y = 2$. Therefore, the right-hand side of the differential equation should evaluate to zero on these lines.Of the given equations, the only two that satisfy this condition are
$y'= y^3 - 4y^2 + 4y = y(y^2 - 4y + 4) = y(y-2)^2$
and
$y'= y^2 - 2y = y(y-2)$.
- Second, the lines $y = 0$ and $y = 2$ divide the plane into $3$ regions. The sign of the slopes in each of the $3$ regions are easily deduced by considering the behavior of the solution curves in these regions. This information is summarized in the following table:
Region$y < 0$$0 < y < 2$$y > 2$Slope$\searrow$$\nearrow$$\nearrow$Sign$\color{red}-$$\color{blue}+$$\color{blue}+$
Of the two remaining options, the only one that gives this behavior is
$y ' = y^3 - 4y^2 + 4y =y(y-2)^2$.

Therefore, the correct answer is

$$
y' =y^3 - 4y^2 + 4y
$$

## Identifying an Equation That Generates a Slope Field by Identifying Asymptotic Behavior

**Example:** Which of the following equations could have generated the slope field shown above?

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/0ef8e5a6169e508db61f76dfc5ec0e3a.png>)

1. $y' = \dfrac{y}{y+1}$
2. $y'= \dfrac1{y^2+y}$
3. $y' = \dfrac{2}{y+1}$

**Explanation**

![](<../Source/5.5.5. Analyzing Slope Fields for Autonomous Differential Equations/Images/e919936fc92a4b4110ef32705d009b5c.png>)

Notice the following properties of the given slope field:

- The slopes of the slope field become vertical as $y\to -1$.
- The slopes of the slope field become horizontal as $y\to\pm\infty$.
- The slopes of the slope field are positive for $y > -1$ and negative for $y < -1$.

With that in mind, let's consider each equation.

- The equation $y' = \dfrac{y}{y+1}$ does *not* give the required behavior. Notice that
$\dfrac{y}{y+1} = \dfrac{1}{\left(1+\dfrac1y\right)}\to 1 \quad\text{as} \quad y\to \pm\infty$.
Therefore, the slopes of the slope field for this differential equation do not become horizontal as $y\to\pm\infty$.
- The equation $y' = \dfrac1{y^2+y}= \dfrac1{y(y+1)}$ does *not* give the required behavior. The slopes of the slope field for this differential equation become vertical for *both* $y \to -1$ *and* $y \to 0$,
which is behavior not exhibited by our slope field.
- The equation $y' = \dfrac{2}{y+1}$ gives the required behavior. Notice that $y'\to 0$ as $y\to\pm\infty$, and $y'\to \infty$ as $y\to -1$. Also, the right-hand side of this equation is positive for $y > -1$ and negative for $y < -1$.

Therefore, the correct answer is "III only."
