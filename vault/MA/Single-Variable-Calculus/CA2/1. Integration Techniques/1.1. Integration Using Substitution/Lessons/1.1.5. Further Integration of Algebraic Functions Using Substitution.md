## Introduction

At first sight, the integral

$$
\displaystyle \int x \sqrt{x+2} \,\textrm{d}x
$$

might not seem like it can be solved using substitution because $x$ is not the derivative of $x+2$. But in fact, with some clever manipulation, we can indeed solve it using substitution!

Setting

$$
u=x+2
$$

and computing the required derivatives gives

$$
\frac{du}{dx} = 1⟹du = dx
$$

Substituting into our integral, we get

$$
\int x \sqrt{x+2} \,\textrm{d}x=\int x \sqrt{u} \,\textrm{d}u
$$

So we still have an $x$ in our integrand. However, we can get rid of it by solving for $x$ in the rule

$$
u=x+2
$$

We get
$x=u-2$,
and using this, the integral becomes

$$
∫x\sqrt{u}du = \mid ∫(u - 2)\sqrt{u}du; = ∫(u - 2)u^{1/2}du
$$

We can now solve the integral by multiplying out the product and integrating the resulting function with respect to $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫(u - 2)u^{1/2}du &= ∫(u^{3/2} - 2u^{1/2})du \\
&= ∫u^{3/2}du - 2∫u^{1/2}du \\
&= \frac{2}{5}u^{5/2} - \frac{4}{3}u^{3/2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

For the final step, we substitute

$$
u=x+2
$$

and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫x\sqrt{x + 2}dx &= \frac{2}{5}(x + 2)^{5/2} - \frac{4}{3}(x + 2)^{3/2} + C \\
&= \frac{2}{5}\sqrt{(x + 2)^{5}} - \frac{4}{3}\sqrt{(x + 2)^{3}} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating Indefinite Integrals of Algebraic Functions Using Substitution

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int x^3\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

Let

$$
u = x^2+1
$$

Then, we have

$$
\frac{du}{dx} = 2x⟹\frac{1}{2}du = xdx
$$

We can now solve the integral in terms of $u$, and then express our final answer in terms of $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫x^{3}\sqrt{x^{2} + 1}dx &= ∫x^{2} \cdot x\sqrt{x^{2} + 1}dx \\
&= ∫x^{2}\sqrt{x^{2} + 1} \cdot xdx \\
&= ∫x^{2}^(⏞)^(u - 1)\sqrt{x^{2} + 1} \cdot xdx^(⏞)^(\frac{1}{2}du) \\
&= ∫(u - 1)\sqrt{u} \cdot \frac{1}{2}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫(u - 1)u^{1/2}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫(u^{3/2} - u^{1/2})du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\frac{2}{5}u^{5/2} - \frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}) + C \\
&= \frac{1}{5}(x^{2} + 1)^{5/2} - \frac{1}{3}(x^{2} + 1)^{3/2} + C \\
&= \frac{1}{5}\sqrt{(x^{2} + 1)^{5}} - \frac{1}{3}\sqrt{(x^{2} + 1)^{3}} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating Definite Integrals of Algebraic Functions Using Substitution

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_0^{1/2}x(1-2x)^5\,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

Let $u=1-2x$. Then, we have

$$
\dfrac{\textrm d u}{\textrm d x} = -2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\dfrac 1 2 \,\textrm d u = \textrm d x
$$

We also note that

$$
x = \dfrac 1 2 (1-u)
$$

Computing our limits of integration, we get

$$
\begin{bmatrix}x & 0 & 1/2 \\ u & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

We can now solve the integral using our new variable $u$, remembering to use the new limits of integration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{1/2}x(1 - 2x)^{5}dx &= ∫_{1}^{0}\frac{1}{2} \cdot (1 - u) \cdot u^{5} \cdot (-\frac{1}{2})du \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}∫_{1}^{0}(1 - u) \cdot u^{5}du \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}∫_{1}^{0}(u^{5} - u^{6})du \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{1}{6}u^{6} - \frac{1}{7}u^{7}) \mid _{1}^{0} \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}([0 - 0] - [\frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{7}]) \\
&=-\frac{1}{4}(-\frac{1}{42}) \\
&= \frac{1}{168}
\end{aligned}
$$
