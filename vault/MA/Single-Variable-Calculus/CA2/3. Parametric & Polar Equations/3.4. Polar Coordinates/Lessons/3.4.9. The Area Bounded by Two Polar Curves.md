## Introduction

Consider the polar curves

$$
r=2(1+\cos\theta)
$$

and $r=2$. What is the area of the shaded region shown below?

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/6be6f7451afd7218d8e26c6ef9d40703.png>)

First, we find the values of $\theta$ for which the two curves intersect:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(1 + \cos θ) &= 2 \\
1 + \cos θ &= 1 \\
\cos θ &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us two solutions for

$$
\theta \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2},\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]
$$

namely

$$
\theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

To find the area, we use the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \Big(\big[ {\color{brown}\text{outer curve}} \big]^2 - \big[ {\color{blue}\text{inner curve}} \big]^2 \Big) \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

In our case, we have

$\qquad$

$$
\theta_1=-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

To determine the inner function and the outer, let's draw an arrow from the origin that passes through the shaded region.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/bf347e3bb8bec3ab59ad3157c1ec81cc.png>)

The arrow

- *enters* the region through the inner function $r=2$, and
- *leaves* through the outer function $r=2(1+\cos\theta)$.

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/2}^{π/2}((2(1 + \cos θ))^{2} - 2^{2})dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/2}^{π/2}(4(1 + 2\cos θ + \cos^{2} θ) - 4)dθ \\
&= 2∫_{- π/2}^{π/2}(2\cos θ + \cos^{2} θ)dθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Writing an Integral Expression For the Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves When the Intersections Are Given

**Example:** Find an integral expression that gives the area of the shaded region that lies between the polar curves $r=\cos\theta$ and $r=\sin\theta$, as shown below.

*Hint: In the first quadrant, the curves intersect when $\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$ and $\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.*

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/05dc5c7a7f9cbca8fd2f40c9a04bb513.png>)

**Explanation**

We are given that our curves intersect when

$$
\theta_1= \dfrac{\pi}{4}
$$

and

$$
\theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

To determine the inner function and the outer, let's draw an arrow from the origin that passes through the shaded region.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/86e425b440b00621226e1d907d744a28.png>)

The arrow

- *enters* the region through the inner function $r=\cos\theta$, and
- *leaves* through the outer function $r=\sin\theta$.

To find the area, we use the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \Big(\big[ {\color{brown}\text{outer}} \big]^2 - \big[ {\color{blue}\text{inner}} \big]^2 \Big) \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

where

$\qquad$

$$
\theta_1= \dfrac{\pi}{4}, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/4}^{π/2}(\sin θ)^{2} - (\cos θ)^{2}dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/4}^{π/2}(\sin^{2} θ - \cos^{2} θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{π/4}^{π/2} - \cos 2θdθ \\
&=-\frac{1}{2}∫_{π/4}^{π/2}\cos 2θdθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Writing an Integral Expression For the Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves

**Example:** Find an integral expression that gives the shaded area between the polar curves $r=2\cos(3\theta)$ and $r=1$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/3d05c927d111eedc08bab7334073ad3f.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider the rightmost petal, oriented along the $x$-axis. First, we find the values of $\theta$ for which the two curves intersect:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\cos (3θ) &= 1 \\
\cos (3θ) &= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us two solutions for

$$
3\theta \in \left[-\pi,\pi\right]
$$

$$
3\theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{3}, \dfrac{\pi}{3} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \theta= -\dfrac{\pi}{9}, \dfrac{\pi}{9}
$$

To determine the inner function and the outer, let's draw an arrow from the origin that passes through the shaded region.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/1020893864e360af2934a4146ccc9d6c.png>)

The arrow

- *enters* the region through the inner function $r=1$, and
- *leaves* through the outer function $r=2\cos(3\theta)$.

To find the area, we use the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \Big(\big[ {\color{brown}\text{outer}} \big]^2 - \big[ {\color{blue}\text{inner}} \big]^2 \Big) \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

where

$\qquad$

$$
\theta_1=-\dfrac{\pi}{9}, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{9}
$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/9}^{π/9}[(2\cos (3θ))^{2} - 1^{2}]dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/9}^{π/9}(4\cos^{2} (3θ) - 1)dθ
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Area of the Region Bounded by Two Polar Curves

**Example:** Find the shaded area that lies between the polar curves $r=\sin\theta$ and $r=2\sin\theta$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/40e3a6d7b23950b5748f6260b449b6ac.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we find the values of $\theta$ for which the two curves intersect:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\sin θ &= \sin θ \\
\sin θ &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us two solutions for

$$
\theta \in \left[0,\pi\right]
$$

$$
\theta = 0, \pi
$$

To determine the inner function and the outer, let's draw an arrow from the origin that passes through the shaded region.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/ae2a3907be1cfaadc1813f2383ecb437.png>)

The arrow

- *enters* the region through the inner function $r=\sin\theta$, and
- *leaves* through the outer function $r=2\sin\theta$.

To find the area, we use the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \Big(\big[ {\color{brown}\text{outer}} \big]^2 - \big[ {\color{blue}\text{inner}} \big]^2 \Big) \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

where

$\qquad$

$$
\theta_1=0, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \theta_2=\pi
$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{π}((2\sin θ)^{2} - (\sin θ)^{2})dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{0}^{π}3\sin^{2} θdθ \\
&= \frac{3}{4}∫_{0}^{π}(1 - \cos 2θ)dθ \\
&= \frac{3}{4}(θ - \frac{\sin 2θ}{2}) \mid _{0}^{π} \\
&= \frac{3}{4}[(π - (\sin (2π))/(2)) - (0 - \frac{\sin 0}{2})] \\
&= \frac{3π}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Area of the Region Bounded by Two Polar Curves With Multiple Intersections

**Example:** Find the **total** area of the shaded region that lies between the polar curves $r=2\cos(2\theta)$ and $r=1$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/b87d7ac0d1e1ec89d90a12760f9bb0d1.png>)

**Explanation**

Consider the rightmost pair of petals. First, we find the values of $\theta$ for which the two curves intersect:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\cos (2θ) &= 1 \\
\cos (2θ) &= \frac{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

This gives us two solutions for $2\theta \in [-\pi,\pi]$:

$$
2\theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{3}, \dfrac{\pi}{3} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \theta= -\dfrac{\pi}{6}, \dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

To determine which is the inner function and which is the outer, let's draw an arrow from the origin that passes through the shaded region.

![](<../Source/3.4.9. The Area Bounded by Two Polar Curves/Images/72cb3f8fc480fb7bcab25466c62f6c99.png>)

The arrow

- *enters* the region through the inner function $r=1$, and
- *leaves* through the outer function $r=2\cos(2\theta)$.

To find the area, we use the formula

$$
A = \dfrac{1}{2} \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \Big(\big[ {\color{brown}\text{outer}} \big]^2 - \big[ {\color{blue}\text{inner}} \big]^2 \Big) \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

where

$\qquad$

$$
\theta_1=-\dfrac{\pi}{6}, \quad
$$

and

$$
\quad \theta_2=\dfrac{\pi}{6}
$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{1} &= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}((2\cos (2θ))^{2} - 1^{2})dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}(4\cos^{2} (2θ) - 1)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}(2(1 + \cos (4θ)) - 1)dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫_{- π/6}^{π/6}(1 + 2\cos (4θ))dθ \\
&= \frac{1}{2}θ \mid _{- π/6}^{π/6} + \frac{1}{4}\sin (4θ) \mid _{- π/6}^{π/6} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(\frac{π}{6} - (-\frac{π}{6})) + \frac{1}{4}(\sin \frac{2π}{3} - \sin (-\frac{2π}{3})) \\
&= \frac{π}{6} + \frac{1}{4}(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - (-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})) \\
&= \frac{π}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, the total area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= 4A_{1} = 4(\frac{π}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}) = \frac{2π}{3} + \sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
