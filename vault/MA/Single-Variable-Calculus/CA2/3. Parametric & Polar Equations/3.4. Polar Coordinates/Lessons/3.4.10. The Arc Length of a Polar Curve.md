## Introduction

To calculate the arc length of a polar curve $r=r(\theta)$ between the rays

$$
\theta=\theta_1
$$

and

$$
\theta=\theta_2
$$

we can use the formula

$$
L = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \sqrt{r^2 +\left(\frac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta}\right)^2}\textrm{d}\theta
$$

For example, suppose that we want to find the arc length of the polar curve $r = 2$, where

$$
\dfrac{\pi}{6} \leq \theta \leq \dfrac{2\pi}{3}
$$

The required arc length is shown in blue in the picture below.

![](<../Source/3.4.10. The Arc Length of a Polar Curve/Images/436777524922a7b28fcf5514f62bd166.png>)

We will use the formula to compute the arc length. Since $r=2$, we have

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta} = 0
$$

and therefore, the arc length can be calculated as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/6}^{2π/3}\sqrt{2^{2} + (0)^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{π/6}^{2π/3}2dθ \\
&= 2∫_{π/6}^{2π/3}dθ \\
&= 2θ \mid _{π/6}^{2π/3} \\
&= 2(\frac{2π}{3} - \frac{π}{6}) \\
&= π
\end{aligned}
$$

## Creating an Integral Expression For the Arc Length of a Polar Curve

**Example:** Find an integral expression for the arc length of the polar curve $r=3-\sin(7\theta)$ for $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi$, shown in blue in the diagram above.

![](<../Source/3.4.10. The Arc Length of a Polar Curve/Images/9a98033ef7124397b87e336d2d7778e9.png>)

**Explanation**

The arc length $L$ of a polar curve $r=r(\theta)$ is given by

$$
L = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \sqrt{r^2 +\left(\frac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm{d}\theta
$$

Since

$$
r=3-\sin(7\theta)
$$

we have

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta} =-7\cos(7\theta)
$$

Therefore, the arc length is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{(3 - \sin (7θ))^{2} + (-7\cos (7θ))^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{9 - 6\sin (7θ) + \sin^{2} (7θ) + 49\cos^{2} (7θ)}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{9 - 6\sin (7θ) + \sin^{2} (7θ) + \cos^{2} (7θ) + 48\cos^{2} (7θ)}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{9 - 6\sin (7θ) + 1 + 48\cos^{2} (7θ)}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{10 - 6\sin (7θ) + 48\cos^{2} (7θ)}dθ
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-arc-length-of-a-polar-curve"></a>
## Finding the Arc Length of a Polar Curve

**Example:** Find the arc length of the polar curve $r=2(1-\cos\theta)$ for $0 \leq \theta \leq \pi$ (shown in blue in the picture above).

![](<../Source/3.4.10. The Arc Length of a Polar Curve/Images/7b8c89d9e599587ac6274b08d6202514.png>)

**Explanation**

The arc length $L$ of a polar curve $r=r(\theta)$ is given by

$$
L = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \sqrt{r^2 +\left(\frac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta}\right)^2}\,\textrm{d}\theta
$$

Since

$$
r=2(1-\cos\theta)
$$

we have

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta} = 2\sin\theta
$$

Therefore, the arc length is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{(2 - 2\cos θ)^{2} + (2\sin θ)^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{4 - 8\cos θ + 4\cos^{2} θ + 4\sin^{2} θ}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{4 - 8\cos θ + 4}dθ \\
&= ∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{8 - 8\cos θ}dθ \\
&= 2\sqrt{2}∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{1 - \cos θ}dθ
\end{aligned}
$$

We now use the half-angle formula

$$
1-\cos\theta =2\sin^2\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2}\right)
$$

to give

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= 2\sqrt{2}∫_{0}^{π}\sqrt{2\sin^{2} (\frac{θ}{2})}dθ \\
&= 2\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} \cdot ∫_{0}^{π}\sin (\frac{θ}{2})dθ \\
&=-4 \cdot 2(\cos (\frac{θ}{2})) \mid _{0}^{π} \\
&=-8(\cos (\frac{π}{2}) - \cos 0) \\
&=-8(0 - 1) \\
&= 8
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="derivation-of-the-main-formula"></a>
## Derivation of the Main Formula

We've been using the following formula to compute the arc length bounded by a polar curve:

$$
L = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \sqrt{r^2 + \left(\frac{\textrm{d}r}{\textrm{d}\theta} \right)^2} \:\textrm{d}\theta
$$

But where does this formula come from? Let's start with the familiar formula for the arc length of a parametric curve:

$$
L = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{\textrm{d}x}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2 +\left(\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}t}\right)^2}\textrm{d}t
$$

A polar curve is really just a parametric curve with parameter $\theta$ and parametric equations given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos θ, y = r\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Writing the arc length formula for a parametric function with parameter $\theta$, we have

$$
L = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \sqrt{\left(\frac{\textrm{d}x}{\textrm{d}\theta}\right)^2 +\left(\frac{\textrm{d}y}{\textrm{d}\theta}\right)^2}\textrm{d}\theta
$$

Since

$$
x = r \cos \theta
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dθ} &= \frac{d}{dθ}(r\cos θ) \\
&= \frac{dr}{dθ}\cos θ + r\frac{d}{dθ}(\cos θ) \\
&= \frac{dr}{dθ}\cos θ - r\sin θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Likewise, since

$$
y=r\sin \theta
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dy}{dθ} &= \frac{d}{dθ}(r\sin θ) \\
&= \frac{dr}{dθ}\sin θ + r\frac{d}{dθ}(\sin θ) \\
&= \frac{dr}{dθ}\sin θ + r\cos θ
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above into our expression for $L$, we reach the desired formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dx}{dθ})^{2} + (\frac{dy}{dθ})^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dr}{dθ}\cos θ - r\sin θ)^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ}\sin θ + r\cos θ)^{2}}dθ \\
&= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{(\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}\cos^{2} θ + r^{2}\sin^{2} θ + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}\sin^{2} θ + r^{2}\cos^{2} θ}dθ \\
&= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ) + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}(\cos^{2} θ + \sin^{2} θ)}dθ \\
&= ∫_{θ_{1}}^{θ_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2} + (\frac{dr}{dθ})^{2}}dθ
\end{aligned}
$$
