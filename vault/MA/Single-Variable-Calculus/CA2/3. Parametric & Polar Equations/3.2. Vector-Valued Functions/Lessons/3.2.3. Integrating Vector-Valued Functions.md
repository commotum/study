## Introduction

How do we integrate a vector-valued function? For example, if

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle e^{2t}, \, \sin{t}\rangle
$$

then how do we calculate

$$
\int \mathbf{r}(t) \,\textrm d t\,
$$

Integrating vector-valued functions is similar to differentiating them, in the sense that we just integrate each component individually. Doing that, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫r(t)dt &= ∫⟨e^{2t}, \sin t⟩dt \\
&= ⟨∫e^{2t}dt, ∫\sin tdt⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{1}{2}e^{2t} + c_{1},-\cos t + c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{1}{2}e^{2t},-\cos t⟩ + ⟨c_{1}, c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{1}{2}e^{2t},-\cos t⟩ + C
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\mathbf C = \langle c_1,\, c_2\rangle
$$

is an arbitrary (vector) constant of integration.

## Finding the Antiderivative of a Vector-Valued Function

**Example:** Find $\mathbf F(t)$ given that $\mathbf F'(t)= \langle 6t^5+2t, \sec^2 t\rangle$.

**Explanation**

Note that

$$
\mathbf F(t)=\int \mathbf F'(t)\, \textrm d t=\int \left\langle 6t^5+2t,\, \sec^2 t\right\rangle \, \textrm d t
$$

To integrate a vector-valued function, we integrate each component separately. So, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫⟨6t^{5} + 2t, \sec^{2} t⟩dt &= ⟨∫(6t^{5} + 2t)dt, ∫\sec^{2} tdt⟩ \\
&= ⟨t^{6} + t^{2} + c_{1}, \tan t + c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨t^{6} + t^{2}, \tan t⟩ + ⟨c_{1}, c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨t^{6} + t^{2}, \tan t⟩ + C
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf C$ is an arbitrary (vector) constant of integration.

## Integrating a Vector-Valued Function

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \left\langle\cos(2t+1), \, 4\right\rangle \,\textrm d t\,$.

**Explanation**

To integrate a vector-valued function, we integrate each component separately:

$$
∫⟨\cos (2t + 1), 4⟩dt = ⟨∫\cos (2t + 1)dt, ∫4dt⟩
$$

- For $x(t) =\cos(2t+1)$, we calculate its integral using substitution. So, we let $u= 2t +1$. Then
$\dfrac{\textrm d u}{\textrm d t} = 2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm d t = \dfrac 1 2 \textrm d u$.
Therefore, we have
$∫x(t)dt|= ∫\cos (2t + 1)dt; = ∫\cos u \cdot \frac{1}{2}du; = \frac{1}{2}∫\cos udu; = \frac{1}{2}(\sin u) + c_{1}; = \frac{1}{2}\sin (2t + 1) + c_{1}$.
- For $y(t) =4$, we have
$∫y(t)dt = ∫4dt = 4t + c_{2}$.

Finally, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫⟨\cos (2t + 1), 4⟩dt &= ⟨\frac{1}{2}\sin (2t + 1) + c_{1}, 4t + c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{1}{2}\sin (2t + 1), 4t⟩ + ⟨c_{1}, c_{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨\frac{1}{2}\sin (2t + 1), 4t⟩ + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Definite Integral of a Vector-Valued Function

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_0^{2} \left\langle 2e^{t},\, 6t^2\right\rangle\, \textrm d t$.

**Explanation**

To integrate a vector-valued function, we integrate each component separately:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{0}^{2}⟨2e^{t}, 6t^{2}⟩dt &= ⟨∫_{0}^{2}2e^{t}dt, ∫_{0}^{2}6t^{2}dt⟩. \\
&= ⟨2e^{t} \mid _{0}^{2}, 2t^{3} \mid _{0}^{2}⟩ \\
&= ⟨[2e^{2} - 2e^{0}], [2 \cdot 2^{3} - 0]⟩ \\
&= ⟨2e^{2} - 2, 16⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="finding-the-antiderivative-of-a-vector-valued-function-that-satisfies-a-given-initial-condition"></a>
## Finding the Antiderivative of a Vector-Valued Function That Satisfies a Given Initial Condition

**Example:** Calculate $\mathbf F(t)$ given that $\mathbf F'(t)= \left\langle \sec t \tan t, \, \sin(2t)\right\rangle$ and $\mathbf F(0)=\langle 0,\, 0\rangle$.

**Explanation**

Note that

$$
\mathbf F(t)=\int \mathbf F'(t)\, \textrm d t=\int \left\langle \sec t \tan t,\, \sin(2t)\right\rangle \, \textrm d t
$$

To integrate a vector-valued function, we integrate each component separately. So, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫⟨\sec t\tan t, \sin (2t)⟩dt &= ⟨∫\sec t\tan tdt, ∫\sin (2t)dt⟩ \\
&= ⟨\sec t + c_{1},-\frac{1}{2}\cos (2t) + c_{2}⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

The initial condition

$$
\mathbf F(0)=\left\langle 0,\, 0 \right\rangle
$$

gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
F(0) = ⟨\sec 0 + c_{1},-\frac{1}{2}\cos (2 \cdot 0) + c_{2}⟩ &= ⟨0, 0⟩ \\
⟨1 + c_{1},-\frac{1}{2} + c_{2}⟩ &= ⟨0, 0⟩
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, $c_1=-1$ and

$$
c_2=\dfrac 12
$$

Consequently,

$$
\mathbf F(t)=\left\langle \sec t - 1, \, -\dfrac 1 2\cos(2t)+ \dfrac12\right\rangle
$$
