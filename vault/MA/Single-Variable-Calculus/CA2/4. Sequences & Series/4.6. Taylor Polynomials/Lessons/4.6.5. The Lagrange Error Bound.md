## Introduction

Suppose that we want to *approximate*

$$
f(x) = e^x
$$

for some value of $x$. We can do this using the second-degree Taylor polynomial about a point $x=a$. For example, the second-degree Taylor polynomial for $e^x$ about the point $x=1$ is shown below:

$$
e^x \approx P_2(x) = e + e(x-1) + \dfrac 1 2 e(x-1)^2
$$

In general, the polynomial $P_2(x)$ is only an approximation to the function $f(x)$, which means that there will usually be some error whenever we use $P_2$ to approximate $f$. We want to know how bad this error can get. Can we find an upper bound for the error?

Let $R_2(x)$ be the **error** (or **remainder**) that we get when we use $P_2$ to approximate

$$
f(x)=e^x
$$

so

$$
R_2(x) = e^x - P_2(x)
$$

The **Lagrange error bound** states that an upper bound for $R_2$ is given by,

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_2 & \leq \dfrac{M}{3!} & x-a\end{vmatrix}^{3}
$$

where $M$ the maximum value of $\mid f^{(3)}(x) \mid$ on the interval between $a$ and $x$.

In our case, since our Taylor polynomial is taken about the point $a=1$, the Lagrange error bound becomes

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_2 & \leq \dfrac{M}{3!} & x-1\end{vmatrix}^{3}
$$

Notice that the Lagrange error bound looks a lot like the last term of the *next* Taylor polynomial, $P_3(x)$.

The tricky part is finding the value of $M$. We'll learn how to do that in the following example.

## Finding an Upper Bound for the Error Using the Second-Degree Taylor Polynomial

**Example:** Let $f(x)=e^{x}$ and let $P_2(x)$ be the second-degree Taylor polynomial of $f(x)$ about $x=1$. Use the Lagrange error bound to find an upper bound for the error when $P_2(x)$ is used to approximate $e^{1.1}$.

**Explanation**

The Lagrange error bound states that an upper bound for the error $R_2$ obtained when using the second-degree Taylor polynomial $P_2$ to approximate $f(x)$ is given by

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_2 & \leq \dfrac{M}{3!} & x-1\end{vmatrix}^{3}
$$

where $M$ is the maximum value of $\mid f^{(3)}(x) \mid$ on the interval between $a=1$ and $x=1.1$. Plugging $x=1.1$ into the above gives

$$
\mid R_{2} \mid \mid \le \frac{M}{3!} \mid 1.1 - 1 \mid ^{3}; \mid R_{2} \mid \mid \le \frac{M}{6} \mid 0.1 \mid ^{3}
$$

To find $M$, we need to compute $\mid f^{(3)}(x) \mid$ and then maximize it on the interval $[1,1.1]$. Taking the derivatives, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= e^{x} \\
f^{′}(x) &= e^{x} \\
f^{″}(x) &= e^{x} \\
f^{(3)}(x) &= e^{x} \\
\begin{vmatrix}f^{(3)}(x) & &= & e^{x}\end{vmatrix} \\
&= e^{x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid f^{(3)}(x) &= e^x
\end{aligned}
$$

is an increasing function on the interval $[1,1.1]$, its value is greatest at $x=1.1$. So

$$
M = \mid f^{(3)}(1.1) \mid =e^{1.1}
$$

and therefore, the upper bound is

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_2 & \leq \dfrac{e^{1.1}}{6} & 0.1\end{vmatrix}^{3} \approx 0.000\,501
$$

rounded to six decimal places.

## The General Result of the Lagrange Error Bound

The Lagrange error bound that we've discussed so far is valid only for the error associated with a second-degree Taylor polynomial. But we can generalize the result to higher-order Taylor polynomials.

Let $R_n(x)$ be the error involved in approximating a function $f(x)$ by its $n$th-degree Taylor polynomial $P_n(x)$ about $x=a$. So, we have

$$
R_n(x) = f(x) - P_n(x)
$$

Then the Lagrange error bound for $R_n$ is given by

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_n(x) & \leq \dfrac{M}{(n+1)!} & x-a\end{vmatrix}^{n+1}
$$

where $M$ is the maximum value of $\mid f^{(n+1)} \mid$ on the interval between $a$ and $x$.

## Finding an Upper Bound for the Error Using the Third-Degree Taylor Polynomial

**Example:** Let $f(x)=\ln(1-x)$ and let $P_3(x)$ be the third-degree Taylor polynomial of $f(x)$ about $x=0$. Use the Lagrange error bound to find an upper bound for the error when $P_3(x)$ is used to approximate $\ln(0.9)$

**Explanation**

The Lagrange error bound states that an upper bound for the error $R_3$ obtained when using the third-degree Taylor polynomial $P_3$ to approximate $f(x)$ is given by

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_3 & \leq \dfrac{M}{4!} & x-0\end{vmatrix}^{4}
$$

where $M$ is the maximum value of $\mid f^{(4)}(x) \mid$ on the interval between $a=0$ and $x=0.1$. Plugging $x=0.1$ into the above gives

$$
\mid R_{3} \mid \mid \le \frac{M}{4!} \mid 0.1 - 0 \mid ^{4}; \mid R_{3} \mid \mid \le \frac{M}{24} \mid 0.1 \mid ^{4}
$$

To find $M$, we need to compute $\mid f^{(4)}(x) \mid$ and then maximize it on the interval $[0,0.1]$. Taking the derivatives, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{′}(x) &= \frac{1}{x - 1} \\
f^{″}(x) &= - (1)/((x - 1)^{2}) \\
f^{‴}(x) &= (2)/((x - 1)^{3}) \\
f^{(4)}(x) &= - (6)/((x - 1)^{4}) \\
\begin{vmatrix}f^{(4)}(x) & &= & - (6)/((x - 1)^{4})\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (6)/((x - 1)^{4})
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid f^{(4)}(x) &= \dfrac{6}{(x-1)^4}
\end{aligned}
$$

is an increasing function on the interval $[0,0.1]$, its value is greatest at $x=0.1$. So

$$
M = \mid f^{(4)}(0.1) \mid =\dfrac{6}{(0.1-1)^4} = \dfrac{6}{(0.9)^4}
$$

and therefore, the upper bound is

$$
\begin{vmatrix}R_3 & \leq \dfrac{\left(\dfrac{6}{(0.9)^4} \right)}{24} & 0.1\end{vmatrix}^{4} \approx 0.000\,038
$$

rounded to six decimal places.
