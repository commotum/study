## Introduction

Suppose we want to find the area bounded between the curves

$$
f(y)=3-y^2
$$

and $g(y)=-1$.

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/728aa72779438761585e3bbea728bedf.png>)

We can adopt the same approach that we used for functions of $x$. The only difference is that now we're working with functions of $y$. So we write the integral in terms of $y$, and we subtract the left curve from the right curve.

So, the area $A$ of the region is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{c}^{d}[(right function) - (left function)]dy
\end{aligned}
$$

First, we find for which values of $y$ the curves intersect. In our case, the intersections of the curves are given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(y) &= g(y) \\
3 - y^{2} &= -1 \\
y^{2} &= 4 \\
y &= ± 2
\end{aligned}
$$

The right function is

$$
f(y) = 3-y^2
$$

and the left function is $g(y)=-1$. So the area that we want can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{-2}^{2}(f(y) - g(y))dy \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}((3 - y^{2}) - (-1))dy \\
&= ∫_{-2}^{2}(4 - y^{2})dy
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluating the integral, we get

$$
A = \dfrac{32}{3}
$$

**Note:** If you're not sure which is the right function and which is the left, one way to find out is to consider some $y$ between the bounds $y=-2$ and $y=2$, and draw a horizontal line that passes through $y$ and points in the positive direction along the $x$-axis, as shown below.

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/9d6742a4b7bdded128495855c639ce94.png>)

The horizontal line always *enters* the region through the left function and *leaves* through the right function.

## Expressing the Area Between Two Curves as an Integral Given the Limits of Integration

**Example:** What integral gives the area of the finite region bounded between the curves $x=1-y^2$ and $x=1-2y+y^2$?

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/f0e79c3e1af4f905df19db0858250857.png>)

**Explanation**

From the diagram, we see that the points of intersection between the two functions occur when $y=0$ and $y=1$. So these will be our limits of integration.

To determine which is the left function and which is the right, let's draw our horizontal arrow (from left to right).

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/6172e99c5ba44503dca0cb0a923dc5f2.png>)

The horizontal line

- *enters* the region through the left function $x=1-2y+y^2$, and
- *leaves* through the right function $x=1-y^2$.

Therefore, the integral that gives our area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{0}^{1}[(right function) - (left function)]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}[(1 - y^{2}) - (1 - 2y + y^{2})]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{1}(-2y^{2} + 2y)dy
\end{aligned}
$$

## Expressing the Area Between Two Curves as an Integral by First Finding the Limits of Integration

**Example:** Which integral gives the area of the finite region bounded by the curves $x=y^4$, $x=6-y^2$, and the $x$-axis?

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/ae24b5c5f50d3595fc564d5b89d3df05.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we want to determine the limits of integration. To do this, we equate the two functions and solve for $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^{4} &= 6 - y^{2} \\
y^{4} + y^{2} - 6 &= 0 \\
(y^{2} + 3)(y^{2} - 2) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Since

$$
y^2+3=0
$$

has no real solutions, we get only

$$
y=\sqrt{2}
$$

Hence, we need to integrate from $y=0$ to

$$
y=\sqrt{2}
$$

To determine which is the left function and which is the right, let's draw our horizontal arrow (from left to right).

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/10ba8fb6194628facf1541ce8eb3ffd0.png>)

The horizontal line

- *enters* the region through the left function $x=y^4$, and
- *leaves* through the right function $x=6- y^2$.

Therefore, the integral that gives our area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{c}^{d}[(right function) - (left function)]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{\sqrt{2}}[(6 - y^{2}) - (y^{4})]dy \\
&= ∫_{0}^{\sqrt{2}}(-y^{4} - y^{2} + 6)dy
\end{aligned}
$$

## Calculating the Area Between Two Curves Expressed as Functions of Y

**Example:** Calculate the area of the finite region bounded between the curves $x=9-2y^2$ and $x=y^2$?

**Explanation**

First, let's draw the graphs of the two functions:

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/12189f5c29ef4a8613b28c2acf308928.png>)

*(Note: Figure not drawn to scale)*

Now, we want to determine the limits of integration. To do this, we equate the two functions and solve for $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^{2} &= 9 - 2y^{2} \\
3y^{2} &= 9 \\
y^{2} &= 3 \\
y &= ± \sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, we need to integrate from

$$
y=-\sqrt 3
$$

to

$$
y=\sqrt 3
$$

To determine which is the left function and which is the right, let's draw our horizontal arrow (from left to right).

![](<../Source/2.1.5. The Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of Y/Images/310b636da18f248542719b1cc6c075b1.png>)

The horizontal line

- *enters* the region through the left function $x=y^2$, and
- *leaves* through the right function $x=9-2y^2$.

Therefore, we can find the area as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= ∫_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}}[(right function) - (left function)]dy \\
&= ∫_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}}[(9 - 2y^{2}) - (y^{2})]dy \\
&= ∫_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}}(9 - 3y^{2})dy \\
&= 9y \mid _{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} - y^{3} \mid _{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} \\
&= 9(\sqrt{3} - (-\sqrt{3})) - ((\sqrt{3})^{3} - (-\sqrt{3})^{3}) \\
&= 18\sqrt{3} - 6\sqrt{3} \\
&= 12\sqrt{3}
\end{aligned}
$$
