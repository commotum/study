## Introduction

Consider the following integral:

$$
\int \dfrac{1}{x^2+2x+5}\, \textrm{d}x
$$

This integral doesn't resemble any of the standard integrals we have encountered so far.

However, notice that we can complete the square in the denominator as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{2} + 2x + 5 &= x^{2} + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot x + 5 \\
&= x^{2} + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot x + (1^{2} - 1^{2}) + 5 \\
&= (x^{2} + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot x + 1^{2}) - 1^{2} + 5 \\
&= (x + 1)^{2} + 4
\end{aligned}
$$

If we plug the above into our integral and collect a factor of ${\color{red}{4}}$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{x^{2} + 2x + 5}dx &= ∫(1)/((x + 1)^{2} + 4)dx \\
&= ∫(1)/(4[(x + 1)^{2}/4 + 1])dx \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫(1)/((x + 1)^{2}/4 + 1)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫(1)/(1 + (x + 1)^{2}/4)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫(1)/(1 + ((x + 1)/2)^{2})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that this now resembles the basic integral for inverse tangent:

$$
\int \dfrac{1}{1+u^2} \, \textrm{d}u = \arctan u + C
$$

The only difference is that instead of $u$ as the variable, we have

$$
\left(\dfrac{x+1}{2}\right)
$$

So, let's perform the substitution

$$
u=\dfrac{x+1}{2}
$$

Differentiating, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=\dfrac{1}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm{d}x = 2\,\textrm{d}u
$$

Finally, we write the integral in terms of $u$, and evaluate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{x^{2} + 2x + 5}dx &= \frac{1}{4}∫(1)/(1 + ((x + 1)/2)^{2})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{4}∫\frac{1}{1 + u^{2}} \cdot 2du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫\frac{1}{1 + u^{2}}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\arctan u + C \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\arctan (\frac{x + 1}{2}) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

And we're done!

## Calculating Inverse Trigonometric Function Integrals Using Substitution

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{36 -(3x - 2)^2}} \, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

Let's rewrite our integral as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫(1)/(\sqrt{36 - (3x - 2)^{2}})dx &= ∫(1)/(\sqrt{36(1 - (3x - 2)^{2}/36)})dx \\
&= ∫(1)/(\sqrt{36} \cdot \sqrt{1 - (3x - 2)^{2}/36})dx \\
&= ∫(1)/(6\sqrt{1 - (3x - 2)^{2}/36})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{6}∫(1)/(\sqrt{1 - (3x - 2)^{2}/36})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{6}∫(1)/(\sqrt{1 - ((3x - 2)/6)^{2}})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

This resembles the basic integral for inverse sine:

$$
\int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,\textrm{d}u = \arcsin u + C
$$

The only difference is that instead of $u$ as the variable, we have

$$
\left(\dfrac{3x-2}{6}\right)
$$

So, let's perform the substitution

$$
u=\dfrac{3x-2}{6}
$$

Differentiating, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x} = \dfrac{1}{2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \textrm{d}x = 2\,\textrm{d}u
$$

Finally, we write the integral in terms of $u$, and evaluate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫(1)/(\sqrt{36 - (3x - 2)^{2}})dx &= \frac{1}{6}∫(1)/(\sqrt{1 - ((3x - 2)/6)^{2}})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{6}∫\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^{2}}} \cdot 2du \\
&= \frac{1}{3}\arcsin u + C \\
&= \frac{1}{3}\arcsin (\frac{3x - 2}{6}) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing an Arctangent Integral by Completing the Square

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{1}{x^2-2x + 3}\, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

First, we complete the square in the denominator:

$$
\begin{aligned} x^2-2x + 3 & = x^2 - 2 \cdot x+ (1^2 - 1^2) + 3 \\[3pt] & = (x^2 - 2 \cdot x+ 1^2) - 1 + 3 \\[3pt] & = (x-1)^2+2 \end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above into the denominator, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{x^{2} - 2x + 3}dx &= ∫(1)/((x - 1)^{2} + 2)dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫(1)/(1 + ((x - 1)/\sqrt{2})^{2})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

This resembles the basic integral for inverse tangent:

$$
\int \dfrac{1}{1+u^2} \, \textrm{d}u = \arctan u + C
$$

The only difference is that instead of $u$ as the variable, we have

$$
\left(\dfrac{x-1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
$$

So, let's perform the substitution

$$
u=\dfrac{x-1}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

Differentiating, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm{d}x = \sqrt{2}\,\textrm{d}u
$$

Finally, we write the integral in terms of $u$, and evaluate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{x^{2} - 2x + 3}dx &= \frac{1}{2}∫(1)/(1 + ((x - 1)/\sqrt{2})^{2})dx \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫\frac{1}{1 + u^{2}} \cdot \sqrt{2}du \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}∫\frac{1}{1 + u^{2}}du \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\arctan u + C \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\arctan (\frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{2}}) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing an Arcsine Integral by Completing the Square

**Example:** Calculate ${\displaystyle \int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4x - 4x^2}} \, \textrm{d}x}$.

**Explanation**

First, we complete the square for the argument of the square root in the denominator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
4x - 4x^{2} &= - (4x^{2} - 4x) \\
&= - ((2x)^{2} - 2 \cdot 2x + 1 - 1) \\
&= - ((2x - 1)^{2} - 1) \\
&= 1 - (2x - 1)^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above in the given integral, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{\sqrt{4x - 4x^{2}}}dx &= ∫(1)/(\sqrt{1 - (2x - 1)^{2}})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

This resembles the basic integral for inverse sine:

$$
\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,\textrm{d}u = \arcsin u+C
$$

The only difference is that instead of $u$ as the variable, we have $(2x - 1)$.

So, let's perform the substitution

$u =2x - 1$.
Differentiating, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm{d}x = \dfrac12\textrm{d}u
$$

Finally, we write the integral in terms of $u$, and evaluate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫\frac{1}{\sqrt{4x - 4x^{2}}}dx &= ∫(1)/(\sqrt{1 - (2x - 1)^{2}})dx \\
&= ∫\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^{2}}} \cdot \frac{1}{2}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}∫\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - u^{2}}}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\arcsin u + C \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\arcsin (2x - 1) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing an Arcsecant Integral by Completing the Square

**Example:** Evaluate the integral $\displaystyle{\int_{1+2/\sqrt{3}}^{3} \dfrac{1}{\mid x-1 \mid \sqrt{x^2-2x}}\, \textrm{d}x}$.

**Explanation**

First, we complete the square for the expression inside the square root:

$$
\begin{aligned} x^2-2x&= x^2 -2x +(1-1) \\[3pt] &= (x^2 -2x +1)-1 \\[3pt] &= (x-1)^2-1 \end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above in the given integral, we get

$$
\int_{1+2/\sqrt{3}}^{3} \dfrac{1}{\begin{vmatrix}x-1 & \sqrt{x^2-2x}}\, \textrm{d}x = \int_{1+2/\sqrt{3}}^{3} \dfrac{1}{\mid x-1\end{vmatrix}\sqrt{(x-1)^2-1}}\, \textrm{d}x
$$

This resembles the basic integral for inverse secant:

$$
\int \frac{1}{\mid u \mid \sqrt{u^2-1}} \, \textrm{d}u = \textrm{arcsec} \, u +C
$$

The only difference is that instead of $u$ as the variable, we have $x-1$.

So, let's perform the substitution
$u=x-1$.
Differentiating, we get

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm{d}x = \textrm{d}u
$$

Let's use the table below to change the limits from $x$ to $u$.

| $x$ | $1+\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$ | $3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $u$ | $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$ | $2$ |

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned} \int_{1+2/\sqrt{3}}^{3} \dfrac{1}{\begin{vmatrix}x-1 & \sqrt{x^2-2x}}\, \textrm{d}x &= \int_{1+2/\sqrt{3}}^{3}\dfrac{1}{\begin{vmatrix}x-1 & \sqrt{(x-1)^2-1}}\,\textrm{d}x \\[5pt] &=\int_{2/\sqrt{3}}^{2} \dfrac{1}{\mid u\end{vmatrix}\sqrt{u^2-1}}\, \textrm{d}u \\[5pt] &= \textrm{arcsec}\, {u}\Big\end{vmatrix}_{2/\sqrt{3}}^{2} \\[5pt] &= \textrm{arcsec} \left(2\right) - \textrm{arcsec}\left(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \right) \\[5pt] &=\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right) \\[5pt] &=\dfrac{\pi}{6}. \end{aligned}
$$
