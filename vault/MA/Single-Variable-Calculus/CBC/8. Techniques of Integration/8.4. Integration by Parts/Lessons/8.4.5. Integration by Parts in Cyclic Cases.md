## Introduction

In some cases, applying integration by parts can lead to a cyclic pattern, where the original integral reappears after a few steps. This cyclic behavior allows us to solve the integral by forming an equation that can be solved algebraically.

To illustrate, let's consider the following example:

$$
I = \int \dfrac{\ln x}{x} \textrm{d}x
$$

Let's try using the method of integration by parts. We suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \ln x & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{1}{x} & ⇒v = \int\frac{1}{x}dx = \ln x\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= \ln x(\ln x) - \int(\ln x)(\frac{1}{x})dx \\
&= \ln^{2} x - \int\frac{\ln x}{x}dx \\
&= \ln^{2} x - I
\end{aligned}
$$

We now have the same integral $I$ on both sides of the equality. Thinking about $I$ as some unknown variable, we can easily solve this equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \ln^{2} x - I \\
I + I &= \ln^{2} x \\
2I &= \ln^{2} x \\
I &= \frac{\ln^{2} x}{2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that we added a constant of integration right at the very end of the calculation.

## Integrating a Logarithmic Expression Using Cyclic Integration by Parts

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{\ln^2{x}}{x} \textrm{d}x$ using integration by parts.

**Explanation**

First, we let

$$
I = \int \dfrac{\ln^2 x}{x} \, \textrm{d}x
$$

To use integration by parts, let's suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = \ln^{2} x & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{2\ln x}{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{1}{x} & ⇒v = \int\frac{1}{x}dx = \ln x\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \int\frac{\ln^{2} x}{x}dx \\
&= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= (\ln^{2} x)(\ln x) - \int(\ln x)(\frac{2\ln x}{x})dx \\
&= \ln^{3} x - 2\int\frac{\ln^{2} x}{x}dx \\
&= \ln^{3} x - 2I
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we solve for $I$, remembering to add a constant of integration $C$ at the very end:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \ln^{3} x - 2I \\
3I &= \ln^{3} x \\
I &= \frac{\ln^{3} x}{3} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Indefinite Integral of a Exponential-Trigonometric Expression Using Integration by Parts

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int e^{x}\sin{x} \, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

To use integration by parts, let's suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = e^{x} & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = e^{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \sin x & ⇒v = \int\sin xdx =-\cos x\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \inte^{x}\sin xdx \\
&= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= e^{x}(-\cos x) - \int(-\cos x)(e^{x})dx \\
&=-e^{x}\cos x + \inte^{x}\cos xdx \\
&=-e^{x}\cos x + J
\end{aligned}
$$

Again, we can use integration by parts to evaluate

$$
J = \int e^{x}\cos{x}\,\textrm{d}x
$$

To do this, let's suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = e^{x} & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = e^{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \cos x & ⇒v = \int\cos xdx = \sin x\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula once more, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
J &= \inte^{x}\cos xdx \\
&= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= e^{x}(\sin x) - \int(\sin x)(e^{x})dx \\
&= e^{x}\sin x - \inte^{x}\sin xdx \\
&= e^{x}\sin x - I
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, substituting this back into our equation for $I$, we can subsequently solve for $I$. We remember to add a constant of integration $C$ at the end.

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= -e^{x}\cos x + J \\
I &= -e^{x}\cos x + e^{x}\sin x - I \\
2I &= -e^{x}\cos x + e^{x}\sin x \\
I &= \frac{e^{x}}{2}(\sin x - \cos x) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Definite Integral of a Exponential-Trigonometric Expression Using Integration by Parts

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi/2} e^x\sin(2x) \, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

Let's denote the indefinite integral by

$$
I = \displaystyle \int e^{x}\sin(2x)\, \textrm{d}x
$$

To use integration by parts, let's suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = e^{x} & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = e^{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \sin (2x) & ⇒v = \int\sin (2x)dx = - (\cos (2x))/(2)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= \inte^{x}\sin (2x)dx \\
&= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= e^{x}(- (\cos (2x))/(2)) - \int(- (\cos (2x))/(2))(e^{x})dx \\
&= - (e^{x}\cos (2x))/(2) + \frac{1}{2}\inte^{x}\cos (2x)dx \\
&= - (e^{x}\cos (2x))/(2) + \frac{1}{2}J
\end{aligned}
$$

Again, we can use integration by parts to evaluate

$$
J = \int e^{x}\cos(2x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

To do this, let's suppose that

$$
\begin{bmatrix}u = e^{x} & ⇒\frac{du}{dx} = e^{x}, \\ \frac{dv}{dx} = \cos (2x) & ⇒v = \int\cos (2x)dx = (\sin (2x))/(2)\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the integration by parts formula once more, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
J &= \inte^{x}\cos (2x)dx \\
&= \intu\frac{dv}{dx}dx \\
&= uv - \intv\frac{du}{dx}dx \\
&= e^{x}((\sin (2x))/(2)) - \int((\sin (2x))/(2))(e^{x})dx \\
&= (e^{x}\sin (2x))/(2) - \frac{1}{2}\inte^{x}\sin (2x)dx \\
&= (e^{x}\sin (2x))/(2) - \frac{1}{2}I
\end{aligned}
$$

So, substituting this into our equation for $I$, we can subsequently solve for $I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I &= - (e^{x}\cos (2x))/(2) + \frac{1}{2}J \\
I &= - (e^{x}\cos (2x))/(2) + \frac{1}{2}((e^{x}\sin (2x))/(2) - \frac{1}{2}I) \\
I &= - (e^{x}\cos (2x))/(2) + (e^{x}\sin (2x))/(4) - \frac{1}{4}I \\
\frac{5}{4}I &= (e^{x}\sin (2x) - 2e^{x}\cos (2x))/(4) \\
I &= (e^{x}(\sin (2x) - 2\cos (2x)))/(5)
\end{aligned}
$$

Finally, we evaluate our definite integral, as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{0}^{π/2}e^{x}\sin (2x)dx &= \left.((e^{x}(\sin (2x) - 2\cos (2x)))/(5))\right|_{0}^{π/2} \\
&= (e^{π/2}(\sin (2 \cdot \frac{π}{2}) - 2\cos (2 \cdot \frac{π}{2})))/(5) - (e^{0}(\sin (2 \cdot 0) - 2\cos (2 \cdot 0)))/(5) \\
&= \frac{2e^{π/2}}{5} + \frac{2}{5} \\
&= \frac{2}{5}(e^{π/2} + 1)
\end{aligned}
$$
