## Introduction

The average rate of change of the function $y=f(x)$ on the interval $[a,a+h]$ is given by

$$
\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}
$$

This can be interpreted as the slope of the secant line that intersects the curve at the points $\color{blue}P$ and ${\color{red}Q}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. The Instantaneous Rate of Change of a Function at a Point/Images/8fa025d6e17703ef7ac0fd8b7746aee8.png>)

The **instantaneous rate of change** of the function $f(x)$ at the point $x = a$ is the limit of the average rate of change as $h \to 0$. It is denoted by $f'(a)$ and given by

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac {f(a+h) - f(a)} {h}
$$

Geometrically, as $h \to 0$, the point $\color{red}Q$ moves along the curve $y=f(x)$ towards the point ${\color{blue}P}$, and the secant transforms into the line that touches the curve at exactly one point $({\color{blue}P})$. That line is called the **tangent line** to the curve $y=f(x)$ at the point $\color{blue}P$.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. The Instantaneous Rate of Change of a Function at a Point/Images/c19ad835d91bf88422f3058e68885970.png>)

The value of $f'(a)$ is equal to the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y=f(x)$ at $x=a$.

<a id="calculating-the-instantaneous-rate-of-change-of-a-function-at-a-point"></a>
## Calculating the Instantaneous Rate of Change of a Function at a Point

**Example:** Find the instantaneous rate of change of $f(x)=3x^2$ at the point $x=5$ using the definition.

**Explanation**

Let's use the definition

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac {f(a+h) - f(a)} {h}
$$

In our problem, we have $a=5$, and so by the definition we have

$$
f^{′}(5) = lim_(h → 0)(f(5 + h) - f(5))/(h)
$$

Applying this to

$$
f(x)=3x^2
$$

, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{′}(5) &= lim_(h → 0)((3(5 + h)^{2} - 3(5)^{2})/(h)) \\
&= lim_(h → 0)(\frac{75 + 30h + 3h^{2} - 75}{h}) \\
&= lim_(h → 0)(\frac{30h + 3h^{2}}{h}) \\
&= lim_(h → 0)(30 + 3h) \\
&= 30 + 3(0) \\
&= 30
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="an-equivalent-definition-of-instantaneous-rate-of-change"></a>
## An Equivalent Definition of Instantaneous Rate of Change

Another way of defining $f'(a)$ is

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac {f(x) - f(a)} {x-a}
$$

We arrive at this definition in the same way as before. But this time, we consider the interval $[a,x]$ instead of $[a,a+h]$, and then take the limit of the average rate of change as $x\to a$.

![](<../Source/3.1.2. The Instantaneous Rate of Change of a Function at a Point/Images/fac63525908a6381883f901da31d4d34.png>)

Sometimes, this version is a bit easier to work with, so let's see an example of how to apply it. We should always arrive at the same answer regardless of which definition we use.

## Calculating the Slope of the Tangent Line to a Curve at a Point Using the Equivalent Definition

**Example:** Using the limit of a difference quotient, calculate the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y=2x-x^2$ at the point $x=2$.

**Explanation**

Let

$$
f(x)=2x-x^2
$$

The slope of the tangent line is equal to $f'(2)$.

Let's use the equivalent definition for $f'(a)$, given by

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac {f(x) - f(a)} {x-a}
$$

Using the above definition with $a=2$, we have

$$
f'(2) = \lim_{x\to 2}\dfrac {f(x) - f(2)} {x-2}
$$

Applying this to

$$
f(x)=2x-x^2
$$

we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{′}(2) &= lim_(x → 2)(([2x - x^{2}] - [2(2) - (2)^{2}])/(x - 2)) \\
&= lim_(x → 2)(\frac{-x^{2} + 2x}{x - 2}) \\
&= lim_(x → 2)((-x(x - 2))/(x - 2)) \\
&= lim_(x → 2)(-x) \\
&=-2
\end{aligned}
$$

So the slope of the tangent line to the curve at $x=2$ is $-2$.
