## Introduction

The function

$$
f(x)=x^2\sin \left(\dfrac{1}{x^2}\right)
$$

is undefined at $x=0$, but does that mean that the limit of this function at $x=0$ is also undefined?

To find out, let's start by looking at the graph of $y=f(x)$.

![](<../Source/1.5.1. The Squeeze Theorem/Images/33f7e50591d5203a8620a3ce3c3f4925.png>)

Based on the graph, it looks like $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to 0$. But let's try to find more convincing evidence.

First, notice that the function $f(x)$ is squeezed (or sandwiched) between the two functions $y=x^2$ and $y=-x^2$. This happens because $\sin(\cdots)$ ranges between $-1$ and $1$, so $x^2 \sin (\cdots)$ ranges between $-x^2$ and $x^2$.

![](<../Source/1.5.1. The Squeeze Theorem/Images/ea70bff2b002f1f607e6f00559625513.png>)

Starting with

$$
-x^2 \leq f(x) \leq x^2
$$

we take the limit as $x\to 0$ and evaluate the two known limits, reaching

$$
\begin{bmatrix}lim_(x → 0)(-x^{2}) \le & lim_(x → 0)f(x) \le lim_(x → 0)(x^{2}) \\ 0 \le & lim_(x → 0)f(x) \le 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

There is only one number that satisfies the final inequality above, and that number is $0$. Therefore,

$$
\lim_{x\to 0}f(x) =0
$$

We just applied the **squeeze theorem**, also called the **sandwich theorem**. Written generally, the squeeze theorem states that if a function $f(x)$ is squeezed between two functions $g(x)$ and $h(x)$, i.e.,

$$
h(x) \le f(x) \le g(x)
$$

and

$$
\lim_{x\to a} h(x)=\lim_{x\to a} g(x) =L
$$

for constants $a$ and $L$, then

$$
\lim_{x\to a} f(x) =L
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-of-a-function-given-two-bounding-functions"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit of a Function Given Two Bounding Functions

**Example:** Find $\lim\limits_{x\to 2} f(x)$ given that

$4x-4 \le f(x) \le x^2$.

**Explanation**

Taking the limits gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix}lim_(x → 2)(4x - 4) \le & lim_(x → 2)f(x) \le lim_(x → 2)x^{2} \\ 4(2) - 4 \le & lim_(x → 2)f(x) \le 2^{2} \\ 4 \le & lim_(x → 2)f(x) \le 4\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, by the squeeze theorem, we must have

$$
\lim\limits_{x\to 2} f(x)=4
$$

## Evaluating the Limit of the Product of an Even Power and a Trigonometric Function

**Example:** Evaluate $\lim\limits_{x\to0} x^4\cos \left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)$.

**Explanation**

Recall that $\cos(\theta)$ ranges between $-1$ and $1$. So we have

$$
-1 \le \cos \left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) \le 1
$$

and multiplying both sides by $x^4$ gives

$$
-x^4 \le x^4\cos\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) \le x^4
$$

Now, taking the limits gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix}lim_(x → 0)(-x^{4}) \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{4}\cos (\frac{1}{x}) \le lim_(x → 0)(x^{4}) \\ 0 \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{4}\cos (\frac{1}{x}) \le 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

which, by the squeeze theorem, implies that

$$
\lim\limits_{x\to0} x^4\cos \left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) = 0
$$

<a id="evaluating-a-limit-with-a-trigonometric-function-in-the-exponent"></a>
## Evaluating a Limit with a Trigonometric Function in the Exponent

**Example:** Evaluate $\lim\limits_{x\to0} x^2e^{\cos (1/x)}$.

**Explanation**

Recall that $\cos(\theta)$ ranges between $-1$ and $1$. So we have

$$
-1 \le \cos \left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right) \le 1
$$

Now, since $e^x$ is a positive, increasing function, we can exponentiate both sides to give

$$
e^{-1} \le e^{\cos (1/x)} \le e^{1}
$$

Multiplying both sides of the inequality by $x^2$ gives

$$
x^2e^{-1} \le x^2e^{\cos (1/x)} \le x^2e^{1}
$$

and taking the limits gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix}lim_(x → 0)x^{2}e^{-1} \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{2}e^{\cos (1/x)} \le lim_(x → 0)x^{2}e^{1} \\ 0 \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{2}e^{\cos (1/x)} \le 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, by the squeeze theorem, we must have

$$
\lim_{x\to0} x^2e^{\cos (1/x)} = 0
$$

## Evaluating the Limit of the Product of an Odd Power and a Trigonometric Function

**Example:** Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}x^3\sin\left(\dfrac 3 x\right)$.

**Explanation**

Recall that $\sin(\theta)$ ranges between $-1$ and $1$. So, we have

$$
\left|\sin{\left(\dfrac{3}{x}\right)}\right|\leq 1
$$

Multiplying the above inequality by $\mid x^3 \mid$ gives

$$
\begin{vmatrix}x^{3} & \cdot & \sin (\frac{3}{x}) & \le & x^{3}\end{vmatrix};\begin{vmatrix}x^{3}\sin (\frac{3}{x}) & \le & x^{3}\end{vmatrix}
$$

and using the definition of absolute value, we have

$$
-\begin{vmatrix}x^3 & \leq x^3 \sin{\left(\dfrac{3}{x}\right)} \leq & x^3\end{vmatrix}
$$

Note that we're using absolute value here because $x^3$ has an odd power.

Now, taking the limit as $x\to 0$, we have

$$
\begin{bmatrix}lim_(x → 0)(-\mid x^{3} \mid) \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{3}\sin (\frac{3}{x}) \le lim_(x → 0)(\mid x^{3} \mid) \\ 0 \le & lim_(x → 0)x^{3}\sin (\frac{3}{x}) \le 0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, by the squeeze theorem, we conclude that

$$
\lim_{x\to 0} x^3 \sin{\left(\dfrac{3}{x}\right)} = 0
$$
