## Introduction

Consider the finite region bounded by the curve

$$
y=\sqrt{x}
$$

the $x$-axis, and the vertical line $x=2$, as shown in the picture below.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/08769f0785cd2a203cdc6da3bc4a7a8f.png>)

When we rotate this region $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis (called the **axis of revolution**), we get a **solid of revolution,** as shown below. How can we find the volume of this solid?

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/4c84a2624638eb422c25822859ad7e16.png>)

To find the volume of the solid generated when the region bounded by a curve $y=f(x)$, the $x$-axis, and the vertical lines $x=a$ and $x=b$ is rotated $2\pi$ radians about the $x$-axis, we use the following formula:

$$
V = \pi \int_{a}^{b} y^2 \:\textrm{d}x = \pi \int_{a}^{b} \left[ f(x) \right]^2 \:\textrm{d}x
$$

The method corresponding to the formula above is called the **disc method** because it involves breaking up the region into many thin discs (or cylinders) of height $\Delta{x}$, as shown below.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/09290f6c5a7efef34e4860a06943fcd6.png>)

When $\Delta{x} \to 0$, the radius of each disc can be considered equal to $y=f(x)$. So, the volume of the disc (cylinder) is

$$
\pi y^2 \Delta{x} = \pi \left[ f(x) \right]^2 \Delta{x}
$$

Integrating this expression from $a$ to $b$, we obtain the formula for the volume of the solid.

In our example above, we have

$$
f(x) = \sqrt{x}
$$

so the volume is given by

$$
V = \pi\int_0^2 (\sqrt{x})^2\,\textrm d x = \pi\int_0^2 x\,\textrm d x
$$

Evaluating this integral gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π \cdot \frac{x^{2}}{2} \mid _{0}^{2} \\
&= π(\frac{2^{2}}{2} - \frac{0^{2}}{2}) \\
&= 2π
\end{aligned}
$$

So the volume of the solid is $2\pi$ cubic units.

## Identifying an Integral Representing the Volume of a Solid Generated by Rotation About the X-Axis

**Example:** Consider the finite region bounded by the curve $y=2x-x^2$ and the $x$-axis, as shown below. What integral gives the volume of the solid generated when that region is rotated $2\pi$ radians about the $x$-axis?

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/3590cbff05b443136c155fc49fbf87d3.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we want to determine the limits of integration. We find the intersections of the curve with the $x$-axis by solving the following equation for $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x - x^{2} &= 0 \\
x(2 - x) &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

The solutions are $x=0$ and $x=2$. So, we need to integrate from $x=0$ to $x=2$.

Finally, the volume can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π∫_{a}^{b}[f(x)]^{2}dx \\
&= π∫_{0}^{2}(2x - x^{2})^{2}dx \\
&= π∫_{0}^{2}(4x^{2} - 4x^{3} + x^{4})dx
\end{aligned}
$$

## Computing the Volume of a Solid Generated by Rotation About the X-Axis

**Example:** Consider the finite region bounded by the curve $y=4-x^2$, the $x$-axis, and the vertical lines $x=0$ and $x=1$, as shown below. Calculate the volume of the solid generated when this region is rotated $2\pi$ radians about the $x$-axis.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/2c2ab6c2062574e99e9e82de817d330e.png>)

**Explanation**

We are told that the limits of integration are from $x=0$ to $x=1$.

Therefore, the volume can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π∫_{a}^{b}[f(x)]^{2}dx \\
&= π∫_{0}^{1}(4 - x^{2})^{2}dx \\
&= π∫_{0}^{1}(16 - 8x^{2} + x^{4})dx \\
&= π(16∫_{0}^{1}dx - 8∫_{0}^{1}x^{2}dx + ∫_{0}^{1}x^{4}dx) \\
&= π(16x\begin{vmatrix}_{0}^{1} - 8 \cdot \frac{x^{3}}{3} & _{0}^{1} + \frac{x^{5}}{5}\end{vmatrix}_{0}^{1}) \\
&= π(16(1 - 0) - 8(\frac{1}{3} - 0) + (\frac{1}{5} - 0)) \\
&= π(16 - \frac{8}{3} + \frac{1}{5}) \\
&= \frac{203π}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Rotation About the Y-Axis

Consider the finite region bounded by the curve $x=y^2$, the $y$-axis, and the horizontal line $y=1$, as shown in the picture below.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/b51843e3aae894be3a8df702a220b966.png>)

When we rotate this region $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis, we again get a solid of revolution, as shown below. How can we find the volume of this solid?

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/281789c431cbf5950ee75bb9aae3de08.png>)

To find the volume of this solid, we can use the same *disc method* approach that we used earlier for the $x$-axis.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/906daf0da5e4b9ba0a59e8a7b7b8b85b.png>)

The only difference is that we need to swap $x$ and $y$. The radius of each disc (cylinder) is equal to $x=g(y)$ and its height is $\Delta{y}$. So, the volume of the disc (cylinder) is

$$
\pi x^2 \Delta{y} = \pi [ g(y) ]^2 \Delta{y}
$$

Finally, we need to integrate this expression. Therefore, to find the volume of the solid generated when the region bounded by a curve $x=g(y)$, the $y$-axis, and the horizontal lines $y=c$ and $y=d$ is rotated $2\pi$ radians about the $y$-axis, we use the following formula:

$$
V = \pi \int_{c}^{d} \left[ g(y) \right]^2 \:\textrm{d}y
$$

In our example above, we have $x=y^2$, so the volume is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= ∫_{0}^{1}[y^{2}]^{2}dy = π∫_{0}^{1}y^{4}dy
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluating this integral gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π∫_{0}^{1}y^{4}dy \\
&= π \cdot \frac{y^{5}}{5} \mid _{0}^{1} \\
&= π(\frac{1}{5} - 0) \\
&= \frac{π}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Identifying an Integral Representing the Volume of a Solid Generated by Rotation About the Y-Axis

**Example:** Consider the finite region bounded by the curve $x=2y^3$, the $y$-axis, and the line $y=1$, as shown below. Which integral gives the volume of the solid generated when this region is rotated by $2\pi$ radians about the $y$-axis?

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/be51b3eef9c8a161af50ddd58168ba42.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we want to determine the limits of integration. We find the intersections of the curve with the $y$-axis by solving the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2y^{3} &= 0 \\
y^{3} &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the solution is $y=0$. Since we consider the region above bounded by the curve $x=2y^3$, the $y$-axis, and the line $y=1$, we need to integrate from $y=0$ to $y=1$.

Finally, the volume can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π∫_{c}^{d}[g(y)]^{2}dy \\
&= π∫_{0}^{1}(2y^{3})^{2}dy \\
&= 4π∫_{0}^{1}y^{6}dy
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="computing-the-volume-of-a-solid-generated-by-rotation-about-the-y-axis"></a>
## Computing the Volume of a Solid Generated by Rotation About the Y-Axis

**Example:** Consider the finite region bounded by the curve $x=2-y^2$ and the $y$-axis, as shown below. Calculate the volume of the solid generated when this region is rotated $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis.

![](<../Source/10.3.1. Volumes of Revolution Using the Disc Method- Rotation About the Coordinate Axes/Images/d5d807e06da54e47da9539a9cf138eda.png>)

**Explanation**

First, we want to determine the limits of integration. We find the intersections of the curve with the $y$-axis by solving for $y$ the following equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 - y^{2} &= 0 \\
y^{2} &= 2
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence, the solutions are

$$
y=\pm \sqrt 2
$$

So, we need to integrate from

$$
y=-\sqrt{2}
$$

to

$$
y=\sqrt 2
$$

Finally, the volume can be found as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= π∫_{c}^{d}[g(y)]^{2}dy \\
&= π∫_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}}(2 - y^{2})^{2}dy \\
&= π∫_{-\sqrt{2}}^{\sqrt{2}}(4 - 4y^{2} + y^{4})dy \\
&= 2π∫_{0}^{\sqrt{2}}(4 - 4y^{2} + y^{4})dy \\
&= 2π[4y - \frac{4}{3}y^{3} + \frac{1}{5}y^{5}]_{0}^{\sqrt{2}} \\
&= 2π([4(\sqrt{2}) - \frac{4}{3}(\sqrt{2})^{3} + \frac{1}{5}(\sqrt{2})^{5}] - [0]) \\
&= 2π(4\sqrt{2} - \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{3} + \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{5}) \\
&= 2π(\frac{32\sqrt{2}}{15}) \\
&= \frac{64π\sqrt{2}}{15}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Note:** Since the region is symmetrical about the $x$-axis, in step $3$ above we were able to write

$$
\displaystyle \pi\int_{-\sqrt 2}^\sqrt 2 [g(y)]^2\,\textrm d y = 2\pi\int_{0}^\sqrt 2 [g(y)]^2\,\textrm d y
$$

This step saved us a bit of work because the antiderivative evaluated to zero at the lower limit.
