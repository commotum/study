## Introduction

Suppose we want to calculate the integral

$$
\displaystyle \int\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}\,\textrm d x
$$

At first glance, this looks kind of tricky. However, if we use the identity

$$
\dfrac{1}{\sin x} = \csc x
$$

we can rewrite the integral as

$$
\int\frac{1}{\sin^{2} x}dx = \int(\frac{1}{\sin x})^{2}dx = \int\csc^{2} xdx
$$

and we see that this is just a standard trigonometric integral:

$$
\int\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}\,\textrm d x = \int\csc^2 x\,\textrm d x =-\cot x + C
$$

When confronted with a tricky-looking trigonometric integral, we can often reduce it to a standard integral using a trigonometric identity.

<a id="integrating-a-product-of-trigonometric-functions-using-trigonometric-identities"></a>
## Integrating a Product of Trigonometric Functions Using Trigonometric Identities

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\left(\cot x + \sin^2 x\right)\,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

We multiply out the parentheses and then use the identity

$$
\dfrac{1}{\sin x} = \csc x
$$

as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{1}{\sin x}(\cot x + \sin^{2} x)dx &= \int(\frac{1}{\sin x} \cdot \cot x + \frac{1}{\sin x} \cdot \sin^{2} x)dx \\
&= \int(\csc x \cdot \cot x + \sin x)dx \\
&= \int\csc x\cot xdx + \int\sin xdx \\
&=-\csc x - \cos x + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Integrating a Quotient of Trigonometric Functions Using Trigonometric Identities

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int_0^{\pi/3} \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}\,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

We can write the integral as

$$
\int_0^{\pi/3} \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}\,\textrm d x = \int_0^{\pi/3} \dfrac{1}{\cos x}\cdot \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,\textrm d x
$$

We then use the fact that

$$
\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x
$$

and

$$
\dfrac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x
$$

to express this as

$$
\int_0^{\pi/3} \sec x\tan x\,\textrm d x
$$

which is a standard trigonometric integral. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{0}^{π/3}\frac{\sin x}{\cos^{2} x}dx &= \int_{0}^{π/3}\sec x\tan xdx \\
&= \left.\sec x\right|_{0}^{π/3} \\
&= \sec (\frac{π}{3}) - \sec (0) \\
&= 2 - 1 \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

## Integrating a Trigonometric Expression Using Identities and Substitution

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \tan{2x}\,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

We use the identity

$$
\tan 2x = \dfrac{\sin 2x}{\cos 2x}
$$

and rewrite the integral as

$$
\int \tan{2x}\,\textrm d x = \int \dfrac{\sin{2x}}{\cos 2x}\,\textrm d x
$$

Notice that the numerator of the integrand is proportional to the derivative of the denominator. So, we can solve this by substitution.

Let $u=\cos 2x$. Then

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=-2 \sin 2x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\dfrac 1 2 \,\textrm d u= \sin 2x \, \textrm d x
$$

Using the above, we can write the integral in terms of $u$ and evaluate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{\sin 2x}{\cos 2x}dx &= -\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{1}{u}du \\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\ln\begin{vmatrix}u & + C \\ =-\frac{1}{2}\ln & \cos 2x\end{vmatrix}+ C \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\ln\begin{vmatrix}(\cos 2x)^{-1} & + C \\ = \frac{1}{2}\ln & \sec 2x\end{vmatrix}+ C
\end{aligned}
$$
