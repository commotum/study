## Introduction

Suppose that we want to approximate the area $\mathcal A$ bound by the curve

$$
y= x^2+1
$$

and the lines $x=0$, $x=1$, and the $x$-axis. We can do this using a left Riemann sum with $n=4$ subintervals, as shown below:

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Defining Definite Integrals Using Left and Right Riemann Sums/Images/d22370dd994cb0feddbd1432fe373f3a.png>)

A better approximation can be found by taking more subintervals. For instance, taking $n=8$ subintervals gives:

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Defining Definite Integrals Using Left and Right Riemann Sums/Images/a69c437279cd34ae1c2124595aecdfad.png>)

As we continue to increase $n$, our approximation gets better and better. In fact, we can get an *exact* value for the area by taking the limit of the Riemann sum as $n\to\infty$.

Let's write down that limit. First, we need to express the left Riemann sum using sigma notation. Using $n$ subintervals between the endpoints $a=0$ and $b=1$, we express

$$
A \mid \approx ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)f(x_{k})Δx; = ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)f(0 + k \cdot Δx)Δx; = ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)f(k \cdot Δx)Δx; = ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)((k \cdot Δx)^{2} + 1)Δx; = ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)(k^{2} \cdot \frac{1}{n^{2}} + 1) \cdot \frac{1}{n}; = ∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)(\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}} + 1) \cdot \frac{1}{n}
$$

In the above, we used the fact that

$$
x_k = 0 + k\Delta x = k\Delta x
$$

and

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{1 - 0}{n} = \dfrac 1 n
$$

as we've seen before.

The exact area is found by computing the limit as $n\to\infty$. So instead of using an approximation symbol

$$
(\approx)
$$

we can now use an equality symbol and write

$$
A = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\dfrac{k^2}{n^2} + 1\right)\cdot \dfrac{1}{n}
$$

The formula above is a bit clunky, so instead of writing it using limits and sigma notation, we introduce a new notation called the **definite integral**, defined as

$$
\mathcal{A} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\dfrac{k^2}{n^2} + 1\right)\cdot \dfrac{1}{n}= \int_0^1 (x^2 + 1)\, \textrm{d}x
$$

In our example,

$\displaystyle \int_0^1 (x^2+1)\, \textrm{d}x$
can be interpreted as the exact area under the graph of

$$
y = f(x) = x^2+1
$$

between $x=0$, $x=1$, and the $x$-axis.

---

<a id="general-notation"></a>
## General Notation

In general, for a function $f(x)$ defined on $[a,b]$, the **definite integral** of $f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ can be defined using the limit of a left Riemann sum as

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\textrm{d}x = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(x_k)\Delta x
$$

where

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}
$$

and

$$
x_k = a + k\Delta x
$$

The limit $x=a$ is referred to as the **lower limit**, whereas $x=b$ is called the **upper limit**.

## Writing the Area Bounded by a Curve as a Definite Integral

**Example:** For the function $y = -2x^3+2$ shown above, write down an expression for the area $\mathcal{A}$ as a definite integral.

![](<../Source/6.3.1. Defining Definite Integrals Using Left and Right Riemann Sums/Images/0cc7c2b91797e6c24577ac66cfd8e215.png>)

**Explanation**

We want to express the area $\mathcal{A}$ between the function

$$
f(x) = -2x^3+2
$$

and the $x$-axis, between $x=0$ and $x=1$.

This can be expressed as the definite integral of

$$
f(x) = -2x^3+2
$$

between the lower limit $x=0$ and the upper limit $x=1$, as follows:

$$
\displaystyle \mathcal{A} = \int_0^1 (-2x^3+2)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

<a id="expressing-a-definite-integral-as-the-limit-of-a-left-riemann-sum"></a>
## Expressing a Definite Integral as the Limit of a Left Riemann Sum

**Example:** Express $\displaystyle \int_1^3(4-x)\,\textrm{d}x$ as the limit of a left Riemann sum.

**Explanation**

The definite integral can be defined in terms of the limit of a left Riemann sum as

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\textrm{d}x = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(x_k)\Delta x
$$

where

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}
$$

and

$$
x_k = a + k\Delta x
$$

In the definite integral

$$
\displaystyle \int_1^3(4-x)\,\textrm{d}x
$$

the function is

$$
f(x) = 4-x
$$

the lower bound is $a=1$, and the upper bound is $b=3$. So, we have

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{3-1}{n} = \dfrac 2 n
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{1}^{3}(4 - x)dx &= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)f(x_{k})Δx \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)[f(1 + k \cdot \frac{2}{n}) \cdot \frac{2}{n}] \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)[f(1 + \frac{2k}{n}) \cdot \frac{2}{n}] \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)[(4 - (1 + \frac{2k}{n})) \cdot \frac{2}{n}] \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 0)^(n - 1)[(3 - \frac{2k}{n}) \cdot \frac{2}{n}]
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="expressing-the-limit-of-a-left-riemann-sum-as-a-definite-integral"></a>
## Expressing the Limit of a Left Riemann Sum as a Definite Integral

**Example:** Find the function $f(x)$ and the value of $b$, given that $\displaystyle \int_{3}^b f(x)\, \textrm d x =\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[\left(3 + \dfrac{5k}{n}\right)^2\cdot \dfrac 5 n\right]$.

**Explanation**

A definite integral can be defined as the limit of a left Riemann sum as follows:

$$
\int_a^b f(x) \, \textrm d x = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f\left(x_k\right)\cdot \Delta x
$$

where

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}
$$

and

$$
x_k = a + k\Delta x
$$

Notice that we're given that the lower limit $a = 3$.

Comparing the given summation with the general expression above, we see that

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{5}{n}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\dfrac{5}{n} = \dfrac{b-3}{n} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad b = 8
$$

Consequently,

$$
x_k = 3 + \dfrac{5k}{n}
$$

and

$$
f(x_k) = x_{k}^2
$$

Hence,

$$
\int_{3}^{8} x^2 \, \textrm d x =\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[\left(3 + \dfrac{5k}{n}\right)^2\cdot \dfrac 5 n\right]
$$

Therefore, the correct answer is

$$
f(x)= x^2
$$

and $b = 8$.

<a id="using-a-right-riemann-sum"></a>
## Using a Right Riemann Sum

There's nothing special about using a left Riemann sum to define a definite integral. A definite integral can also be defined as the limit of a right Riemann sum,

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\textrm d x = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k)\cdot\Delta x
$$

where

$$
x_k = a + k\Delta x
$$

and

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}
$$

Nothing has changed, except that the index starts from $k=1$ instead of $k=0$, and goes up to $k=n$.

**Note:** In the limit as $n \to \infty$, the left and right Riemann sums give *the exact same result*!

<a id="expressing-a-definite-integral-as-the-limit-of-a-right-riemann-sum"></a>
## Expressing a Definite Integral as the Limit of a Right Riemann Sum

**Example:** Express $\displaystyle \int_1^2 x^2\,\textrm d x$ as the limit of a right Riemann sum.

**Explanation**

The definite integral can be defined in terms of the limit of a right Riemann sum as

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\textrm d x = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^n f(x_k)\cdot\Delta x
$$

where

$$
x_k = a + k\Delta x
$$

and

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}
$$

In the definite integral

$$
\displaystyle \int_1^2 x^2\,\textrm d x
$$

the function is

$$
f(x) = x^2
$$

the lower bound is $a=1$ and the upper bound is $b=2$. So, we have

$$
\Delta x = \dfrac{2-1}{n} = \dfrac 1 n
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
∫_{1}^{2}x^{2}dx &= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 1)^(n)f(x_{k})Δx \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 1)^(n)[f(1 + k \cdot \frac{1}{n}) \cdot \frac{1}{n}] \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 1)^(n)[f(1 + \frac{k}{n}) \cdot \frac{1}{n}] \\
&= lim_(n → ∞)∑_(k = 1)^(n)[(1 + \frac{k}{n})^{2} \cdot \frac{1}{n}]
\end{aligned}
$$
