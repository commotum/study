## Introduction

Suppose we want to find the integral of a power function multiplied by a constant, like $5x^2$. How do we proceed?

According to the **constant factor rule**, we can just take the constant factor out of the integral, like this:

$$
\int 5 x^2\, \textrm{d}x = 5\cdot \int x^2 \,\textrm{d}x
$$

Then, we solve the integral using the power rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
5 \cdot \intx^{2}dx &= 5 \cdot (\frac{x^{2 + 1}}{2 + 1}) + C \\
&= \frac{5}{3}x^{3} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

We can use this trick in general. Whenever the integrand is a function $f(x)$ multiplied by a constant $k$, we can simplify as

$$
\int k f(x)\, \textrm{d}x = k \int f(x) \,\textrm{d}x
$$

**Watch out!** We can only take *constant factors* out of integrals. We *cannot* take any variables out of integrals.

## Using the Constant Factor Rule to Integrate a Power Function

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac{2}{3} x \,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

We can just take the constant factor out of the integral, and then apply the power rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{2}{3}xdx &= \frac{2}{3}\intxdx \\
&= \frac{2}{3}(\frac{x^{1 + 1}}{1 + 1}) + C \\
&= \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{x^{2}}{2} + C \\
&= \frac{1}{3}x^{2} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Using the Constant Factor Rule to Integrate a Constant Function

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int 6 \,\textrm d x$.

**Explanation**

We can just take the constant factor out of the integral, and then apply the power rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int6dx &= 6\int1dx \\
&= 6\intx^{0}dx \\
&= 6 \cdot \frac{x^{0 + 1}}{0 + 1} + C \\
&= 6 \cdot x + C \\
&= 6x + C
\end{aligned}
$$

In general, we can see that for any constant $k$, we have

$$
\displaystyle \int k \,\textrm d x = kx + C
$$
