## Introduction

Let's take a look at the function

$$
y=\dfrac 1 x
$$

below.

![](<../Source/1.6.7. Discontinuities Due to Vertical Asymptotes/Images/ced7d425b27c52b44b231c6ae73fb83b.png>)

The function has a vertical asymptote at $x=0$. There is also a discontinuity in the function at $x=0$, since we cannot draw the points on the curve without taking our pen off the paper to cross the asymptote.

So, we say that

$$
y = \dfrac 1 x
$$

has a **discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote** at $x=0$. Discontinuities due to vertical asymptotes are also called **infinite discontinuities**.

Notice that here, both one-sided limits are infinite:

$$
\lim_{x\to 0^-} \left(\dfrac 1 x\right) =-\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x\to 0^+} \left(\dfrac 1 x \right)=\infty
$$

In general, a function $y=f(x)$ has a discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote at $x=c$ if either one-sided limit is infinite:

$$
\lim_{x\to c^-} f(x) =\pm \infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\lim_{x\to c^+} f(x) =\pm \infty
$$

Note that we only need a single limit to become infinite to conclude that there is an infinite discontinuity at $x=c$.

## Finding Discontinuities Due to Vertical Asymptotes Using Graphs

**Example:** For the function $y=f(x)$ below, where does the function have a discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote?

![](<../Source/1.6.7. Discontinuities Due to Vertical Asymptotes/Images/b1ed0c7ce4435e9a43981fb69f39c198.png>)

**Explanation**

The function has discontinuities due to vertical asymptotes at $x=\pm 1$. Note that

$$
\lim_{x\to (-1)^{+}} f(x) = -\infty
$$

which is enough to conclude that there is a discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote at $x=-1$. Similarly

$$
\lim_{x\to 1^{-}} f(x) = \infty
$$

which is enough to conclude that there is a discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote at $x=1$.

There is also a discontinuity at $x=-2$, but this is a point discontinuity, not a discontinuity due to a vertical asymptote.

## Finding Infinite Discontinuities of Rational Functions

**Example:** Find all the points where the function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2-5x+6}$ has infinite discontinuities.

**Explanation**

Infinite discontinuities coincide with the vertical asymptotes of $y=f(x)$.

To find the vertical asymptotes, let's start by factoring the expression in the denominator. This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \frac{1}{x^{2} - 5x + 6} \\
&= (1)/((x - 3)(x - 2))
\end{aligned}
$$

There are no common factors in the numerator and denominator, so we can find the vertical asymptotes by setting the denominator to zero:

$$
(x-3)(x-2) = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow\qquad x=2,3
$$

Therefore, the function has infinite discontinuities at $x=2$ and $x=3$.

## Finding Infinite Discontinuities of Rational Functions With Common Factors

**Example:** Find all the points where the function $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2+x-6}{x^2-4}$ has infinite discontinuities.

**Explanation**

Infinite discontinuities coincide with the vertical asymptotes of $y=f(x)$.

To find the vertical asymptotes, let's start by factoring the expressions in the numerator and denominator. This gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \frac{x^{2} + x - 6}{x^{2} - 4} \\
&= ((x - 2)(x + 3))/((x - 2)(x + 2)) \\
&= ((x - 2)(x + 3))/((x - 2)(x + 2)) \\
&= \frac{x + 3}{x + 2}
\end{aligned}
$$

To find the vertical asymptotes, we set the denominator equal to zero and solve:

$$
x+2 = 0 \qquad \Longrightarrow\qquad x=-2
$$

Therefore, the function has an infinite discontinuity at $x=-2$.

<a id="finding-infinite-discontinuities-of-a-piecewise-function"></a>
## Finding Infinite Discontinuities of a Piecewise Function

**Example:** Find all of the points where $f(x)$ below has infinite discontinuities.

${\frac{1}{x - 5},\begin{vmatrix}x < 5 \\ \frac{1}{x - 4},\end{vmatrix}x \ge 5$.

**Explanation**

Infinite discontinuities coincide with the vertical asymptotes of $y=f(x)$. The only possible way that $y=f(x)$ can have vertical asymptotes is if either

$$
\dfrac{1}{x-5}
$$

or

$$
\dfrac{1}{x-4}
$$

grow without bound within their respective subdomains.

- We first compute the asymptotes of $\dfrac{1}{x-5}$. Setting the denominator equal to zero, we get
$x-5=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=5$.
Although this branch of the function is defined for $x < 5$, we do have that
$\lim_{x\to 5^{-}}\left(\dfrac{1}{x-5}\right) = -\infty$,
and therefore we conclude that $x=5$ is indeed an infinite discontinuity.
- Next, we compute the asymptotes of $\dfrac{1}{x-4}$. Setting the denominator equal to zero, we get
$x-4=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x=4$.
However, this branch of the function is defined for $x \geq 5$, which does not contain the neighborhood of $x=4$. So $x=4$ cannot be a vertical asymptote, and therefore $x=4$ cannot be an infinite discontinuity.

Therefore, the function $f(x)$ only has a single infinite discontinuity, and it is at $x=5$.

This matches up with what we see in the graph of $y=f(x)$, shown below.
