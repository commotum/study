## Introduction

How do we evaluate a limit like

$$
\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^2}{\sqrt{9x^4+x}}
$$

Because the ${\color{blue}{x^4}}$ in the radical is a perfect square, we can factor it outside of the radical.

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{9x^{4} + x}} &= lim_(x → ∞)(x^{2})/(\sqrt{x^{4}(9 + \frac{x}{x^{4}})}) \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{4}} \cdot \sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^{3}}}} \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2}}{\mid x^{2} \mid \sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^{3}}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $x^2>0$ for all $x$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid x^2 &= x^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Then, we can cancel the $x^2$ in the numerator and denominator, and evaluate the limit.

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2}}{\mid x^{2} \mid \sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^{3}}}} &= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}\sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^{3}}}} \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{1}{\sqrt{9 + \frac{1}{x^{3}}}} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{9 + 0}} \\
&= \frac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Finding the Horizontal Asymptote of a Radical Function

**Example:** Find the horizontal asymptote of $y=f(x)$ where $f(x)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2x^4+5}}{x^2+3}$.

**Explanation**

The horizontal asymptote of $y=f(x)$ is

$$
y=\lim_\limits{x \rightarrow \infty} f(x)
$$

To evaluate the limit, note that the $x^4$ in the radical is a perfect square, so we can rewrite the function as

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → ∞)f(x) &= \frac{\sqrt{2x^{4} + 5}}{x^{2} + 3} \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)(\sqrt{x^{4}(2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}})})/(x^{2} + 3) \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\sqrt{x^{4}} \cdot \sqrt{2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}}}}{x^{2} + 3} \\
&= lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\mid x^{2} \mid \cdot \sqrt{2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}}}}{x^{2} + 3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $x^2>0$ for all $x$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mid x^2 &= x^2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\mid x^{2} \mid \cdot \sqrt{2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}}}}{x^{2} + 3} = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x^{2} \cdot \sqrt{2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}}}}{x^{2} + 3}
$$

Now, we divide the top and bottom of the fraction by $x^2$ and evaluate the limit, which gives

$$
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\sqrt{2 + \frac{5}{x^{4}}}}{1 + \frac{3}{x^{2}}} = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\sqrt{2 + 0}}{1 + 0} = \sqrt{2}
$$

Therefore, the horizontal asymptote is

$$
y=\sqrt{2}
$$

## Evaluating the Limit at Infinity of a Rational Function with a Radical in the Numerator

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle {\lim_{x\to \infty}\dfrac{\sqrt{2x^2-3x}}{x^2+x}}$.

**Explanation**

We note that the $x^2$ in the radical is a perfect square, so we can rewrite the function as

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\sqrt{2x^{2} - 3x}}{x^{2} + x} &= lim_(x → ∞)(\sqrt{x^{2}(2 - \frac{3x}{x^{2}})})/(x^{2} + x) = lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}} \cdot \sqrt{2 - \frac{3}{x}}}{x^{2} + x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that

$$
\sqrt{x^2}= \mid x \mid
$$

Since $x \to \infty$, we have $x>0$, and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{x^2}= \mid x &= x
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the above reduces to

$$
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{x \cdot \sqrt{2 - \frac{3}{x}}}{x^{2} + x}
$$

Now, we divide the top and bottom of the fraction by $x^2$ and evaluate the limit, which gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → ∞)\frac{\frac{1}{x}\sqrt{2 - \frac{3}{x}}}{1 + \frac{1}{x}} &= \frac{0 \cdot \sqrt{2 - 0}}{1 + 0} = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating the Limit at Infinity of a Rational Function with a Radical in the Denominator

**Example:** Find the horizontal asymptote of the function $y=f(x)$ where $f(x)$ is given by

$f(x)=\dfrac{2x^4+x}{\sqrt{x^6-x}}, \quad x< 0$.

**Explanation**

The horizontal asymptote of $f(x)$ in the domain $x< 0$ is

$$
y=\lim_\limits{x \rightarrow -\infty} f(x)
$$

To find the horizontal asymptote, we note that the $x^6$ in the radical is a perfect square, and so we rewrite the function as

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → - ∞)f(x) &= lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x^{4} + x}{\sqrt{x^{6} - x}}, \\
&= lim_(x → - ∞)(2x^{4} + x)/(\sqrt{x^{6}(1 - \frac{x}{x^{6}})}) \\
&= lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x^{4} + x}{\sqrt{x^{6}} \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{x^{5}}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that

$$
\sqrt{x^6}= \mid x^3 \mid
$$

Since $x \to -\infty$, we have $x^3<0$, and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{x^6}= \mid x^3 &= -x^3
\end{aligned}
$$

So, the above reduces to

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → - ∞)f(x) &= lim_(x → - ∞)(2x^{4} + x)/((-x^{3}) \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{x^{5}}}) =-lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x^{4} + x}{x^{3} \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{x^{5}}}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we divide the top and bottom of the fraction by $x^3$ and evaluate the limit, which gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
lim_(x → - ∞)f(x) &= -lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x + \frac{x}{x^{3}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{x^{5}}}} \\
&=-lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x + \frac{1}{x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{x^{5}}}} \\
&=-lim_(x → - ∞)\frac{2x + 0}{\sqrt{1 - 0}} \\
&=-lim_(x → - ∞)2x \\
&= ∞
\end{aligned}
$$

The limit is not a finite number. Therefore, there is no horizontal asymptote.
