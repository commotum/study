## Introduction

Suppose that we want to calculate $\displaystyle \int 2x e^{x^2}\,\textrm d x$.

Notice that if we let $u = x^2$, then both $u$ and its derivative $u'=2x$ appear in the integrand:

$$
\int \underbrace{2x}_{u'} e^{\overbrace{x^2}^u}\,\textrm d x
$$

Integrals like this can always be solved using substitution.

Let $u=x^2$. Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d} u}{\textrm{d}x} = 2x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textrm d u = 2x\,\textrm d x
$$

We now substitute our expressions into the integral and evaluate it as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int 2xe^{x^{2}}\,\textrm{d}x &= \int e^{x^{2}}\underbrace{2x\,\textrm{d}x}_{\textrm{d}u} \\
&= \int e^{u}\,\textrm{d}u \\
&= e^{u} + C \\
&= e^{x^{2}} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Integrating Products Containing the Exponential Function

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle{\displaystyle \int x^2 e^{x^3+1}\, \textrm{d}x}$.

**Explanation**

Let's introduce the new variable

$$
u = x^3+1
$$

Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}= 3x^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\dfrac 1 3 \textrm{d}u = x^2 \, \textrm{d}x
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x^{2}e^{x^{3} + 1}\,\textrm{d}x &= \int e^{x^{3} + 1} \cdot x^{2}\,\textrm{d}x \\
&= \int e^{u} \cdot \frac{1}{3}\,\textrm{d}u \\
&= \frac{1}{3}\int e^{u}\,\textrm{d}u \\
&= \frac{1}{3}e^{u} + C \\
&= \frac{1}{3}e^{x^{3} + 1} + C
\end{aligned}
$$

## Integrating Products Containing Other Exponential Functions

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int x^3\,{3^{1-x^4}} \, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

Let's introduce the new variable $u= 1-x^4$. Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x} = -4x^3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^3 \, \textrm{d}x = -\dfrac{1}{4} \textrm{d}u
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned} \int {x^3\,{3^{1-x^4}}}\, \textrm{d}x &=\int {{3^{1-x^4}}\cdot x^3}\, \textrm{d}x \\[5pt] &= \int {3^{u}} \cdot \left(-\dfrac{1}{4}\right) \textrm{d}u \\[5pt] &= -\dfrac{1}{4}\int {3^{u}} \textrm{d}u \\[5pt] &= -\dfrac{1}{4}\cdot\dfrac{3^{u}}{\ln 3} + C \\[5pt] &= -\dfrac{3^{1-x^4}}{4\ln 3}+ C. \end{aligned}
$$

<a id="more-complex-cases"></a>
## More Complex Cases

**Example:** Calculate $\displaystyle \int \dfrac {e^{2x}} {e^{2x} + 1} \, \textrm{d}x$.

**Explanation**

Notice that the numerator is proportional to the derivative of the denominator. So, we introduce the new variable

$$
u = e^{2x}+1
$$

Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}= 2e^{2x}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dfrac{1}{2}\, \textrm{d}u = e^{2x} \, \textrm{d}x
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{e^{2x}}{e^{2x} + 1}dx &= \int\frac{1}{e^{2x} + 1} \cdot e^{2x}dx \\
&= \int\frac{1}{u} \cdot \frac{1}{2}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\int\frac{1}{u}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\ln\left|u\right| + C \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\ln (e^{2x} + 1) + C
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that, in the last step, we used the fact that $e^{2x} + 1 > 0$ for all $x$.

## Definite Integrals

Let's evaluate the following definite integral:

$$
\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{3} xe^{x^{2}}\, \textrm{d}x}
$$

We'll use the change of variable method, which comprises three steps:

**Step1:** Change of variable in the integrand.

Let's introduce the new variable $u = x^2$. Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x} = 2x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dfrac{1}{2}\textrm{d}u = x \, \textrm{d}x
$$

**Step 2:** Find the new limits of integration.

We create a table below to change our limits of integration from $x$ to $u{:}$

| $x$ | $0$ | $3$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $u$ | $0$ | $9$ |

**Step 3:** Evaluate the integral in the new variable $u$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{0}^{3}xe^{x^{2}}dx &= \int_{0}^{3}e^{x^{2}} \cdot xdx \\
&= \int_{0}^{9}e^{u} \cdot \frac{1}{2}du \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{9}e^{u}du \\
&= \left.\frac{1}{2}e^{u}\right|_{0}^{9} \\
&= \frac{1}{2}(e^{9} - e^{0}) \\
&= \frac{e^{9} - 1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Evaluating Definite Integrals

**Example:** Evaluate the integral $\displaystyle{\displaystyle \int_{0}^{\sqrt {\ln 4}} 16xe^{-2x^2}\, \textrm{d}x}$.

**Explanation**

Let's introduce the new variable $u=-2x^2$. Differentiating gives

$$
\dfrac{\textrm{d}u}{\textrm{d}x}=-4x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad -4 \, \textrm{d}u=16x \, \textrm{d}x
$$

Next, we determine the new limits of integration:

| $x$ | $0$ | $\sqrt {\ln 4}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $u$ | $0$ | $-2\ln 4$ |

Therefore, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\ln 4}}16xe^{-2x^{2}}dx &= \int_{0}^{\sqrt{\ln 4}}e^{-2x^{2}} \cdot 16xdx \\
&= \int_{0}^{-2\ln 4}e^{u} \cdot (-4du) \\
&=-4\int_{0}^{-2\ln 4}e^{u}du \\
&= \left.- 4e^{u}\right|_{0}^{-2\ln 4} \\
&=-4(e^{-2\ln 4} - e^{0}) \\
&=-4(\frac{1}{16} - 1) \\
&= 4(\frac{15}{16}) \\
&= \frac{15}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$
